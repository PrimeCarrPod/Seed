## Economic Dependency
## 6. Economic Dependency -- Municipal Budgets, Royalties, Local Procurement

Mining communities in Chile are not just near mining -- they are **fiscally dependent** on it. Municipal budgets, employment, procurement, and social services are structured around mining revenue. This dependency creates both vulnerability (commodity cycles) and leverage (communities negotiate hard because they have no alternative). Tardigradia must understand this economic architecture to design mutually beneficial frameworks.

Key insight: **Smaller municipalities are more mining-dependent**. Sierra Gorda (pop 3,200) gets 85% of budget from mining -- it effectively cannot exist without it. This creates a power asymmetry: the community needs the mine more than the mine needs that specific community (until Consulta blocks it). Tardigradia can rebalance this by diversifying the economic base (Aegis-Forge jobs, REE processing, technology training).

### 6.2 Royalty Minera (Law 21,210) -- Mechanism and Impact

New royalty regime (effective 2024):
- **Ad valorem**: 1% on copper sales (all producers)
- **Operating margin**: Progressive 8-26% on operating margin (copper >50 kt/yr)
- **Lithium**: 1.8% ad valorem + 6.8-40% on margin
- **Distribution**: 65% to mining communes (formula: 50% population, 30% poverty, 20% mining intensity), 35% to regional governments

2024 projected national collection: **US.8B**. Antofagasta Region receives ~US50M. This is NEW money -- previously municipalities got only 40% of old royalty (Law 18,248). Communities now have resources to demand better services and negotiate harder.

### 6.3 Local Procurement -- The Economic Multiplier

Mining companies report "local procurement" percentages (goods/services purchased in region):

| Company | Local Procurement % | Local Spend (US/yr) | Top Categories | SME Count |
|---------|----------------------|----------------------|----------------|-----------|
| Codelco | 42% | 1,200 | Maintenance, transport, catering, engineering | 1,800 |
| Antofagasta Minerals | 38% | 450 | Drilling, tires, lubricants, camp services | 650 |
| CAP Minería | 35% | 180 | Explosives, grinding media, pump parts | 320 |
| Lundin (Candelaria) | 30% | 220 | Contractor services, consumables | 280 |
| Anglo American | 28% | 350 | Haulage, maintenance, IT | 400 |

**Gap**: Most "local procurement" goes to **regional hubs (Antofagasta, Calama, Copiapó)**, not the smaller communities (Tierra Amarilla, Andacollo, Sierra Gorda) that host the impacts. SMEs in small communities lack capacity (certification, financing, scale) to bid.

Tardigradia opportunity: **Aegis-Forge supply chain localization**. Commit to:
- 50% of Aegis-Forge consumables (argon, ceramic liners, insulation) sourced from Atacama/Antofagasta SMEs
- **Supplier development program**: Technical assistance, advance payments, certification support for Vallenar/Tierra Amarilla/Andacollo firms
- **Local maintenance hubs**: Train community technicians for Aegis-Forge unit maintenance (15-20 jobs per hub)
- **Target**: 60% local procurement WITHIN host commune (not just region) by Year 3

### 6.4 Commodity Cycle Vulnerability -- The Boom-Bust Reality

Copper price cycles (2003-2023): $0.80/lb (2003) -> $4.50 (2011) -> $2.00 (2016) -> $4.80 (2022) -> $3.80 (2024). Municipal budgets track copper with 12-month lag. During 2015-2017 downturn:
- Calama cut 15% staff, deferred hospital expansion
- Sierra Gorda nearly bankrupt (royalty dropped 60%)
- Vallenar delayed water project 3 years

Tardigradia counter-cyclical design: **Aegis-Forge revenue is Fe3O4 + REE priced, not Cu-priced**. Iron ore and REE prices correlate weakly with copper (r=0.3). During Cu downturn, Fe3O4 demand for Fe16N2 (EV motors, grid transformers) may RISE (green transition). This provides **fiscal stability** to host municipalities -- a new argument for social license.
