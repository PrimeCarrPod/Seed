## DEFINITIONS AND SCOPE

### Key Definitions

**"Wildland-Urban Interface (WUI)"** — The zone where structures and other human development meet or intermingle with undeveloped wildland or vegetative fuels, as mapped by the USDA Forest Service SILVIS laboratory.

**"Vegetation Management Plan (VMP)"** — A documented plan for the systematic management of vegetation within utility rights-of-way and on federal lands to reduce wildfire ignition risk, consistent with the standards established under Title I of this Act.

**"Fire-Resistant Building Material"** — Any construction material that meets or exceeds the Class A fire resistance rating under ASTM E84 and has been certified under the Fire-Resistant Material Certification Program established under Sec. 302.

**"Grid Hardening"** — The physical modification of electrical infrastructure to reduce ignition risk during high-wind, low-humidity events, including but not limited to covered conductor installation, undergrounding, rapid fault isolation, and fast curve switching.

**"Public Safety Power Shutoff (PSPS)"** — The preemptive de-energization of electrical lines during extreme fire weather conditions, subject to the standards and protocols established under Sec. 204.

**"Defensible Space"** — The area within 100 feet of a structure where vegetation and combustible materials are managed to reduce fire spread potential, as defined by the California Department of Forestry and Fire Protection (CAL FIRE) or equivalent state authority.

**"Microgrid"** — A group of interconnected loads and distributed energy resources within clearly defined electrical boundaries that acts as a single controllable entity with respect to the grid.

**"Covered Conductor"** — An electrical conductor encased in a thick layer of insulating material rated to withstand momentary contact with vegetation without arcing or ignition.

### Geographic Scope

This Act applies to:
- All 50 states and the District of Columbia
- All U.S. Territories (Puerto Rico, Guam, USVI, American Samoa, Northern Mariana Islands)
- Tribal lands subject to federal trust responsibilities
- Federal lands managed by the Department of Interior and USDA Forest Service

### Temporal Scope

This Act takes effect upon enactment, with implementation phases as specified in Title VII.

---

## PURPOSE OF THE BILL

The Wildfire Prevention and Grid Resilience Act of 2026 (WPGR Act) establishes a comprehensive national program to reduce wildfire risk through vegetation management, grid hardening, building code modernization, early warning deployment, and response force modernization. The Act authorizes $46 billion over 10 years, fully offset by carbon pricing, subsidy redirection, and green bond proceeds.

---

## THE PROBLEM

Wildfires have reached crisis levels in the United States. The six largest fire seasons in recorded history have occurred since 2020. In 2025 alone, wildfires consumed 12.4 million acres, destroyed 47,000 structures, caused 312 fatalities, and generated $89 billion in direct damages. Climate change is extending fire seasons, increasing the geographic extent of fire-prone areas, and intensifying fire behavior beyond historical norms.

Electrical infrastructure has emerged as a significant ignition source. Power lines have caused some of the most destructive wildfires in California, Oregon, Colorado, and other western states. The 2018 Camp Fire, ignited by Pacific Gas & Electric transmission lines, killed 85 people and caused $16.5 billion in damages. The 2021 Marshall Fire, sparked by a telecom line, destroyed 1,084 homes in suburban Denver.

The Wildland-Urban Interface (WUI) has expanded by 40% since 1990, placing more communities at risk. Current building codes in most WUI jurisdictions do not require fire-resistant construction materials or defensible space. Early warning systems cover less than 15% of high-risk areas. Federal wildfire response forces rely on aging aircraft and inadequate coordination frameworks.

- NERC directed to develop grid hardening audit protocols

## SECTION 201. CONGRESSIONAL FINDINGS

The Congress finds the following:

**(1) Wildfire Crisis Severity**
Wildfires have increased in frequency, intensity, and destructiveness over the past two decades. The six largest fire seasons in United States recorded history have occurred since 2020. In 2025 alone, wildfires consumed 12.4 million acres, destroyed 47,000 structures, caused 312 fatalities, and generated $89 billion in direct damages.

**(2) Climate Change Acceleration**
Climate change is extending fire seasons, increasing the geographic extent of fire-prone areas, and intensifying fire behavior beyond historical norms. Average fire season length has increased from 120 days in 1990 to 265 days in 2025. Average temperatures in fire-prone regions have increased 2.3°F since 1980, reducing snowpack and extending the dry season.

**(3) Electrical Infrastructure Ignition Source**
Electrical infrastructure has been identified as the ignition source for multiple catastrophic wildfires. The 2018 Camp Fire (85 fatalities, $16.5B damages), 2020 Oregon Fire Siege (11 fatalities, $6B damages), and 2021 Marshall Fire (2 fatalities, $5B damages) were all ignited by electrical infrastructure.

**(4) Wildland-Urban Interface Expansion**
The Wildland-Urban Interface (WUI) has expanded by 40% since 1990, placing more communities at risk. An estimated 44 million homes are now located in the WUI. Current building codes in most WUI jurisdictions do not require fire-resistant construction materials or defensible space.

**(5) Vegetation Management Deficit**
Decades of fire suppression have created dangerous fuel loads on federal, state, and private lands. The USDA Forest Service estimates 80 million acres of national forestland require immediate vegetation management. Current treatment rates address less than 2% of the annual need.

**(6) Grid Hardening Underinvestment**
Electrical utilities have underinvested in grid hardening despite known ignition risks. Only 2,500 miles of the 200,000+ miles of overhead lines in high fire risk areas have been hardened. At current rates, full hardening would take 80+ years.

**(7) Early Warning Gap**
Early warning and detection systems cover less than 15% of high fire risk areas. Average alert time in covered areas is 8 minutes — insufficient for evacuation of vulnerable populations. False alarm rates of 35% reduce public trust and compliance.

**(8) Response Force Obsolescence**
Federal wildfire response forces rely on aging aircraft and inadequate coordination frameworks. The average age of the federal air tanker fleet is 52 years. Interagency coordination protocols have not been updated since 2003.

## SECTION 204. DEFINITIONS

**SEC. 204.** In this Act:

**(1) "Wildland-Urban Interface (WUI)"**
The zone where structures and other human development meet or intermingle with undeveloped wildland or vegetative fuels, as mapped by the USDA Forest Service SILVIS laboratory or successor mapping program.

**(2) "Vegetation Management Plan (VMP)"**
A documented plan for the systematic management of vegetation within utility rights-of-way and on federal lands to reduce wildfire ignition risk, consistent with the standards established under Title I of this Act.

**(3) "Fire-Resistant Building Material"**
Any construction material that meets or exceeds the Class A fire resistance rating under ASTM E84 and has been certified under the Fire-Resistant Material Certification Program established under Sec. 302.

**(4) "Grid Hardening"**
The physical modification of electrical infrastructure to reduce ignition risk during high-wind, low-humidity events, including but not limited to covered conductor installation, undergrounding, rapid fault isolation, and fast curve switching.

**(5) "Public Safety Power Shutoff (PSPS)"**
The preemptive de-energization of electrical lines during extreme fire weather conditions, subject to the standards and protocols established under Sec. 204.

**(6) "Defensible Space"**
The area within 100 feet of a structure where vegetation and combustible materials are managed to reduce fire spread potential, as defined by the California Department of Forestry and Fire Protection (CAL FIRE) or equivalent state authority.

**(7) "Microgrid"**
A group of interconnected loads and distributed energy resources within clearly defined electrical boundaries that acts as a single controllable entity with respect to the grid, as defined by IEEE 2030.7.

**(8) "Covered Conductor"**
An electrical conductor encased in a thick layer of insulating material rated to withstand momentary contact with vegetation without arcing or ignition, consistent with IEEE 1202 standards.

**(9) "High Fire Risk Area"**
Any area designated as Fire Hazard Severity Zone "High" or "Very High" by the state fire marshal, or any area within the WUI as mapped by the USDA Forest Service.

**(10) "Disadvantaged Community"**
Any community identified as disadvantaged under the Justice40 Initiative, CalEnviroScreen, or equivalent state screening tool, or any community with median household income below 80% of the state median.

**(11) "Tribal Nation"**
Any federally recognized Indian Tribe, Alaska Native village, or Native Hawaiian community eligible for federal Indian programs.

**(12) "Environmental Justice"**
The fair treatment and meaningful involvement of all people regardless of race, color, national origin, or income with respect to the development, implementation, and enforcement of environmental laws, regulations, and policies.

**(13) "Carbon Pricing"**
A fee on fossil fuel combustion based on carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2e) emissions, as established under Sec. 601.

**(14) "Green Bond"**
A debt instrument issued by the U.S. Treasury specifically designated for climate resilience and environmental infrastructure investment.

**(15) "Traditional Ecological Knowledge"**
The evolving knowledge acquired by Indigenous and local peoples over hundreds or thousands of years through direct contact with the environment, encompassing spiritual relationships, cultural values, and practices.

**SEC. 301.** This title may be cited as the "Wildfire Prevention Act of 2026."

## SEC. 302. FINDINGS SPECIFIC TO WILDFIRE PREVENTION

**SEC. 302.** The Congress finds the following with respect to wildfire prevention:

**(1) Prevention Gap**
Current federal spending allocates less than 5% of wildfire budgets to prevention. Over 95% of federal wildfire funding is directed toward suppression and response. This imbalance perpetuates a cycle of increasing fire severity and cost. Every dollar shifted from suppression to prevention yields a 3:1 return in avoided costs.

**(2) Vegetation Management Crisis**
Fuel loads on federal lands have reached historically dangerous levels. The USDA Forest Service estimates that 80 million acres require immediate vegetation management treatment. Current treatment rates address only 1.5 million acres annually — less than 2% of the need. At current rates, full treatment would take over 50 years.

**(3) Ignition Source Analysis**
Human-caused ignitions account for 85% of all wildfires. Electrical infrastructure ignitions, while representing only 10% of starts, account for 50% of total acreage burned due to ignition during extreme weather conditions. Targeted prevention of human ignitions could reduce total fire starts by 70%.

**(4) Defensible Space Deficit**
Only 15% of homes in the Wildland-Urban Interface have adequate defensible space. Communities with certified defensible space programs experience 60% lower structure loss rates. Current federal funding for defensible space programs is $50 million annually — less than $1 per acre of WUI land.

**(5) Prescribed Fire Backlog**
Prescribed fire is the most cost-effective vegetation management tool, costing $50-500 per acre compared to $500-5,000 per acre for mechanical treatment. Current prescribed fire application treats only 3 million acres annually. The backlog of acres needing prescribed fire exceeds 40 million acres.

**(6) Prevention Workforce Shortage**
The wildfire prevention workforce has declined 30% since 2000 despite increasing need. Federal agencies face 25% vacancy rates in prevention-focused positions. Training programs graduate fewer than 5,000 prevention specialists annually against a need of 25,000.

## SEC. 303. PURPOSES OF TITLE I

**SEC. 303.** The purposes of this title are to:

**(1)** Establish a national wildfire prevention strategy that prioritizes proactive risk reduction over reactive suppression.

**(2)** Dramatically scale vegetation management on federal, state, and private lands to reduce hazardous fuel loads to safe levels within 10 years.

**(3)** Mandate defensible space standards for all structures in the Wildland-Urban Interface.

**(4)** Expand the use of prescribed fire and cultural burning as primary vegetation management tools.

**(5)** Create a dedicated, sustainable funding mechanism for wildfire prevention that is insulated from suppression budget pressures.

**(6)** Build a world-class wildfire prevention workforce through training, recruitment, and retention programs.

**(7)** Deploy advanced ignition detection and prevention technologies across all high fire risk areas.

**(8)** Establish accountability metrics and reporting requirements to ensure prevention programs achieve measurable risk reduction.

## SEC. 304. DEFINITIONS FOR TITLE I

**SEC. 304.** In this title:

**(1) "Hazardous Fuel"**
Any vegetative material, live or dead, that constitutes a fire hazard and threat to public safety, property, or natural resources, including but not limited to dead trees, dense understory, invasive species, and ladder fuels.

**(2) "Fuel Break"**
A strategically located barrier of reduced fuel density designed to slow or stop fire spread, including but not limited to shaded fuel breaks, defensible space zones, and firebreaks.

**(3) "Prescribed Fire"**
Any fire intentionally ignited in accordance with a written prescription plan for the purpose of achieving land management objectives, including vegetation management, ecosystem restoration, and wildfire risk reduction.

**(4) "Cultural Burning**
The intentional use of fire by Indigenous peoples for cultural, spiritual, and land management purposes, consistent with Traditional Ecological Knowledge and practices developed over millennia.

**(5) "Community Wildfire Protection Plan (CWPP)"**
A plan developed by a community in consultation with federal, state, and local agencies that identifies and prioritizes areas for hazardous fuel reduction treatments, recommends measures to reduce structural ignitability, and recommends measures to assist community preparation and evacuation.

**(6) "Fire-Adapted Community"**
A community that has taken proactive measures to reduce wildfire risk through vegetation management, building code enforcement, defensible space compliance, and emergency preparedness planning.

**(7) "Ignition-Resistant Construction"**
Building methods and materials that meet or exceed the standards established under Sec. 310 of this title, designed to resist ignition from ember exposure, radiant heat, and direct flame contact.

**(8) "Prevention Treatment"**
Any activity undertaken to reduce wildfire risk, including vegetation management, defensible space creation, fuel break construction, prescribed fire, and ignition-resistant construction.

# TITLE II — GRID HARDENING

## SEC. 401. SHORT TITLE

**SEC. 401.** This title may be cited as the "Grid Hardening and Resilience Act of 2026."

## SEC. 402. FINDINGS SPECIFIC TO GRID HARDENING

**SEC. 402.** The Congress finds the following with respect to electrical grid hardening:

**(1) Ignition Risk**
Electrical infrastructure is the ignition source for the most destructive wildfires in U.S. history. The 2018 Camp Fire (85 fatalities, $16.5B damages), 2020 Oregon Fire Siege (11 fatalities, $6B damages), and 2021 Marshall Fire (2 fatalities, $5B damages) were all ignited by electrical infrastructure during extreme weather events.

**(2) Underinvestment**
Electrical utilities have systematically underinvested in grid hardening despite known ignition risks. Only 2,500 miles of the 200,000+ miles of overhead lines in high fire risk areas have been hardened. At current rates, full hardening would take 80+ years — far too slow to address the current crisis.

**(3) Cost of Inaction**
The cost of grid hardening ($1.5M per mile for covered conductors, $3-5M per mile for undergrounding) is far less than the cost of wildfire damages ($8.5M per mile in wildfire damages prevented). Every dollar invested in grid hardening returns $5.70 in avoided wildfire costs.

**(4) Technology Availability**
Technologies exist today that can dramatically reduce ignition risk. Covered conductors reduce ignition risk by 95%. Rapid fault isolation prevents 80% of ignitions from conductor contact. Fast curve switching reduces ignition risk from damaged equipment by 70%.

**(5) Regulatory Failure**
State utility commissions have failed to mandate adequate grid hardening. Only 5 states have adopted comprehensive grid hardening standards. Most states rely on voluntary utility programs that have proven insufficient.

**(6) Climate Change Acceleration**
Climate change is increasing the frequency and intensity of extreme fire weather events. The number of Red Flag Warning days has increased 40% since 2000. Grid hardening standards must account for future climate conditions, not just historical weather patterns.

## SEC. 403. PURPOSES OF TITLE II

**SEC. 403.** The purposes of this title are to:

**(1)** Establish mandatory grid hardening standards for all electrical infrastructure in high fire risk areas.

**(2)** Accelerate the deployment of covered conductors, undergrounding, and other hardening technologies to reduce ignition risk.

**(3)** Create a dedicated funding mechanism for grid hardening that is sustainable and sufficient to address the scale of the need.

**(4)** Modernize grid infrastructure to improve reliability, reduce outage frequency, and support renewable energy integration.

**(5)** Establish accountability metrics and reporting requirements to ensure utilities achieve measurable risk reduction.

**(6)** Protect ratepayers from bearing the full cost of grid hardening through federal cost-share programs.

**(7)** Ensure that grid hardening investments prioritize disadvantaged communities and high-risk areas.

**(8)** Support workforce development for grid hardening construction and maintenance.

## SEC. 404. DEFINITIONS FOR TITLE II

**SEC. 404.** In this title:

**(1) "Grid Hardening"**
The physical modification of electrical infrastructure to reduce ignition risk during high-wind, low-humidity events, including but not limited to covered conductor installation, undergrounding, rapid fault isolation, fast curve switching, and equipment replacement.

**(2) "Covered Conductor"**
An electrical conductor encased in a thick layer of insulating material rated to withstand momentary contact with vegetation without arcing or ignition, consistent with IEEE 1202 standards.

**(3) "High Fire Risk Area"**
Any area designated as Fire Hazard Severity Zone "High" or "Very High" by the state fire marshal, or any area within the Wildland-Urban Interface as mapped by the USDA Forest Service.

**(4) "Public Safety Power Shutoff (PSPS)"**
The preemptive de-energization of electrical lines during extreme fire weather conditions, subject to the standards and protocols established under Sec. 406.

**(5) "Rapid Fault Isolation"**
Technology that detects and isolates faults on electrical lines within 1/10th of a second, preventing the sustained arcing that causes vegetation ignitions.

**(6) "Fast Curve Switching"**
Technology that reduces the energy delivered to a fault by modifying the protective relay settings, reducing ignition risk from damaged equipment.

**(7) "Microgrid"**
A group of interconnected loads and distributed energy resources within clearly defined electrical boundaries that acts as a single controllable entity with respect to the grid, as defined by IEEE 2030.7.

**(8) "Undergrounding"**
The replacement of overhead electrical lines with underground cables, eliminating the ignition risk from overhead line contact with vegetation.

## SEC. 405. NATIONAL GRID HARDENING STANDARDS

**SEC. 405.** (a) IN GENERAL.—Not later than 1 year after the date of enactment, the Secretary of Energy, in coordination with the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC), shall establish national grid hardening standards for electrical infrastructure in high fire risk areas.

**(b) STANDARDS.—**The standards shall include:

**(1) Covered Conductor Requirements**
All new overhead distribution lines in high fire risk areas shall use covered conductor. Existing overhead distribution lines in high fire risk areas shall be retrofitted with covered conductor within 10 years.

**(2) Undergrounding Requirements**
All new distribution lines in the highest fire risk areas (Fire Hazard Severity Zone "Very High") shall be undergrounded. Existing distribution lines in these areas shall be undergrounded within 15 years where technically feasible.

**(3) Rapid Fault Isolation**
All distribution lines in high fire risk areas shall be equipped with rapid fault isolation technology within 5 years.

**(4) Fast Curve Switching**
All protective relays on distribution lines in high fire risk areas shall be configured with fast curve switching settings within 2 years.

**(5) Equipment Replacement**
All aging equipment (conductors, insulators, transformers, switches) on distribution lines in high fire risk areas shall be replaced on an accelerated schedule, with priority for equipment over 40 years old.

**(6) Vegetation Management**
All vegetation within the utility right-of-way in high fire risk areas shall be managed consistent with the standards established under Sec. 309(b)(1).

**(7) Weather Monitoring**
All utilities in high fire risk areas shall deploy weather monitoring stations at intervals of no more than 10 miles along distribution lines.

**(8) System Hardening Plans**
All utilities shall develop and submit to the Secretary a system hardening plan that identifies the schedule, cost, and prioritization of hardening activities.

**(c) COMPLIANCE TIMELINE.—**

**(1) SHORT-TERM.—**Utilities shall achieve compliance with fast curve switching and weather monitoring requirements within 2 years.

**(2) MEDIUM-TERM.—**Utilities shall achieve compliance with rapid fault isolation and equipment replacement requirements within 5 years.

**(3) LONG-TERM.—**Utilities shall achieve compliance with covered conductor and undergrounding requirements within 10-15 years.

**SEC. 501.** This title may be cited as the "National Vegetation Management Act of 2026."

## SEC. 502. FINDINGS SPECIFIC TO VEGETATION MANAGEMENT

**SEC. 502.** The Congress finds the following with respect to vegetation management:

**(1) Fuel Load Crisis**
Decades of fire suppression have created historically dangerous fuel loads on federal, state, and private lands. The USDA Forest Service estimates 80 million acres of national forestland require immediate vegetation management treatment. Current treatment rates address less than 2% of the annual need.

**(2) Treatment Backlog**
The backlog of acres needing vegetation management exceeds 100 million acres across all ownerships. At current treatment rates of 3 million acres annually, full treatment would take over 30 years — far too slow to address the current crisis.

**(3) Ecological Benefits**
Vegetation management is not only a wildfire prevention tool but also an ecologically beneficial practice. Thinning overcrowded forests improves forest health, increases drought resilience, enhances wildlife habitat, and promotes biodiversity.

**(4) Economic Opportunity**
Vegetation management creates economic opportunities in rural communities. Timber harvesting, biomass utilization, and ecosystem restoration provide jobs and revenue. Every million acres treated creates approximately 15,000 jobs.

**(5) Climate Benefits**
Healthy, well-managed forests sequester more carbon than overstocked, fire-prone forests. Vegetation management reduces wildfire carbon emissions and enhances long-term carbon sequestration.

**(6) Water Security**
Vegetation management in watersheds protects water quality and supply. Post-fire erosion contaminates water supplies for millions of Americans. Proactive vegetation management is more cost-effective than post-fire watershed restoration.

**SEC. 601.** This title may be cited as the "Fire-Resistant Building Standards Act of 2026."

## SEC. 602. FINDINGS SPECIFIC TO BUILDING STANDARDS

**SEC. 602.** The Congress finds the following with respect to building standards:

**(1) Structure Ignitability**
Structure ignitability is the primary determinant of structure loss during wildfires. Over 90% of structures destroyed in wildfires are ignited by embers, not direct flame contact. Fire-resistant building materials and designs can reduce structure ignition probability by 80%.

**(2) Building Code Deficiency**
Current building codes in most Wildland-Urban Interface jurisdictions do not require fire-resistant construction materials or designs. Only 5 states have adopted comprehensive WUI building codes. The majority of structures in the WUI are built to standards that do not address wildfire risk.

**(3) Retrofit Cost-Effectiveness**
Retrofitting existing structures with fire-resistant materials is cost-effective. The average retrofit cost of $15,000-25,000 per structure is far less than the average wildfire loss of $250,000 per structure. Every dollar invested in fire-resistant construction returns $10 in avoided losses.

**(4) Insurance Incentives**
Insurance companies are increasingly refusing to insure structures in the WUI that do not meet fire-resistant construction standards. Fire-resistant construction can reduce insurance premiums by 30-50%.

**(5) Life Safety**
Fire-resistant construction provides additional time for evacuation, reducing the risk of fatalities. Structures built to fire-resistant standards maintain structural integrity 3x longer during fire exposure.

**(6) Community Resilience**
Fire-resistant construction at the community level reduces the cascading fire spread that destroys entire neighborhoods. Communities with fire-resistant construction standards experience 70% lower structure loss rates.

## SEC. 604. DEFINITIONS FOR TITLE IV

**SEC. 604.** In this title:

**(1) "Wildland-Urban Interface (WUI)"**
The zone where structures and other human development meet or intermingle with undeveloped wildland or vegetative fuels, as mapped by the USDA Forest Service SILVIS laboratory.

**(2) "Fire-Resistant Building Material"**
Any construction material that meets or exceeds the Class A fire resistance rating under ASTM E84 and has been certified under the Fire-Resistant Material Certification Program.

**(3) "Ignition-Resistant Construction"**
Building methods and materials designed to resist ignition from ember exposure, radiant heat, and direct flame contact.

**(4) "Ember-Resistant Vent"**
A vent designed to prevent the entry of embers into a structure, with screening of 1/8-inch mesh or smaller.

**(5) "Class A Roof Assembly"**
A roof assembly that meets the highest fire resistance rating under ASTM E108, including non-combustible covering and underlayment.

**(6) "Defensible Space"**
The area within 100 feet of a structure where vegetation and combustible materials are managed to reduce fire spread potential.

# TITLE V — RESPONSE MODERNIZATION

## SEC. 701. SHORT TITLE

**SEC. 701.** This title may be cited as the "Wildfire Response Modernization Act of 2026."

---

## SEC. 702. FINDINGS SPECIFIC TO RESPONSE MODERNIZATION

**SEC. 702.** The Congress finds the following with respect to wildfire response:

**(1) Aging Fleet**
The federal wildfire response fleet is dangerously obsolete. The average age of the federal air tanker fleet is 52 years. Many aircraft are no longer supported by manufacturers. Maintenance costs have increased 300% in the past decade.

**(2) Inadequate Coordination**
Interagency coordination protocols have not been updated since 2003. Communication systems are incompatible across agencies. Joint training exercises are infrequent and underfunded.

**(3) Technology Gap**
Advanced firefighting technologies are not widely deployed. Night vision capabilities are limited. Unmanned aerial systems are underutilized. Real-time fire mapping is not available to all incident commanders.

**(4) Workforce Shortage**
Federal agencies face 25% vacancy rates in firefighter positions. The wildfire workforce has not kept pace with increasing fire activity. Mental health support for firefighters is inadequate.

**(5) Community Notification**
Early warning and evacuation systems are inadequate. Alert times in covered areas average 8 minutes — insufficient for vulnerable populations. False alarm rates of 35% reduce public trust.

**(6) Post-Fire Recovery**
Post-fire recovery programs are underfunded and slow. Watershed restoration, debris removal, and community rebuilding take years. Mental health support for affected communities is inadequate.

**SEC. 801.** This title may be cited as the "Wildfire Prevention and Grid Resilience Funding Act of 2026."

---

## SEC. 802. FINDINGS SPECIFIC TO FUNDING

**SEC. 802.** The Congress finds the following with respect to funding:

**(1) Funding Gap**
Current federal wildfire funding is insufficient to address the scale of the crisis. The annual funding gap between current spending and estimated need is $30-40 billion. Without additional funding, the wildfire crisis will continue to escalate.

**(2) Current Funding Sources**
Current wildfire funding comes from discretionary appropriations that are subject to annual budget pressures and continuing resolutions. This creates uncertainty and prevents long-term planning.

**(3) Dedicated Funding Needed**
A dedicated, sustainable funding mechanism is needed to provide reliable, long-term funding for wildfire prevention and grid resilience. Dedicated funding should be insulated from annual appropriations battles.

**(4) Multiple Funding Sources**
A combination of funding sources is needed, including carbon pricing, green bonds, utility assessments, and federal appropriations. No single source is sufficient to address the full need.

**(5) Economic Return**
Every dollar invested in wildfire prevention returns $5.90 in avoided damages. The economic return on investment justifies significant public investment.

**(6) Intergenerational Equity**
Wildfire prevention investments benefit future generations. Funding mechanisms should reflect the intergenerational nature of the benefits.

**(2) Phased Approach**
A phased implementation approach is necessary to allow for capacity building, workforce development, and technology deployment. Phasing must balance urgency with feasibility.

**(3) Coordination Complexity**
Implementation requires coordination across multiple federal agencies, states, Tribal nations, local governments, utilities, and private stakeholders. Clear roles and responsibilities are essential.

**(4) Adaptive Management**
Implementation must incorporate adaptive management principles, allowing for course correction based on experience and changing conditions.

**(5) Accountability**
Clear timelines, milestones, and accountability mechanisms are essential to ensure implementation stays on track.

**(6) Long-Term Commitment**
Wildfire prevention is a long-term effort requiring sustained commitment over 10+ years. Implementation must be designed for long-term success.

## SEC. 1102. FINDINGS SPECIFIC TO ECONOMIC IMPACT

**SEC. 1102.** The Congress finds the following with respect to economic impact:

**(1) Wildfire Economic Toll**
Wildfires cost the U.S. economy $50-100 billion annually in direct damages, suppression costs, and indirect economic losses. The 2025 fire season alone caused $89 billion in direct damages.

**(2) Prevention Return on Investment**
Every dollar invested in wildfire prevention returns $5.90 in avoided damages, based on Congressional Budget Office analysis. Grid hardening costs $1.5M per mile but prevents $8.5M per mile in wildfire damages.

**(3) Insurance Market Collapse**
Major insurers have withdrawn from 12 western states due to wildfire risk. The California FAIR Plan has seen exposure increase from $12B to $180B in 3 years. Without intervention, an estimated 8 million homeowners will be uninsurable by 2030.

**(4) Economic Disproportionality**
Wildfire impacts disproportionately affect low-income communities, communities of color, and Tribal nations. 72% of structures destroyed in 2024 were in communities with median income below the state average.

**(5) Job Creation**
Wildfire prevention and grid hardening investments create significant employment. Every million acres treated creates approximately 15,000 jobs. Grid hardening creates 20 jobs per mile of line hardened.

**(6) Fiscal Impact**
Wildfire prevention investments reduce federal disaster spending, reduce state and local emergency response costs, and protect property tax bases in fire-prone areas.

## SEC. 1310. APPENDIX F — GLOSSARY OF TERMS

**SEC. 1310.** The following terms are defined for purposes of this Act:

**(1) "Adaptive Management"**
A systematic process for continually improving management policies and practices by learning from the outcomes of operational programs.

**(2) "Biomass"**
Organic material derived from vegetation management activities, including trees, shrubs, slash, and other plant material.

**(3) "Carbon Pricing"**
A fee on fossil fuel combustion based on carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2e) emissions.

**(4) "Community Wildfire Protection Plan (CWPP)"**
A plan developed by a community in consultation with federal, state, and local agencies that identifies and prioritizes areas for hazardous fuel reduction treatments.

**(5) "Covered Conductor"**
An electrical conductor encased in a thick layer of insulating material rated to withstand momentary contact with vegetation without arcing or ignition.

**(6) "Cultural Burning"**
The intentional use of fire by Indigenous peoples for cultural, spiritual, and land management purposes.

**(7) "Defensible Space"**
The area within 100 feet of a structure where vegetation and combustible materials are managed to reduce fire spread potential.

**(8) "Fire-Adapted Community"**
A community that has taken proactive measures to reduce wildfire risk through vegetation management, building code enforcement, defensible space compliance, and emergency preparedness planning.

**(9) "Fire-Resistant Building Material"**
Any construction material that meets or exceeds the Class A fire resistance rating under ASTM E84.

**(10) "Fuel Break"**
A strategically located barrier of reduced fuel density designed to slow or stop fire spread.

**(11) "Green Bond"**
A debt instrument issued by the U.S. Treasury specifically designated for climate resilience and environmental infrastructure investment.

**(12) "Grid Hardening"**
The physical modification of electrical infrastructure to reduce ignition risk during high-wind, low-humidity events.

**(13) "Hazardous Fuel"**
Any vegetative material, live or dead, that constitutes a fire hazard and threat to public safety, property, or natural resources.

**(14) "High Fire Risk Area"**
Any area designated as Fire Hazard Severity Zone "High" or "Very High" by the state fire marshal.

**(15) "Ignition-Resistant Construction"**
Building methods and materials designed to resist ignition from ember exposure, radiant heat, and direct flame contact.

**(16) "Microgrid"**
A group of interconnected loads and distributed energy resources within clearly defined electrical boundaries that acts as a single controllable entity with respect to the grid.

**(17) "Prescribed Fire"**
Any fire intentionally ignited in accordance with a written prescription plan for the purpose of achieving land management objectives.

**(18) "Public Safety Power Shutoff (PSPS)"**
The preemptive de-energization of electrical lines during extreme fire weather conditions.

**(19) "Traditional Ecological Knowledge"**
The evolving knowledge acquired by Indigenous and local peoples over hundreds or thousands of years through direct contact with the environment.

**(20) "Wildland-Urban Interface (WUI)"**
The zone where structures and other human development meet or intermingle with undeveloped wildland or vegetative fuels.

## SEC. 1314. APPENDIX J — ENVIRONMENTAL JUSTICE ANALYSIS

(C) Technical assistance and training
(D) Co-management agreements

**(c) RURAL COMMUNITY BENEFITS.—
