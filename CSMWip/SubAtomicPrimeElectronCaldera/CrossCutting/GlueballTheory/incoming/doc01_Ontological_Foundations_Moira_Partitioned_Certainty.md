## Chunk 1 of 6: Etymological Topology and Proto-Indo-European Semantic Architecture

The term moira (μοῖρα) operates as a primitive semantic atom in the Greek lexical system, irreducible to simpler constituents yet generative of an entire conceptual framework governing existence. Etymological reconstruction traces moira to the Proto-Indo-European root *(s)mer-, denoting "to allot," "to assign," "to measure out a portion." This root yields a semantic cluster of extraordinary density: Greek meros (part, share, portion), moros (doom, fate, death), moira (allotted portion, fate, destiny); Latin merere (to earn, deserve), merx (goods, merchandise), mors (death); Sanskrit smṛ (to remember), smara (remembrance); Old English mǣru (famous, renowned). The semantic field spans allocation, merit, memory, mortality, and measurement — a unified topology where to receive a portion is to be measured, to be measured is to be bounded, to be bounded is to be mortal.

The Mycenaean Linear B attestation *mo-ri* (KN V 52) confirms the term's Bronze Age antiquity, appearing in administrative contexts recording allotted rations, land portions, and labor shares. The bureaucratic origin is not incidental: moira originates as a quantifiable distributive unit, a discrete quantum of resource assigned by central authority. The theological abstraction emerges through metaphorical extension — the cosmos itself becomes a distributive system, each entity receiving its moira as ration, its lifespan as allotment, its death as final accounting. The shift from administrative token to metaphysical principle mirrors the evolution of the palace economy into the polis: what was once a scribe's notation becomes a god's decree.

Three semantic strata accumulate without replacement. Stratum One: moira as physical portion — a measured length of cloth, a weighed quantity of grain, a demarcated plot of land. Stratum Two: moira as temporal allotment — the span of years assigned to a life, the duration of a reign, the term of an office. Stratum Three: moira as qualitative destiny — the specific configuration of fortunes, sufferings, honors, and shames that constitute an individual's lot. Each stratum preserves the core structure: a whole is partitioned, a partition is assigned, an assignment is inalienable. The verb nemō (to distribute, assign, allot) governs the operation; the noun moira names the result. The perfect participle memnēmai (I remember) shares the root — to remember is to re-collect one's allotted portions, to reassemble the distributed self.

The singular/plural oscillation in early texts reveals the concept's phase transition. Homeric Moira (singular) functions as impersonal cosmic constant: "Moira spins the thread of life for each man at birth" (Iliad 24.209). Hesiodic Moirai (plural) personify the distributive function as three distinct agents. The transition from singular field to plural operators parallels the mathematical shift from a scalar potential to a vector field — from a single destiny-function f(x) to a triadic operator suite {C, L, A} acting on the state space of possible lives. The singular Moira represents the invariant total; the plural Moirai represent the differential mechanisms by which the total is partitioned into individual trajectories.

The PIE root *(s)mer- generates a derivational network of exceptional coherence. The zero-grade *smr- yields Greek moros (doom, fate) — the portion that kills. The e-grade *mer- yields meros (part) — the portion that constitutes. The lengthened grade *mēr- yields moira (allotted portion) — the portion that is assigned. The nasal-infixed *sm-n- yields Latin merere (to earn) — the portion that is deserved. The semantic logic is inexorable: the universe contains a finite quantum of existence; this quantum is partitioned (meros); each partition is assigned (moira); the assignment determines the terminus (moros); the recipient earns the partition through the very act of receiving it (merere); the memory of the partition persists after the recipient perishes (smṛ). The derivational chain encodes the entire lifecycle of the allotted portion in morphological form.

The Gulses (Gulšeš) appear in the KUB 17.10 ritual text as "the goddesses of fate who hold the mirror and the spindle." They spin the king's thread of life at birth, measure it, and cut it at death. The mirror reflects the allotted form; the spindle produces the allotted substance. The Hittite term gulš- derives from PIE *gʷel- (to turn, wind, spin) — a distinct root from *(s)mer- but converging on the same textile technology. The convergence is structural: any culture that develops spinning independently discovers that the spindle is the optimal metaphor for the production of continuous, tensile, measurable thread from discontinuous, loose, unmeasurable fiber. The spindle is not a cultural choice; it is a technological inevitability. The Gulses predate the Moirai by centuries, yet the operational isomorphism is complete: three goddesses, spindle-tool, birth-to-death trajectory, royal focus. The Moirai inherit a fully-formed computational architecture.

## Chunk 3 of 6: The Textile Metaphor as Computational Isomorphism

The weft threads (mobile, horizontal, inserted sequentially) represent the contingent events of a life — choices, encounters, accidents, achievements. The shed (the temporary separation of warp threads) represents the decision window: the finite set of possibilities open at each moment. The shuttle (carrying the weft) represents agency: the capacity to select one thread from the available palette and insert it into the fabric. The beat (pressing the weft into place) represents irreversibility: once woven, the event joins the fixed past, becoming part of the warp for subsequent rows.

The warp/weft distinction maps exactly to the necessity/contingency distinction in modal logic. The warp threads are necessary truths: □(physical laws hold), □(biological constraints bind), □(cosmic order persists). The weft threads are contingent truths: ◇(this marriage occurs), ◇(this battle is won), ◇(this illness strikes).
