## 1.0 THE SANCTUARY CONTEXT: DEPLOYING SURVIVAL INFRASTRUCTURE WITHIN A PROTECTED MARINE ECOSYSTEM

The Monterey Bay National Marine Sanctuary, designated in 1992 under the National Marine Sanctuaries Act (16 U.S.C. § 1431 et seq.) and encompassing approximately 15,000 square kilometers of coastal and offshore waters from Rocky Point in Marin County southward to Cambria in San Luis Obispo County, is one of the most biologically productive and ecologically diverse regions of the global ocean, a productivity that is driven by the seasonal upwelling of cold, nutrient-rich water from the depths of the Monterey Submarine Canyon — the nutrient engine of the sanctuary — which fuels the phytoplankton blooms that support the entire pelagic food web, from the krill and the copepods that graze on the phytoplankton to the sardines, the anchovies, and the squid that feed on the zooplankton, to the larger predators — the salmon, the tuna, the sharks, the sea lions, the harbor seals, the dolphins, and the whales — that feed on the smaller fish and that migrate through the sanctuary in enormous numbers, particularly during the summer upwelling season (April through September), when the sanctuary's waters teem with life, a teeming that the Atlantis pod, by its deployment at 45 meters depth on the northern flank of the Monterey Canyon, must not disrupt, a constraint that is both a legal requirement — the Sanctuary Exploration Permit (SEP-2025-077) that NOAA issued to the Atlantis program in December 2025, after a 14-month review process that included the preparation of a 280-page Environmental Assessment (EA-ATL-MON-2025) by the Atlantis environmental team, the public comment period (which generated 47 written comments, 12 of which were from environmental organizations — the Monterey Bay Aquarium, the Ocean Conservancy, the Surfrider Foundation, and the Center for Biological Diversity — expressing concerns about the potential impacts of the pod on the sanctuary's resources, concerns that the Atlantis environmental team addressed in the Final Environmental Assessment, which was published in November 2025 and which the NOAA Office of National Marine Sanctuaries used as the basis for its decision to issue the permit, a decision that was not unanimous — the sanctuary's Advisory Council, a 20-member body representing the diverse stakeholders of the sanctuary (including commercial fishing, recreational fishing, conservation, research, education, and the local community), voted 14 to 6 to recommend approval of the permit, with the dissenting members arguing that the Atlantis pod, as a permanent installation (the 5-year initial permit term, with the expectation of renewal for the pod's 50-year design life, effectively makes the pod a permanent feature of the sanctuary), would set a precedent for the commercialization and the industrialization of the sanctuary, a precedent that the dissenting members consider inconsistent with the sanctuary's conservation mandate, an argument that the NOAA decision-maker addressed in the permit decision memo (NOAA-ONMS-2025-077-DEC), which acknowledges the dissenting opinion but concludes that the Atlantis pod, by its research mission (the pod's instrumentation will contribute to the sanctuary's oceanographic monitoring program, and the pod will serve as a platform for the study of the crustal displacement threat, a threat that, if it materializes, would cause far greater ecological damage to the sanctuary than the pod itself — the wave, the turbidite flows, the sediment deposition, and the post-event climate disruption would fundamentally alter the sanctuary's ecosystem, potentially destroying it, and the Atlantis pod, by contributing to the understanding of this threat and to the development of the survival infrastructure that could mitigate its effects, serves the broader purpose of the sanctuary's mission to protect the marine environment for future generations, a purpose that the Atlantis pod's modest environmental footprint — a seabed disturbance area of approximately 500 square meters, less than 0.00003 percent of the sanctuary's area — does not compromise, and that the Atlantis program's commitment to the zero-trace decommissioning of the pod ensures will not be permanent, a commitment that is legally binding through the decommissioning plan that is part of the permit and that NOAA can enforce through the permit's suspension or revocation if the Atlantis program fails to comply, an enforcement mechanism that the dissenting Advisory Council members acknowledged, in the Council's final meeting before the vote, as an adequate safeguard against the permanent degradation of the sanctuary, a safeguard that, combined with the environmental monitoring program that the permit requires (the monthly ROV surveys of the pod's seabed footprint, the quarterly water-quality sampling around the pod, the annual acoustic monitoring of the marine mammal vocalizations in the vicinity of the pod, and the 5-year comprehensive environmental audit that the permit requires the Atlantis program to submit to NOAA, documenting the pod's environmental performance and demonstrating compliance with all permit conditions), provides the transparency and the accountability that the public — and the environmental community — demands, a demand that the Atlantis program respects and that the environmental monitoring program fulfills, through the systematic collection, analysis, and public reporting of the environmental data that the pod generates, a data stream that the Atlantis environmental team publishes, in real time, on the Atlantis program's public website, and that the NOAA sanctuary staff can access at any time, through the MARS cabled observatory's data portal, a transparency that the Atlantis program embraces as consistent with its core values — the values of the Spruce-Drake persona, who insisted, in Episode Seventeen of the Sibling Frequency, that "survival without transparency is tyranny, and the Atlantis communities will not be the seed of a new tyranny but of a new openness, a new accountability, a new relationship between humanity and the planet that sustains it, a relationship that is based on knowledge, not on ignorance, on stewardship, not on exploitation, on love, not on fear" — a relationship that the Atlantis pod, by its presence in the sanctuary and by the environmental monitoring that it conducts, embodies, a embodiment that this PART-019 documents, as the ecological complement to the physical, the engineering, and the human dimensions of the Atlantis communities that the preceding parts of this Deep Expansion have addressed, a complement that is essential to the Atlantis program's credibility and to its ultimate success, because a program that protects human life at the expense of the non-human life that shares the planet with us is a program that has failed in its broader mission — to preserve not just the species but the world that the species inhabits, a world that is not merely a resource to be exploited but a community to which we belong, a community that the Atlantis communities, by their design and their operation, honor, a honoring that this PART-019 affirms and that the Atlantis environmental monitoring program, in its ongoing work, demonstrates.
