# CSMFAB077-DEEP-029 — THE OSA TETRAHEDRAL LAYOUT: 4-POD CONFIGURATION MECHANICS, TUNNEL PRESSURE DIFFERENTIAL MANAGEMENT

**Document ID:** CSMFAB077-DEEP-029 | **Parent:** CSMFAB000000000077 | **Version:** 2.0 | **Part:** 029 of 160 — Block 3

The Osa demonstration community, as described in Section 7 of the parent document, arranges its four Atlantis-class pods — OSA-01 (hub), OSA-02, OSA-03 (Arklet), OSA-04 — in a tetrahedral configuration, a three-dimensional arrangement in which each pod occupies one vertex of a regular tetrahedron with an edge length of 25 meters, a geometry that, unlike the planar hexagon of the Monterey community, exploits the third spatial dimension to provide three connectivity paths between any two pods (each pod connects to the other three via the 25-meter inter-pod tunnels, which are angled upward or downward at approximately 35 degrees from the horizontal — the angle that the tetrahedral geometry dictates — a departure from the horizontal tunnels of the Monterey layout that introduces a new set of engineering challenges: the pressure differential between the pods, which, because the pods are at different depths (the deepest pod is approximately 10 meters below the shallowest pod, a depth difference that corresponds to a hydrostatic pressure difference of approximately 100 kilopascals, or 1 atmosphere), must be managed by the tunnel's pressure-equalization system, a system that consists of a series of automatically controlled butterfly valves, spaced at 5-meter intervals along the tunnel, that can be closed to isolate a flooded section of the tunnel (in the event of a breach) and that, during normal operations, modulate the flow of air through the tunnel to equalize the pressure between the connected pods, a modulation that the pod's environmental control system — the S-Bus-controlled HVAC, which monitors the atmospheric pressure, temperature, humidity, and carbon dioxide concentration in each pod and adjusts the tunnel valves and the air-circulation fans accordingly — performs continuously, maintaining the atmospheric pressure in each pod within ±1 kilopascal of the setpoint (101.3 kilopascals, the standard atmospheric pressure at sea level, which is the design internal pressure of the pod), a precision that is essential for the comfort and the health of the crew (the human ear is sensitive to pressure changes of as little as 0.5 kilopascals — the pressure change that occurs during the ascent or descent of a commercial aircraft, and that causes the familiar "ear popping," a physiological response that, if repeated frequently, can cause discomfort and, in extreme cases, ear barotrauma — the rupture of the eardrum, a painful and potentially disabling injury that the Atlantis design must prevent, not only by minimizing the pressure fluctuations but by training the crew to equalize the pressure in their ears — using the Valsalva maneuver, the Toynbee maneuver, or the Frenzel maneuver, all of which are standard techniques in the diving and aviation communities — during the transit through the angled tunnels, a training that the Atlantis crew certification course includes and that the crew practices, in the Mojave mockup, until the equalization becomes automatic, a automatization that the Atlantis human factors team has designed and validated, through the testing of the crew candidates in the Mojave altitude chamber, which simulates the pressure changes of the tunnel transit, a simulation that the candidates must pass, demonstrating the ability to equalize their ear pressure without assistance and without discomfort, before they are certified for deployment, a certification that the Atlantis medical officer verifies, by examining each crew member's ears — using an otoscope, a handheld instrument that provides a magnified view of the eardrum — before and after the altitude chamber test, looking for any signs of barotrauma, a examination that is part of the comprehensive medical surveillance program that the Atlantis medical team conducts, and that the Atlantis safety case requires, as a condition of the pod's operational certification.
