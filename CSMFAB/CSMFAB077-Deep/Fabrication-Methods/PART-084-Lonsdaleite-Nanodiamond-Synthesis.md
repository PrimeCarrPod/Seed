# CSMFAB077-PART-084 — LONSDALEITE NANODIAMOND SYNTHESIS: DETONATION PROCESS, 3000K/20GPa, 5nm DIAMETER, HEXAGONAL PHASE XRD CONFIRMATION
## Block 8 — Fabrication Methods | Atlantis-Class Subsea Habitation Module Production
## Carrington Storm Motors — Safe Pod Engineering Division | Aegis C Material Architecture

---

The lonsdaleite nanodiamond — so named for Kathleen Lonsdale, the Irish crystallographer who first proved the hexagonal structure of benzene through X-ray diffraction in 1929 and who, in a fitting tribute to the interdisciplinary nature of extreme materials science, lends her name to the hexagonal polymorph of diamond that forms under the extraordinary conditions of shock compression generated by the detonation of high explosives in an oxygen-deficient atmosphere, conditions that briefly replicate the pressure-temperature regime of the Earth's transition zone between 410 and 660 kilometers depth — is the nanoscale reinforcement that transforms the LBFRP-001 basalt-Elium composite from a merely adequate structural material into an extraordinary one, and the synthesis of this material, at the kilogram quantities required for the Atlantis demonstration program (approximately 120 kilograms of lonsdaleite nanodiamond for the 10-pod production run, corresponding to a nominal loading of 3 weight percent in the structural laminate and 5 weight percent in the outer 5-millimeter impact-resistant layer, a quantity that is minuscule by the standards of industrial carbon black production — a typical tire-grade carbon black plant produces 120,000 kilograms per day, or 1,000 times the Atlantis requirement — but that is unprecedented for the lonsdaleite nanodiamond material, which prior to the Atlantis program existed only as laboratory-scale quantities of a few grams produced at academic research facilities in the United States, Russia, and China, none of which possessed the production capacity, the quality control infrastructure, or the industrial safety protocols required to supply a production-scale composite manufacturing program), constitutes the subject of the present document, PART-084, which describes the detonation synthesis process as implemented at the Mojave facility's dedicated Lonsdaleite Production Cell (LPC), a reinforced concrete structure located in a remote corner of the Mojave site, isolated by a 200-meter blast-exclusion zone from the main AFP and composite fabrication buildings, and designed to safely contain the detonation of high explosives — primarily a mixture of trinitrotoluene (TNT) and hexogen (RDX, cyclotrimethylenetrinitramine) in a 60:40 weight ratio, compounded with graphite powder as the carbon precursor and cooled by a surrounding water jacket that provides both the thermal mass to quench the detonation products and the confinement to maintain the high pressure during the critical few microseconds of the diamond nucleation and growth phase — in a process that, while violent, transient, and extraordinarily dangerous, is also, when properly controlled and instrumented, the only known method for producing lonsdaleite in sufficient yield and purity to serve as a practical composite reinforcement material, a position that the Atlantis program has reached after evaluating and rejecting the alternative synthesis routes — chemical vapor deposition (CVD), which can produce lonsdaleite films on suitable substrates but at deposition rates of a few nanometers per minute, making bulk production economically impossible; high-pressure high-temperature (HPHT) static compression, which can convert graphite to diamond but favors the cubic diamond polymorph rather than hexagonal lonsdaleite, because the lower-pressure, longer-duration conditions of HPHT allow the carbon atoms to rearrange into the thermodynamically stable cubic phase rather than trapping the metastable hexagonal phase that forms under the rapid compression and quenching of the detonation process; and ball milling of cubic diamond, which can introduce stacking faults into the diamond lattice that produce local hexagonal-like environments but that does not produce true lonsdaleite with the continuous hexagonal stacking sequence that is the hallmark of the lonsdaleite crystal structure and that is necessary for the enhanced mechanical properties — specifically, the approximately 60 percent higher indentation hardness relative to cubic diamond, as measured by nanoindentation of individual lonsdaleite crystallites using a diamond Berkovich indenter tip and analyzed by the Oliver-Pharr method, a measurement that the Atlantis materials team has validated on lonsdaleite samples supplied by the Institute of High Pressure Physics of the Russian Academy of Sciences, the world's leading center for lonsdaleite research and the source of the fundamental detonation synthesis parameters that the Mojave LPC has adapted and scaled from the laboratory to the production environment — that the lonsdaleite provides as a composite reinforcement.

The detonation synthesis process begins with the preparation of the explosive charge, a precisely-formulated mixture of TNT and RDX — the two most widely-used military high explosives, selected for this application not because of their military availability (although the Mojave facility's Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives (ATF) Type 33 federal explosives license, which authorizes the manufacture, storage, and use of high explosives for industrial and research purposes, was secured after a 14-month application, inspection, and compliance verification process that included the installation of the blast-containment infrastructure described below, the development of standard operating procedures for explosives handling that were reviewed and approved by an independent explosives safety consultant, and the completion of an ATF on-site inspection that verified compliance with the storage quantity limits, the magazine construction standards, and the personnel security requirements specified in 27 CFR Part 555, the federal explosives regulations) — but because of their well-characterized detonation properties, their chemical compatibility with the graphite carbon precursor that is mixed into the explosive charge to provide the carbon atoms that will ultimately form the lonsdaleite nanodiamond, and their oxygen balance, a parameter that is critical for the nanodiamond synthesis because the detonation must be oxygen-deficient — that is, the explosive must contain insufficient oxygen to fully oxidize all its carbon content to CO or CO₂ — so that a fraction of the carbon remains as elemental carbon after the detonation, available to condense into the diamond phase during the subsequent cooling and expansion of the detonation products, rather than being consumed by oxidation to carbon monoxide or carbon dioxide, which are gaseous at the detonation temperature and would be lost to the atmosphere, contributing nothing to the nanodiamond yield and representing a waste of both the explosive's energy content and the carbon precursor material. The TNT (C₇H₅N₃O₆, molecular weight 227.13 g/mol, oxygen balance −74 percent — meaning that complete oxidation of the TNT molecule's carbon and hydrogen content would require additional oxygen beyond what the molecule itself contains, and that TNT detonation therefore produces significant quantities of elemental carbon (soot) as a detonation product, a property that, for military applications where the detonation of TNT produces a visible black smoke cloud, is a disadvantage (it reveals the detonation location) but that, for the nanodiamond synthesis application, is exploited as the source of the carbon that will constitute the diamond product) and the RDX (C₃H₆N₆O₆, molecular weight 222.12 g/mol, oxygen balance −22 percent — significantly less oxygen-deficient than TNT, because the RDX molecule contains a higher proportion of oxygen relative to its carbon and hydrogen content, and the detonation of RDX therefore produces less elemental carbon and more CO and CO₂, making RDX alone a poor carbon source for nanodiamond synthesis, but its higher detonation velocity — approximately 8,750 meters per second for RDX at its theoretical maximum density, versus approximately 6,900 meters per second for TNT — and its higher detonation pressure — approximately 34 GPa for RDX versus approximately 19 GPa for TNT — provide the extreme pressure and temperature conditions that are necessary to transform the carbon from the sp²-hybridized graphite precursor into the sp³-hybridized diamond product, a transformation that requires pressures in excess of approximately 15 GPa at the detonation temperature of approximately 3,000 K — the graphite-diamond phase boundary as determined by the Bundy phase diagram of carbon, which maps the thermodynamically stable phase of carbon as a function of pressure and temperature and which shows that, at 3,000 K, diamond (both cubic and hexagonal) is the stable phase at pressures above approximately 15 GPa, while graphite is the stable phase at lower pressures, meaning that the detonation must sustain pressures above this threshold for a duration sufficient to nucleate and grow the diamond crystallites — a duration that, for the TNT/RDX detonation, is on the order of 0.5 to 1.0 microseconds (the characteristic time for the detonation wave to traverse the explosive charge, during which the pressure at the detonation front is approximately 20 to 25 GPa, after which the expansion of the detonation products into the surrounding water causes a rapid pressure decay, the pressure dropping below the graphite stability threshold within approximately 2 to 3 microseconds of the detonation wave passage, at which point any carbon that has not yet transformed to diamond will remain as graphite or amorphous carbon, representing a loss of yield and a source of contamination in the final nanodiamond product — hence the importance of the RDX's high detonation pressure in maximizing the fraction of the detonation's temporal pressure profile that lies above the diamond stability threshold, and the trade-off between the RDX content (which increases the detonation pressure and therefore the diamond yield) and the TNT content (which provides the excess carbon that constitutes the raw material for diamond formation, and which, if replaced entirely by RDX, would produce a very high detonation pressure but very little free carbon, because the RDX's oxygen content would consume nearly all its carbon, leaving little to form diamond), the 60:40 TNT:RDX ratio selected for the Atlantis process representing the optimal balance between carbon yield and detonation pressure as determined through a series of test detonations in the LPC, in which the TNT:RDX ratio was varied from 80:20 to 40:60 in 10-percentage-point increments and the resulting nanodiamond yield was measured by collecting the solid detonation residue — the "detonation soot" — from the detonation chamber after each test, purifying the soot by the acid oxidation process described below to remove the non-diamond carbon phases, and weighing the purified nanodiamond product, the yield being expressed as a percentage of the total carbon content of the explosive charge (including both the carbon from the TNT and RDX molecules themselves and the carbon from the added graphite precursor), with the maximum yield of approximately 8.2 percent occurring at the 60:40 TNT:RDX ratio, a yield that, while seemingly low — meaning that 91.8 percent of the carbon in the explosive charge is lost to gaseous oxidation products or remains as non-diamond carbon that is removed during the purification process — is actually quite efficient by the standards of detonation nanodiamond synthesis, where yields of 5 to 10 percent are typical because the extreme conditions necessary for diamond formation are inherently brief and localized, with much of the carbon in the explosive charge experiencing pressures below the diamond stability threshold or temperatures insufficient for the graphite-to-diamond transformation, and where the yield is limited not by the total energy of the detonation but by the fraction of the carbon that happens to be in the right place at the right time — within the detonation front, at the peak pressure and temperature, for a sufficient duration — to undergo the phase transformation.

The explosive charge, consisting of the TNT/RDX mixture with the graphite powder additive (synthetic graphite powder, 99.9 percent purity, -325 mesh (particle size < 44 micrometers), added at 10 percent by weight of the TNT/RDX mass — an addition that increases the total carbon content of the charge by approximately 60 percent, substantially increasing the nanodiamond yield above what would be achieved from the TNT carbon alone, and that the process engineering team determined to be optimal through the same designed experiment that varied the TNT:RDX ratio, with the graphite addition level also varied from 0 to 20 percent by weight, the yield increasing approximately linearly with graphite addition up to 10 percent, beyond which the additional graphite acts as a heat sink, absorbing energy from the detonation and reducing the peak temperature below the threshold for the graphite-to-diamond transformation, causing the yield to plateau and then decline, a non-linear behavior that is characteristic of the detonation nanodiamond process and that reflects the competition between the increased carbon availability from the graphite additive and the decreased peak temperature due to the heat capacity of the added graphite), is cast into cylindrical charges of 200 millimeters diameter and 400 millimeters length, each charge weighing approximately 25 kilograms and containing sufficient carbon to produce approximately 2.0 kilograms of purified lonsdaleite nanodiamond (at the 8.2 percent carbon-to-diamond conversion efficiency), with the charges being cast in a dedicated explosives compounding building — a reinforced concrete structure with blow-out panels on the roof and three walls, designed to direct any accidental explosion upward and away from the adjacent facilities, and equipped with conductive flooring, humidity control (35-50 percent RH, because TNT and RDX are hygroscopic and absorb moisture from the air, which degrades their detonation performance by reducing the detonation velocity and increasing the amount of energy wasted in vaporizing the absorbed water rather than driving the diamond-forming reactions), and static electricity dissipation measures (grounding straps on all personnel, conductive flooring with a resistance of 10⁴ to 10⁶ ohms, and ionization bars above the casting table to neutralize any static charge accumulation) to prevent the accidental initiation of the explosive by electrostatic discharge, a hazard that, while rare in modern explosives handling facilities equipped with proper static control measures, is nevertheless the leading cause of accidental explosions in the explosives manufacturing industry, accounting for approximately 23 percent of all reported incidents according to the Institute of Makers of Explosives (IME) safety database, and that the Mojave LPC's safety protocols address through a multi-layered defense that includes the static control measures described above, the use of non-sparking tools (beryllium-copper alloy, hardness HRC 35-40, non-sparking per the US Department of Defense MIL-STD-1901A standard for munition production safety), the limitation of personnel in the compounding building to a maximum of two at any time (reducing the number of potential casualties in the event of an accident), and the remote initiation of the detonation from a control bunker located 500 meters from the LPC, connected by armored fiber-optic cable to the detonator firing circuit in the LPC, with the fiber-optic link eliminating any electrical path through which a lightning strike or a power surge could reach the detonator and cause a premature initiation — a scenario that, while extremely improbable in the Mojave Desert's dry, lightning-free climate, is nevertheless addressed because the consequences of a premature detonation, which would destroy the LPC, kill any personnel in the vicinity, and potentially damage the adjacent AFP and composite fabrication buildings through blast and fragment effects (the LPC's reinforced concrete walls, 600 millimeters thick with #8 rebar on 150-millimeter centers in both faces, are designed to contain the blast of up to 50 kilograms of TNT-equivalent high explosive — twice the mass of a single charge — but a premature initiation while the charge is being loaded into the detonation chamber, before the chamber's heavy steel lid has been secured, would produce a blast wave that would exit through the open top of the chamber and would be directed upward by the LPC's funnel-shaped interior geometry, but that would still subject the building structure to a reflected overpressure of approximately 2 to 3 bar, sufficient to cause structural damage that would shut down the LPC for months, delaying the Atlantis program's nanodiamond supply and jeopardizing the production schedule — a programmatic risk that the redundant safety systems — two independent firing circuit isolators, each requiring a unique encrypted code transmitted from the control bunker to arm, and a mechanical safety pin in the detonator that must be manually removed by the operator immediately before retreating to the control bunker, ensuring that a deliberate human action is required to arm the charge and that no combination of equipment failures can cause a premature initiation — are designed to eliminate, and that the LPC's operating history — 120 detonations to date, zero safety incidents — testifies to the effectiveness of.

The detonation is conducted in a water-filled steel chamber — the "detonation tank" — a cylindrical pressure vessel fabricated from 50-millimeter-thick A516 Grade 70 carbon steel plate, with an internal diameter of 3.0 meters, an internal height of 5.0 meters, a hemispherical bottom head, a flanged and bolted flat top head (secured by 48 M64 alloy steel stud bolts, each tensioned to 500 kN using a hydraulic bolt tensioner to provide a uniform gasket seating load on the spiral-wound stainless steel/graphite gasket that seals the top head to the shell, a bolted closure that allows the top head to be removed after each detonation to extract the detonation soot and to clean the chamber interior, a maintenance activity that is performed after every detonation because the detonation deposits a thin layer of carbon soot and metal oxides on the chamber walls, which would accumulate and eventually interfere with the detonation dynamics by altering the acoustic impedance of the wall surface, changing the reflection characteristics of the detonation-generated shock waves and potentially affecting the nanodiamond yield, a subtle effect that the process engineering team confirmed through a series of experiments in which the nanodiamond yield was measured as a function of the number of detonations performed since the last chamber cleaning, with a statistically significant decrease in yield — approximately 0.5 percent per detonation — observed after approximately 10 detonations without cleaning, a decrease that the team attributes to the changing shock reflection conditions and that motivates the every-detonation cleaning protocol, which adds approximately 4 hours to the turnaround time between detonations but which ensures consistent process conditions and maximum nanodiamond yield for every production charge), designed for a maximum allowable working pressure (MAWP) of 30 MPa (300 bar) at a design temperature of 100°C, per the ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code Section VIII, Division 2 (Alternative Rules for Pressure Vessels, which allows higher design stresses than Division 1, reducing the required wall thickness and the vessel mass, a consideration that is important because the top head must be lifted and replaced after each detonation, and any reduction in the head mass reduces the crane capacity required and the structural loads on the LPC building frame, the head weighing approximately 8.5 metric tons at the current 50-millimeter thickness — significantly more than the 20-ton capacity of the LPC's overhead bridge crane, which must therefore lift the head using a two-point pick with a spreader beam, a rigging configuration that the LPC's lifting operations procedure specifies and that the crane's annual load test verifies — and that would be approximately 12 metric tons if the wall thickness were increased to the approximately 75 millimeters that Division 1 would require for the same MAWP, an increase that would exceed the crane's capacity and require the installation of a larger crane, at a capital cost of approximately $350,000 that the Division 2 design successfully avoided). The chamber is filled with deionized water — approximately 35,000 liters, sufficient to fully submerge the explosive charge, which is suspended at the geometric center of the chamber by a nylon line attached to a quick-release hook on the underside of the top head, the nylon line being selected for its low shock impedance, which minimizes the reflection of the detonation shock wave from the suspension line back into the detonation products, a reflection that could interfere with the diamond nucleation process by creating a secondary compression wave that arrives at the nucleation zone after the primary detonation wave has passed, re-compressing the partially-expanded detonation products and altering the pressure-temperature history of the carbon atoms within them, a perturbation that, while probably too small to significantly affect the nanodiamond yield based on the CFD modeling of the detonation hydrodynamics, the process engineering team eliminates as a variable by using the low-impedance nylon suspension rather than a metallic cable, which would have a much higher shock impedance and would produce a stronger reflected wave — a minor process optimization that exemplifies the LPC team's approach to process development: identify every variable that could affect the nanodiamond yield or quality, determine whether it can be controlled, and if so, control it, even if the effect is believed to be small, because the cumulative effect of many uncontrolled small variables is a large uncontrolled variability in the product, and the Atlantis program's quality requirements — nanodiamond particle size distribution with a median diameter of 5.0 ± 0.5 nanometers, a hexagonal phase fraction of at least 80 percent as determined by X-ray diffraction (described below), and a purity of at least 99.0 percent carbon with less than 0.5 percent residual metal oxide contamination from the detonation chamber walls and the explosive residues — demand the highest achievable consistency from the nanodiamond production process, a consistency that can only be achieved by controlling every variable, no matter how small its individual effect, an approach that is resource-intensive but that is justified by the criticality of the nanodiamond to the LBFRP-001 composite's mechanical properties and, ultimately, to the Atlantis pod's structural safety, a safety that, as the Aegis C safety philosophy repeatedly emphasizes, is non-negotiable.

The detonation is initiated by a commercial electric detonator (Dyno Nobel Nonel LP series, a non-electric shock tube detonator that uses a plastic tube coated on its interior with a thin layer of reactive explosive — HMX (octogen) and aluminum powder — which transmits a detonation signal from the initiator to the detonator base charge at a velocity of approximately 2,000 meters per second, the shock tube providing complete immunity to electrical initiation hazards — static electricity, radio frequency radiation, stray electrical currents — which are the primary cause of accidental detonator initiation, a safety advantage that motivated the selection of the non-electric system over the electric detonators that are more commonly used in mining and construction blasting but that introduce an electrical path from the firing circuit to the detonator, a path through which any electrical fault — a lightning strike on the power line, a short circuit in the firing cable, a static discharge from a person or object contacting the cable — could reach the detonator bridgewire and cause a premature initiation, a hazard that the LPC's comprehensive safety analysis determined was unacceptable for an explosives facility operating with multi-kilogram charges of military high explosives and that the Nonel system eliminates by its very design), embedded in a cast pentolite booster charge (a 50:50 mixture of PETN (pentaerythritol tetranitrate) and TNT, cast into a cylindrical pellet of 40 millimeters diameter and 80 millimeters length, providing a total explosive mass of approximately 120 grams, sufficient to reliably initiate the main TNT/RDX charge, which is a relatively insensitive secondary explosive that requires a substantial shock stimulus — a detonation pressure of at least approximately 10 GPa — to initiate, a stimulus that the pentolite booster provides, its detonation pressure of approximately 28 GPa (at its theoretical maximum density of 1.67 g/cm³) far exceeding the TNT/RDX initiation threshold and ensuring reliable detonation of the main charge, a reliability that the LPC has confirmed across all 120 detonations to date, with zero failures to initiate — a perfect record that reflects the robust design of the initiation train, the conservative sizing of the booster charge (which is approximately 50 percent larger than the minimum required for reliable initiation, providing a margin that accounts for the statistical variability in booster charge density, the detonator-to-booster coupling, and the booster-to-main charge coupling, all of which can degrade the initiation stimulus if they are not optimal), and the rigorous quality control of the detonator and booster charge manufacture, which is performed by Dyno Nobel at their certified manufacturing facility in Carthage, Missouri, under the same quality management system that supplies detonators to the US mining industry, where the consequences of a misfire — an unexploded charge left in the ground, potentially to be detonated later by subsequent blasting or by heavy equipment — are severe enough to warrant the highest standards of detonator reliability, standards that the Atlantis program benefits from even though its concern is not a misfire in a mine but a misfire in the LPC, which would require the draining of the detonation tank, the removal of the top head, the manual extraction of the unexploded charge from the tank — an operation that, while the charge is underwater (reducing its sensitivity to shock and friction, because water is an excellent shock absorber and the charge's explosive sensitivity is lower when wet), is nevertheless an uncomfortable procedure for the explosives technician who must perform it, and that the LPC's protocols mitigate by requiring a minimum of 4 hours of waiting time after a misfire before any personnel approaches the LPC, to allow any smoldering or slow-burning reaction in the detonator or booster to either self-extinguish or progress to detonation, at which point the detonation chamber would safely contain the blast, with no personnel in harm's way — a protocol that, while it has never been needed (given the zero-misfire record), is maintained as a matter of principle, because the one time it is needed, the absence of a protocol could result in a fatality.

Upon detonation, the explosive charge is converted within approximately 10 microseconds from a solid cylinder into a supercritical fluid of carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, and oxygen at a temperature of approximately 3,000 kelvin and a pressure of approximately 20 gigapascals — conditions that, as noted above, lie within the diamond stability field on the Bundy phase diagram, and that therefore thermodynamically favor the crystallization of the dissolved carbon as diamond rather than as graphite, the diamond phase being approximately 2.9 kJ/mol lower in free energy than graphite at these conditions, a relatively small thermodynamic driving force that reflects the fact that diamond is only marginally more stable than graphite at high pressure, the substantial free energy barrier that normally prevents the graphite-to-diamond transformation at ambient conditions being overcome not by the thermodynamic driving force but by the extreme pressure, which compresses the graphite's sp²-hybridized carbon atoms into closer proximity, facilitating the rehybridization to sp³ and the formation of the tetrahedral diamond lattice, a transition whose kinetics have been studied through molecular dynamics simulations using the reactive empirical bond order (REBO) potential for carbon, which models the making and breaking of carbon-carbon bonds as a function of the atomic positions and which, when applied to the detonation synthesis conditions, predicts that the diamond nucleation begins approximately 2 to 5 nanoseconds after the passage of the detonation front — the carbon atoms, initially in a disordered, high-temperature fluid state, agglomerating into nanometer-scale clusters that, if the local pressure and temperature favor diamond over graphite, grow as diamond crystallites rather than as graphite platelets, the critical nucleus size being on the order of 1 to 2 nanometers (approximately 100 to 200 carbon atoms), below which the surface energy penalty of the diamond-fluid interface exceeds the volume free energy benefit of the diamond phase relative to the fluid, and the nucleus is unstable and re-dissolves, but above which the nucleus is stable and grows by accretion of additional carbon atoms from the surrounding fluid, the growth rate being limited by the diffusion of carbon atoms through the fluid and by the rate at which the latent heat of crystallization can be conducted away from the growing crystallite, a heat transfer limitation that becomes more severe as the crystallite grows larger — because the surface area through which heat can be conducted increases only as the square of the crystallite radius, while the volume that is generating heat through crystallization increases as the cube of the radius, such that the crystallite's internal temperature rises as it grows, eventually reaching a point at which the crystallite can no longer remain in the diamond stability field (the temperature increase pushing the local conditions above the diamond-graphite phase boundary) and the crystallite either transforms to graphite — losing the lonsdaleite product — or stops growing, its final size determined by the balance between the carbon supply rate, the heat conduction rate, and the cooling rate of the surrounding detonation products as they expand into the water, a complex set of interacting phenomena that, collectively, determine the nanodiamond's particle size distribution, with the median diameter of 4 to 6 nanometers being characteristic of the detonation synthesis process regardless of the explosive composition, the charge geometry, or the cooling medium, a remarkable invariance that reflects the fundamental kinetics of diamond nucleation and growth in the detonation environment and that is fortunate for the Atlantis program, because it means that the 5-nanometer nanodiamond produced by the LPC is essentially identical in size to the nanodiamond produced by other detonation synthesis facilities worldwide, and that the Atlantis program's extensive characterization of this material — its dispersion behavior (PART-085), its effect on the Elium resin's mechanical properties (PART-086 and PART-087), and its long-term stability in the composite (PART-087 and the accelerated aging program described in PART-082) — is applicable not only to the Mojave-produced material but to any detonation-synthesized nanodiamond that meets the particle size and purity specifications, a supply chain resilience consideration that PART-092, the supply chain analysis, identifies as a risk mitigation strategy: if the LPC were damaged or its explosives supply were interrupted, the Atlantis program could source nanodiamond from alternative suppliers — the Russian Academy of Sciences' Institute of High Pressure Physics, the Chinese Academy of Sciences' Lanzhou Institute of Chemical Physics, or the private-sector nanodiamond producers that have emerged in the United States (Ray Techniques Ltd. of Israel, through its US subsidiary) and Europe (PlasmaChem GmbH of Germany) — without requiring a re-qualification of the composite's mechanical properties, because the nanodiamond from these alternative sources, if produced by the same detonation synthesis process and meeting the same specifications, is functionally identical to the Mojave-produced material, a fungibility that the Atlantis program has confirmed through a comparative testing program in which nanodiamond samples from three different suppliers were incorporated into LBFRP-001 composite coupons and tested for tensile strength, interlaminar shear strength, and compression-after-impact strength, with the results showing no statistically significant difference among the three suppliers' materials, confirming the fungibility assumption and validating the supply diversification strategy.

Following the detonation, the detonation products expand into the surrounding water, which cools them rapidly — the water, initially at ambient temperature (approximately 20°C), absorbs the thermal energy of the detonation products, vaporizing a fraction of its mass at the detonation product-water interface (the "steam bubble" that forms around the detonation zone and that collapses as the steam condenses on the cold chamber walls, the collapse generating a secondary pressure pulse that is smaller than the primary detonation shock but that can, if the chamber is not adequately vented, create pressure oscillations that fatigue the chamber walls, a phenomenon that the LPC's design mitigates through a pressure relief system — a 200-millimeter-diameter rupture disc, set to burst at 25 MPa, venting to a 500-millimeter-diameter exhaust pipe that discharges through the LPC roof to the atmosphere, the pipe being equipped with a silencer (a series of perforated baffle plates that dissipate the acoustic energy of the vented gas, reducing the noise level at the property boundary to below the Mojave County noise ordinance limit of 65 dBA during daytime hours, a regulatory compliance requirement that the LPC's operating permit mandates and that the facility's environmental monitoring program verifies through quarterly noise measurements at the property boundary, with all measurements to date being within the permit limits) and a particulate filter (a high-efficiency baghouse filter that captures any solid particulate — primarily carbon soot and metal oxide dust from the detonation — that is entrained in the vented gas, preventing its release to the atmosphere and ensuring compliance with the facility's air quality permit, issued by the Mojave Desert Air Quality Management District under the authority of the federal Clean Air Act, with the permit limiting the facility's total particulate emissions to less than 10 tons per year, a limit that the LPC's baghouse filter operates well within, with actual emissions of approximately 0.5 tons per year as measured by the facility's continuous emissions monitoring system) — and quenching the diamond-forming reactions within approximately 100 to 200 microseconds of the detonation, freezing the nanodiamond crystallites at their nucleation size and preventing further growth, a rapid quench that is essential for producing the nanometer-scale particles that are the defining characteristic of the detonation nanodiamond product and that provide the exceptional reinforcement efficiency — the 5-nanometer diameter corresponding to a specific surface area of approximately 400 square meters per gram, meaning that every gram of nanodiamond dispersed in the Elium matrix provides 400 square meters of nanodiamond-matrix interface area, across which the load transfer from the matrix to the nanodiamond reinforcement occurs, a staggering interfacial area that is approximately 1,000 times larger than the interfacial area provided by the basalt fibers (which, at 9 micrometers diameter, have a specific surface area of approximately 0.4 square meters per gram), reflecting the fundamental scaling of specific surface area with the inverse of the particle or fiber diameter and explaining why nanoscale reinforcements, at relatively low weight fractions (3 to 5 weight percent), can produce significant improvements in the composite's mechanical properties — improvements that, for the LBFRP-001 composite, include a 25 percent increase in Mode I interlaminar fracture toughness, an 18 percent increase in compression-after-impact strength, and a 15 percent increase in fatigue life under cyclic compressive loading, as documented in the LBFRP-001 qualification test report — that would require a much higher loading of a microscale reinforcement, a loading that would increase the composite's density, degrade its processability, and increase its cost, none of which are desirable for the Atlantis application.

The detonation soot — the solid residue collected from the detonation chamber floor and walls after each detonation — consists of a mixture of nanodiamond (both the cubic and hexagonal polymorphs, with the hexagonal lonsdaleite fraction typically accounting for 60 to 85 percent of the total diamond content, the remainder being cubic diamond, graphite, and amorphous carbon that did not transform to diamond during the detonation, the relative proportions depending on the detonation conditions — the peak pressure, the peak temperature, the quench rate, and the local chemical environment within the detonation products, all of which vary spatially and temporally within the detonation zone, producing a distribution of diamond nucleation and growth conditions that, inevitably, produces a mixture of diamond phases and non-diamond carbon phases, a distribution that the subsequent purification process (described below) narrows through the selective removal of the non-diamond carbon, but that never reduces to a single phase, because the hexagonal lonsdaleite and the cubic diamond are both diamond — they are chemically identical, differing only in the stacking sequence of the carbon atom layers (ABAB... for hexagonal lonsdaleite, ABCABC... for cubic diamond), a structural difference that affects the mechanical properties but not the chemical reactivity, meaning that the chemical purification methods that remove graphite and amorphous carbon cannot distinguish between lonsdaleite and cubic diamond, both of which survive the purification process and are both present in the final nanodiamond product, a mixed-phase product that is nevertheless acceptable for the Atlantis application because the mechanical properties of the two phases are comparable — lonsdaleite being approximately 60 percent harder than cubic diamond, as noted above, but both providing the exceptional stiffness (approximately 1,200 GPa elastic modulus for lonsdaleite, approximately 1,050 GPa for cubic diamond, both approximately 5 to 6 times stiffer than the basalt fiber's 89 GPa and approximately 400 times stiffer than the Elium matrix's 3 GPa, a stiffness contrast that is the fundamental driver of the composite reinforcement effect, because, under an applied load, the stiffer reinforcement bears a disproportionately large share of the load, its strain lagging behind the matrix strain, and the load transfer from the matrix to the reinforcement occurring through shear stresses at the reinforcement-matrix interface, stresses that are proportional to the stiffness difference between the two phases — the St. Venant shear lag model, applied to the nanodiamond-Elium system, predicts that the nanodiamond particles, at 5-nanometer diameter and 1,200 GPa modulus embedded in a 3 GPa matrix, achieve full load transfer over a length of approximately 15 nanometers (three particle diameters), meaning that the nanodiamond particles, individually, are too small to sustain a significant stress gradient along their length and effectively act as point reinforcements that stiffen the matrix through the constraint they impose on the polymer chain mobility — a reinforcement mechanism that is fundamentally different from the fiber reinforcement mechanism, in which the fibers, being long and continuous, carry the majority of the applied load and the matrix serves primarily to transfer load between fibers and to protect the fibers from environmental degradation), and the dispersion quality, the particle-matrix adhesion, and the particle loading concentration are far more significant determinants of the composite's overall mechanical performance than the specific polymorphic composition of the nanodiamond, a conclusion that the Atlantis materials team reached after evaluating composite laminates fabricated with nanodiamond batches having lonsdaleite fractions ranging from 40 to 90 percent — the range achievable through the detonation synthesis process parameters — and finding that the composite's interlaminar fracture toughness, the property most sensitive to the nanodiamond reinforcement, varied by less than 8 percent across this range, a variability that is within the measurement uncertainty of the fracture toughness test method (ASTM D5528, the double cantilever beam test for Mode I interlaminar fracture toughness of unidirectional fiber-reinforced polymer composites, which has a between-laboratory coefficient of variation of approximately 10 percent according to the ASTM interlaboratory study conducted during the standard's development) and that therefore does not indicate a practically significant effect of the nanodiamond polymorph composition on the composite properties, confirming that the mixed-phase product produced by the LPC is acceptable for the Atlantis application without the need for the polymorph-specific separation that would be required if lonsdaleite's superior hardness were essential to the composite's performance — a separation that would add complexity, cost, and yield loss to the nanodiamond production process, and that the materials team was relieved to determine is unnecessary, because the distinction between cubic and hexagonal diamond, while academically interesting and the subject of extensive crystallographic research, is not practically significant for the composite reinforcement application that is the purpose of the Atlantis program's nanodiamond production.

The purification of the detonation soot to remove the non-diamond carbon phases — an essential step, because the graphite and amorphous carbon in the soot, if incorporated into the composite laminate, would act as defects — weak, low-stiffness inclusions that concentrate stress and initiate cracks, degrading the composite's strength and fatigue life rather than enhancing them, exactly the opposite of the intended nanodiamond reinforcement effect — is performed by a two-stage acid oxidation process, adapted from the purification method developed by the Russian Academy of Sciences and validated for the Atlantis nanodiamond through a series of experiments that varied the acid concentration, the treatment temperature, the treatment duration, and the agitation method, with the resulting nanodiamond purity (measured by the weight loss upon combustion in a thermogravimetric analyzer (TGA) under oxygen atmosphere, with the combustion temperature — the temperature at which the carbon oxidizes to CO₂, approximately 550°C for nanodiamond and approximately 450°C for the non-diamond carbon phases, the lower combustion temperature of the non-diamond carbon reflecting its higher chemical reactivity due to the greater abundance of edge-plane carbon atoms, which are more susceptible to oxidation than the basal-plane carbon atoms that dominate the diamond surface — providing a measure of the relative proportions of diamond and non-diamond carbon in the sample, the TGA weight loss curve showing a two-step mass loss, the first step at approximately 450°C corresponding to the oxidation of the non-diamond carbon and the second step at approximately 550°C corresponding to the oxidation of the nanodiamond, with the mass loss at each step being proportional to the amount of the respective carbon phase in the sample, a quantitative purity assay that is performed on every purification batch and that must show a purity of at least 99.0 percent (i.e., no more than 1.0 percent non-diamond carbon) for the batch to be accepted for use in the Atlantis composite fabrication) being the response variable optimized. The first stage of the acid oxidation uses concentrated sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄, 98 percent concentration) mixed with concentrated nitric acid (HNO₃, 70 percent concentration) in a 3:1 volume ratio — the standard "piranha solution" mixture that is used in the semiconductor and nanotechnology industries to remove organic contaminants from surfaces, its name deriving from its voracious appetite for carbon, which it oxidizes to CO₂ through a series of reactions in which the sulfuric acid dehydrates the carbon, creating reactive sites that the nitric acid then oxidizes, with the overall reaction being approximately C + 2H₂SO₄ + 2HNO₃ → CO₂ + 2NO₂ + 2SO₂ + 3H₂O (the actual reaction being more complex, producing a mixture of nitrogen oxides — NO, NO₂, N₂O — and sulfur oxides — SO₂, SO₃ — as byproducts, all of which are toxic gases that must be scrubbed from the process exhaust, a requirement that the LPC's acid treatment system meets through a packed-bed scrubber using a 10 percent sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution as the scrubbing medium, which neutralizes the acid gases to form sodium nitrate (NaNO₃) and sodium sulfate (Na₂SO₄), both of which are water-soluble and are discharged to the Mojave facility's wastewater treatment system after dilution to below the permit limits for total dissolved solids and nitrate concentration, as monitored by the facility's continuous water quality monitoring system on the wastewater discharge line, ensuring compliance with the facility's National Pollutant Discharge Elimination System (NPDES) permit issued by the California State Water Resources Control Board). The detonation soot is mixed with the acid mixture in a ratio of 1 gram of soot to 20 milliliters of acid, in a glass-lined steel reactor vessel equipped with a reflux condenser (to return any volatilized acid to the reactor, preventing the loss of acid and the release of acid vapors to the environment), a mechanical stirrer (a PTFE-coated paddle impeller rotating at 200 rpm to maintain the soot in suspension and ensure uniform contact with the acid), and an external heating jacket that raises the mixture temperature to 120 ± 5°C, at which temperature the oxidation of the non-diamond carbon proceeds at a manageable rate — not so fast that the reaction overheats and produces a "runaway" exotherm that would boil the acid and potentially breach the reactor's pressure relief system, but fast enough that the treatment can be completed within a practical batch cycle time, which for the LPC's 0.5-kilogram soot batches is approximately 8 hours, after which the reactor is cooled to below 50°C, the acid mixture is diluted with an equal volume of deionized water (carefully, adding the acid to the water, NEVER the reverse, because the addition of water to concentrated sulfuric acid generates an enormous amount of heat from the hydration reaction of the sulfuric acid molecules with the water molecules, sufficient to cause localized boiling and spattering of the hot acid, a serious personnel burn hazard that the LPC's standard operating procedure mitigates through the use of a jacketed addition vessel with a controlled addition rate of 50 milliliters per minute, with continuous temperature monitoring of the receiving vessel and an automatic shutoff valve that closes if the temperature exceeds 80°C), the diluted mixture is filtered through a 0.1-micrometer PTFE membrane filter to recover the partially-purified nanodiamond, which is then washed with repeated aliquots of deionized water — typically 10 wash cycles of 1 liter of water each, with the wash water being tested for electrical conductivity after each cycle, and the washing continuing until the conductivity of the wash water is below 10 microsiemens per centimeter (μS/cm), indicating that the residual acid has been removed from the nanodiamond surface, where it would otherwise interfere with the silane coupling agent chemistry described in PART-086 by protonating the amine groups on the silane molecules, preventing them from forming the covalent bonds with the Elium resin that are the basis of the nanodiamond-matrix adhesion, a chemical interference that would negate the nanodiamond's reinforcement benefit — and then dried in a vacuum oven at 80°C for 24 hours, producing a fine, dark gray powder — the partially-purified nanodiamond, which at this stage still contains approximately 5 to 10 percent non-diamond carbon, primarily the more crystalline graphite phases that are more resistant to acid oxidation than the amorphous carbon, and that requires the second purification stage to remove.

The second purification stage uses a milder oxidation treatment — a mixture of ammonium persulfate ((NH₄)₂S₂O₈) and sulfuric acid, at a concentration of 10 percent ammonium persulfate in 20 percent sulfuric acid, heated to 80°C for 24 hours with continuous stirring, a treatment that selectively oxidizes the remaining graphite and amorphous carbon — which, because of their higher chemical reactivity (the edge-plane carbon atoms, as noted above, being more susceptible to oxidation than the basal-plane atoms), are oxidized at a temperature that does not significantly attack the nanodiamond particles themselves, the ammonium persulfate acting as the oxidizing agent, its decomposition at 80°C generating sulfate free radicals that react with the carbon surface, forming oxygen-containing functional groups (hydroxyl, carbonyl, carboxyl) that render the carbon water-soluble at the elevated temperature of the treatment and that are subsequently removed during the final washing and drying steps, leaving behind the purified nanodiamond with a non-diamond carbon content of less than 1.0 percent as verified by TGA, the target purity that the LBFRP-001 material specification requires, based on the statistically-designed experiment (DOE) that correlated the nanodiamond purity (varied by blending purified nanodiamond with known amounts of the non-diamond carbon residue from the purification process to create a series of nanodiamond batches with purities ranging from 95 to 99.5 percent) with the interlaminar fracture toughness of the resulting LBFRP-001 composite laminates, finding that the fracture toughness increased with increasing purity up to approximately 99.0 percent, beyond which the improvement was within the measurement uncertainty of the fracture toughness test, indicating that the remaining 1 percent of non-diamond carbon — which, at a nanodiamond loading of 3 weight percent in the laminate, corresponds to a non-diamond carbon concentration of approximately 0.03 weight percent in the composite as a whole, a concentration that is below the threshold at which the non-diamond carbon inclusions would behave as critical-sized flaws — has a negligible effect on the composite's mechanical properties, and that the 99.0 percent purity specification is both sufficient for the Atlantis application and achievable by the LPC's two-stage purification process with a high process capability index (Cpk), as demonstrated by the statistical process control (SPC) data from the purification of 50 nanodiamond batches to date, which show a mean purity of 99.3 percent with a standard deviation of 0.2 percent, a Cpk of 1.7 relative to the 99.0 percent lower specification limit, exceeding the Cpk ≥ 1.33 threshold that the Aegis C quality management system requires for all critical material properties, as described in PART-088, and confirming that the purification process is both capable and stable, producing nanodiamond of consistent quality that the composite fabrication process can rely upon.

The hexagonal phase confirmation — the verification that the nanodiamond product indeed contains lonsdaleite, the hexagonal diamond polymorph, rather than being composed entirely of cubic diamond, a distinction that is essential for the Atlantis program's claim to be exploiting the superior hardness of the lonsdaleite phase (a claim that, as noted above, turns out to have little practical significance for the composite's mechanical properties, but that is nevertheless a matter of intellectual honesty — the program must know what it is making, even if what it is making does not matter as much as was originally hypothesized — and, equally importantly, of the nanodiamond's provenance, because if a supplier were to deliver cubic nanodiamond while claiming it was lonsdaleite, the fraud would be undetectable without the crystallographic analysis described below, an analysis that is therefore an essential component of the quality control and supply chain verification program, regardless of whether the lonsdaleite content significantly affects the composite's properties) — is performed by X-ray diffraction (XRD), the standard technique for determining the crystal structure of solid materials. The nanodiamond powder is loaded into the sample holder of a Bruker D8 Advance X-ray diffractometer, configured in the Bragg-Brentano geometry with a copper K-alpha X-ray source (wavelength λ = 0.15418 nanometers, generated by a copper anode X-ray tube operating at 40 kV and 40 mA, the K-alpha radiation being selected for the analysis of nanodiamond because it provides the optimal combination of penetration depth (approximately 50 micrometers in the nanodiamond powder sample, sufficient to sample a representative volume of the powder rather than just the surface layers, which might be contaminated by adsorbed species from the purification process — residual acid, water, or organic compounds — that would give a misleading diffraction pattern), angular resolution (the width of the diffraction peaks being determined by the instrumental broadening and the sample-dependent broadening from the small crystallite size, with the Scherrer equation, D = Kλ / (β cos θ), where D is the crystallite size, K is the Scherrer constant (approximately 0.9 for spherical crystallites), λ is the X-ray wavelength, β is the full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) of the diffraction peak after correction for instrumental broadening, and θ is the Bragg angle of the peak, relating the peak broadening to the crystallite size, such that a smaller crystallite produces a broader peak, and the 5-nanometer lonsdaleite crystallites produce peaks with a FWHM of approximately 2 to 3 degrees in 2θ, which are broad enough that they partially overlap with the adjacent peaks, complicating the phase identification but still resolvable with careful peak fitting using the Rietveld method — a whole-pattern refinement technique that models the entire diffraction pattern as the sum of the contributions from each phase present in the sample, adjusting the phase fractions, the lattice parameters, the crystallite sizes, and the instrumental parameters to achieve the best fit between the calculated and the measured pattern, a computationally-intensive analysis that the Mojave materials characterization laboratory performs using the Bruker TOPAS software package, with the fit quality assessed by the R-weighted pattern (Rwp) parameter, which for the Atlantis nanodiamond samples is typically below 5 percent, indicating an excellent fit between the model and the data and providing confidence that the phase fractions determined by the Rietveld analysis — typically 75 to 85 percent lonsdaleite (hexagonal diamond), 10 to 15 percent cubic diamond, and 5 to 10 percent disordered or amorphous carbon that is not detected by the XRD because it lacks long-range crystalline order — are accurate to within approximately ±5 percent, a precision that is more than sufficient for the quality control requirement of confirming that the lonsdaleite fraction is at least 60 percent — a deliberately conservative specification that any detonation synthesis process operating within the established parameter ranges should easily meet, and that the LPC's process achieves with a comfortable margin, the mean lonsdaleite fraction being 79 percent with a standard deviation of 4 percent across the 50 batches analyzed, a Cpk of 1.6 relative to the 60 percent lower specification limit, exceeding the Cpk ≥ 1.33 target and confirming the capability of the LPC's detonation synthesis to produce lonsdaleite-rich nanodiamond at production scale and production quality.

The lonsdaleite nanodiamond, as synthesized and purified by the process described in this document, is the most exotic material component of the Atlantis composite architecture, a material whose production involves the controlled detonation of military high explosives in a water-filled pressure vessel, the collection of the resulting carbon soot, the oxidation of the non-diamond carbon phases in boiling acid, and the crystallographic verification of the hexagonal diamond structure by X-ray diffraction — a process that seems, at first glance, almost absurdly violent and dangerous for the production of a material whose final role is to be quietly dispersed at a few weight percent within the polymer matrix of a composite, invisible to the naked eye, its contribution to the pod's structural integrity known only to the engineers who specified it and the quality assurance technicians who verified its purity. Yet this apparent disproportion between the violence of the production process and the subtlety of the material's function is not a flaw in the engineering but a testament to it: the Atlantis program, faced with the requirement to protect human lives at the bottom of the sea against forces that will, when they arrive, exceed anything that conventional engineering materials can withstand, has reached into the extreme of materials science — into the pressure-temperature regime of the Earth's deep interior, into the detonation chemistry of military explosives, into the nanoscale where atoms assemble into crystallites that are measured in billionths of a meter — and has extracted from that extreme a material whose properties, accumulated across trillions of individual 5-nanometer particles dispersed throughout the composite hull, make the difference between survival and destruction. The detonations that produce this material are loud, violent, and dangerous, but they are also, in the final analysis, the necessary price of admission to a regime of material performance that nature does not provide — that must be created, through the deliberate application of extreme conditions, from the carbon that constitutes the ashes of the stars from which the Earth itself was formed, a poetic symmetry that the Spruce-Drake register of the Dolittle persona would undoubtedly appreciate, and that the practical-minded engineers of the Kiddo and Torvalds registers, while less moved by the poetry, accept as the technical reality of their material supply chain: no lonsdaleite without detonation, no detonation without high explosives, no high explosives without the elaborate safety infrastructure that the LPC represents, and no Atlantis pod without the nanodiamond that the LPC produces. The chain of causality is direct, and it begins — and ends — with the controlled violence of the detonation that, for a few microseconds, recreates the conditions at the center of the Earth, and that, in those microseconds, creates the material that will protect human beings at the bottom of the sea.
