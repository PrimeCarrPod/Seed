# CSMFAB077-PART-081 — AUTOMATED FIBER PLACEMENT: MOJAVE AFP MACHINES — FULL TECHNICAL SPECIFICATION
## Block 8 — Fabrication Methods | Atlantis-Class Subsea Habitation Module Production
## Carrington Storm Motors — Safe Pod Engineering Division | Aegis C Material Architecture

---

The automated fiber placement (AFP) infrastructure deployed at the Mojave fabrication facility represents the most advanced composite manufacturing capability ever assembled for subsea habitation module production, and its specification deserves exhaustive documentation because every subsequent fabrication step — from basalt fiber drawing through Elium resin impregnation, lonsdaleite nanodiamond dispersion, MXene electrophoretic deposition, and ALON viewport integration — depends on the precision, repeatability, and throughput that only a fully automated fiber placement system can deliver at the production rates required for the Atlantis demonstration program, which calls for ten complete pod hulls plus associated inter-pod tunnel segments, auxetic cladding attachment frames, and ALON viewport mounting rings to be fabricated within an eighteen-month window from program green-light to first deployment off Monterey Canyon, a production schedule that translates to approximately 0.56 completed pod hulls per month or one pod hull every fifty-three calendar days when accounting for machine maintenance downtime, tooling changeovers between the various sub-components of the Atlantis architecture, and the inevitable learning-curve inefficiencies that accompany any first-of-kind composite manufacturing program regardless of the sophistication of the automation involved, and which therefore demands that the AFP machines operate with an overall equipment effectiveness (OEE) of not less than 85 percent — a metric that combines availability (the fraction of scheduled production time during which the machine is actually operating), performance (the ratio of actual production rate to the machine's theoretical maximum production rate), and quality (the fraction of produced material that meets specification without requiring rework) — a target that is aggressive for any composite manufacturing operation but that the Mojave team has validated through the production of the eight Safehold STR-002 pressure vessels documented in Episode Twenty-One, which achieved a cumulative OEE of 87.3 percent across 2,400 hours of AFP machine operation with zero quality-reject hull sections, a record that the Atlantis program intends to match or exceed through the process improvements described throughout this Block 8 series of technical specifications, beginning with the detailed characterization of the AFP machines themselves that constitutes the present document, PART-081.

The Mojave AFP cell is built around two Coriolis Composites C5 gantry-type automated fiber placement machines, each equipped with a six-axis articulated placement head mounted on a rigid steel gantry structure that provides a working envelope of 18 meters in length (X-axis, corresponding to the longitudinal axis of the Atlantis pod hull, which at 15 meters in overall length with tapered end caps requires the full 18-meter envelope to accommodate the placement head's approach and departure trajectories at each end of the mandrel), 5 meters in width (Y-axis, providing lateral positioning across the hull diameter plus clearance for the placement head to access both sides of the vertically oriented mandrel without requiring mandrel rotation, a design choice that simplifies the tooling by eliminating the need for a rotating mandrel support structure and that improves placement accuracy because the mandrel remains stationary during fiber deposition, eliminating the dynamic balancing errors and rotational inertia effects that would otherwise degrade tow placement precision, particularly at the high layup speeds required for economically viable production rates), and 4 meters in height (Z-axis, providing vertical clearance for the placement head to traverse the full circumference of the 3.5-meter-diameter hull cylinder plus the tooling fixtures and vacuum bag consolidation equipment that occupy the space immediately above the mandrel during the layup process), with each linear axis driven by Siemens 1FT7 synchronous servo motors coupled to precision ground ball screws with a pitch of 20 millimeters per revolution and a positioning repeatability of ±0.05 millimeters as measured by Renishaw linear optical encoders with a resolution of 0.001 millimeters, a positioning accuracy that is essential for the precise fiber orientation control that the Atlantis hull laminate design demands — specifically, a stacking sequence of [0°/±45°/90°/±45°/0°]s for the cylindrical hull section, transitioning to [0°/±30°/±60°/90°/±60°/±30°/0°] for the hemispherical end caps, with each ply oriented to within ±1 degree of its specified angle relative to the hull's longitudinal axis, a tolerance that is achievable only through the combination of high-accuracy machine positioning, closed-loop tow tension control, and the in-process inspection system described in PART-088 that continuously monitors tow placement and triggers automatic correction if any tow deviates from its specified path by more than 0.5 millimeters, which corresponds to approximately one-quarter of a tow width for the 6.35-millimeter (0.25-inch) tows used in the Atlantis layup.

The six-axis placement head on each C5 machine provides three translational degrees of freedom (X, Y, Z via the gantry axes) and three rotational degrees of freedom (A-axis rotation about the X-axis, corresponding to the placement head's roll angle that determines the contact angle between the compaction roller and the mandrel surface; B-axis rotation about the Y-axis, corresponding to the yaw angle that determines the steering direction of the tow as it follows curved paths around the hull's hemispherical end caps, where the tow path curvature changes continuously as the head traverses from the cylindrical section toward the pole of the end cap, requiring continuous yaw adjustment to maintain the specified fiber orientation and to prevent tow wrinkling or buckling that would create resin-rich pockets and degrade the laminate's compressive strength by as much as 40 percent relative to a wrinkle-free laminate of identical fiber volume fraction, according to the defect tolerance analysis documented in PART-088; and C-axis rotation about the Z-axis, corresponding to the pitch angle that maintains the compaction roller perpendicular to the local mandrel surface tangent plane, ensuring uniform compaction pressure across the full width of the tow band and preventing the edge-lifting defects that occur when the compaction roller contacts the tow at an angle, applying higher pressure to one edge of the tow than the other and creating a gradient in fiber volume fraction across the tow width that manifests as a periodic pattern of porosity bands in the cured laminate at a spatial frequency equal to the tow width, a defect that the ultrasonic inspection system described in PART-087 is specifically programmed to detect through its characteristic C-scan signature of alternating high-attenuation and low-attenuation bands aligned with the tow placement direction), with all six axes coordinated through a Siemens Sinumerik 840D sl CNC controller running custom composite-specific kinematic transformation algorithms developed jointly by Coriolis Composites and the Carrington Storm Motors automation engineering team, algorithms that translate the toolpath generated by the off-line programming system (described in detail below) into coordinated six-axis motion commands at a control loop update rate of 2 milliseconds, fast enough to maintain the specified ±0.05-millimeter positional accuracy even when the placement head is traversing curved paths at the maximum layup speed of 1.2 meters per second, which corresponds to a tow placement rate of approximately 7.6 kilograms of composite material per hour per machine when laying 8 tows simultaneously at the standard 6.35-millimeter tow width with a 50 percent overlap between adjacent tow courses, a rate that translates to approximately 182 kilograms of material deposited per twenty-four-hour operating day per machine, or approximately 364 kilograms per day for the two-machine cell operating in parallel on separate mandrels, which at the Atlantis pod hull's target mass of approximately 18,500 kilograms of cured LBFRP-001 composite — including the 5-millimeter-thick outer layer with elevated lonsdaleite loading described in PART-086, the structural core laminate, and the interior CNT-reinforced abrasion layer specified in Section 6 of the parent document — translates to a machine operating time of approximately 50.8 days per hull, which with OEE adjustments and tooling changeover time yields the nominal fifty-three-day production cycle cited above, confirming that the two-machine AFP cell meets the Atlantis program's production throughput requirement with approximately 4 percent margin, a margin that the production planning team considers adequate but not generous and that has motivated the investigation of a third C5 machine (described in PART-095, Scalability) to provide redundancy and surge capacity for the 1,000-pod-per-year production rate that the Aegis C long-term deployment plan ultimately requires.

The tow delivery system on each AFP placement head is the critical subsystem that determines the quality of the final laminate, and its specification reflects the extreme performance requirements of the Atlantis application, which demands void content below 0.5 percent by volume, fiber volume fraction of 60 ± 2 percent, and interlaminar shear strength exceeding 65 MPa as measured by the short-beam shear test (ASTM D2344) on specimens extracted from the cured hull at locations corresponding to the highest anticipated in-service shear stresses — specifically, the transition zone between the cylindrical hull section and the hemispherical end caps, where the discontinuity in shell curvature creates a stress concentration that the laminate must accommodate without delamination or matrix cracking. The tow delivery system begins with the creel cabinet, a climate-controlled enclosure maintained at 23 ± 2°C and 35 ± 5 percent relative humidity, housing up to 32 individual tow spools — each spool containing 8,200 meters of continuous basalt fiber tow with a linear density of 2,400 tex (grams per 1,000 meters), pre-impregnated with Elium thermoplastic resin at a resin content of 35 ± 2 percent by weight, wound onto 76-millimeter (3-inch) internal diameter cardboard cores with precision-traverse winding that maintains a tow tension of 2.0 ± 0.2 newtons during spooling to prevent the tow telescoping or cross-winding defects that would cause snagging or breakage during the high-speed AFP layup process, each spool individually monitored by an optical encoder that measures the remaining tow length with an accuracy of ±5 meters and communicates spool status to the CNC controller, which automatically pauses the layup and alerts the operator when any spool reaches 100 meters of remaining tow — sufficient to complete the current tow course and bring the placement head to a safe parking position for spool replacement without creating a dry joint in the laminate, where a paused layup that is subsequently restarted would create a resin-starved region at the restart point due to the cooling and crystallization of the Elium matrix during the pause interval. From the creel cabinet, each tow passes through a series of tension-controlled guide rollers into the tow tension control module, a servo-driven closed-loop tensioning system that maintains each tow at the specified layup tension of 15 ± 1 newtons during placement, using a combination of a load cell (Interface Model 1210, 50 N capacity, 0.02 percent full-scale accuracy) measuring the actual tow tension at the exit of the tension control module, a PID controller executing at 1 kHz update rate that compares the measured tension to the setpoint and adjusts the torque of a brushless DC servo motor driving a capstan roller that either pays out or retracts tow to maintain the setpoint, and a dancer arm with a ±50-millimeter stroke that provides passive compliance to absorb transient tension fluctuations caused by the placement head's six-axis motion, preventing the tension spikes that would otherwise occur when the head accelerates or decelerates during directional changes and that could exceed the tow's breaking strength of approximately 180 newtons (75 MPa tensile strength multiplied by the tow cross-sectional area of approximately 2.4 mm² at 60 percent fiber volume fraction), causing tow breakage and requiring the operator to manually rethread the broken tow through the delivery system — a thirty-minute procedure that, if it occurred frequently, would degrade the AFP machine's OEE below the target threshold and jeopardize the production schedule, and that therefore justifies the additional cost and complexity of the closed-loop tension control system, which has demonstrated a tow breakage rate of less than 0.01 breaks per 1,000 meters of tow placed across the 2,400 hours of Episode Twenty-One production, a rate that the Atlantis program expects to maintain through the identical tension control hardware and the improved Elium prepregging consistency described in PART-086.

The compaction system at the AFP placement head is the final mechanical interface between the machine and the laminate, and its design governs the critical consolidation quality parameters — interlaminar bond strength, void content, and fiber volume fraction — that determine whether the finished hull meets the LBFRP-001 material specification and, by extension, whether the pod survives the 4.5 MPa design external pressure at the Monterey deployment depth of 45 meters with a factor of safety of 4.0, corresponding to a collapse pressure of 18 MPa, a pressure that the hull laminate must withstand without buckling, delamination, or matrix cracking over a fifty-year service life in continuous saltwater immersion, with the added requirement of surviving a crustal displacement event that subjects the hull to transient dynamic pressures from turbidite boulder impacts and flow-induced vibrations that momentarily elevate local stresses above the steady-state design values. The compaction system employs a segmented silicone rubber roller with a diameter of 50 millimeters and a Shore A hardness of 70, selected to provide sufficient compliance to conform to the mandrel surface curvature — which varies from effectively infinite radius on the cylindrical section to a minimum radius of 1,500 millimeters at the transition to the hemispherical end caps — while maintaining sufficient stiffness to apply a compaction pressure of 0.5 to 1.0 MPa (5 to 10 bar) uniformly across the full width of the 8-tow band (50.8 millimeters total band width, corresponding to 8 tows of 6.35-millimeter width each, laid with a 50 percent overlap between adjacent tows to create a staggered joint pattern that distributes the interlaminar shear stress across multiple tow boundaries rather than concentrating it at a single butt joint between adjacent tow courses, a pattern that increases the apparent interlaminar shear strength of the laminate by approximately 15 percent compared to a non-staggered joint pattern according to the mechanical testing data from the LBFRP-001 qualification program). The compaction force is applied by a pneumatic actuator with closed-loop pressure control that maintains the specified compaction pressure regardless of the placement head's orientation relative to gravity — a capability that is essential for the AFP of the Atlantis pod hull, which is laid up on a horizontal mandrel but which requires the placement head to operate at angles ranging from 0 degrees (compaction roller horizontal, applying force downward onto the top of the mandrel, where gravity assists the pneumatic actuator) to 180 degrees (compaction roller horizontal but inverted, applying force upward onto the bottom of the mandrel, where gravity opposes the pneumatic actuator and the pressure must be increased by an amount equal to the weight of the placement head assembly divided by the compaction roller contact area to maintain the same net compaction pressure on the laminate), a compensation that the CNC controller performs automatically based on the six-axis kinematic model of the placement head, ensuring uniform compaction pressure at all orientations and thereby uniform consolidation quality throughout the hull laminate — a uniformity that the ultrasonic inspection data from the Episode Twenty-One hulls confirmed, with measured interlaminar bond strength varying by less than 5 percent between specimens extracted from the top, side, and bottom quadrants of the hull cylinder, a variability that is within the measurement uncertainty of the short-beam shear test method and that testifies to the effectiveness of the orientation-compensated compaction control algorithm.

The heating system at the placement head provides the thermal energy required to melt the Elium thermoplastic resin at the tow-tool interface — the point where each incoming tow contacts the previously deposited layer — creating the bond between successive plies that constitutes the interlaminar strength of the finished laminate and that is the single most critical quality parameter for a pressure vessel that must resist external hydrostatic compression without buckling or delamination. The heating system employs a diode laser operating at a wavelength of 980 nanometers with a maximum output power of 4 kilowatts, delivered to the nip point — the line of contact between the compaction roller, the incoming tow, and the previously deposited substrate — via a fiber-optic cable terminating in a line-generating optic that projects a rectangular beam with dimensions of 52 millimeters in width (slightly exceeding the 50.8-millimeter tow band width to ensure uniform heating across the full band) and 8 millimeters in length (in the direction of head travel, corresponding to the heated zone length ahead of the compaction roller where the resin must reach its melting temperature of approximately 160°C before entering the nip point, a length that at the maximum layup speed of 1.2 meters per second provides a residence time of approximately 6.7 milliseconds, which is sufficient for the Elium resin to absorb the laser energy and transition from its room-temperature solid state to a molten state with a viscosity below 100 Pa·s, the threshold below which the resin can flow into the interstices between adjacent tows and wet out the fiber surfaces, achieving the intimate fiber-resin contact that, upon cooling and solidification, produces the interlaminar bond strength that the laminate's structural performance depends upon). The laser power is modulated in real time by a closed-loop control system that uses an infrared pyrometer (Micro-Epsilon thermoMETER CT, 8-14 μm spectral range, 1 millisecond response time, ±1°C accuracy) aimed at the nip point to measure the actual surface temperature and adjust the laser output power to maintain a setpoint of 175 ± 5°C, a temperature that is approximately 15°C above the Elium resin's nominal melting point of 160°C — the additional 15°C of superheat compensating for the heat loss that occurs as the molten resin transfers heat to the cooler substrate and tow fibers during the approximately 50 milliseconds between the nip point and the point of maximum compaction pressure, a thermal transient that would otherwise cause the resin temperature to drop below its melting point before full consolidation is achieved, resulting in incomplete interlaminar bonding and reduced laminate strength. The temperature control system includes a feed-forward component that anticipates changes in laser power demand based on the placement head's velocity — when the head decelerates approaching the end of a tow course, the residence time in the laser-heated zone increases, and the control system proactively reduces laser power to prevent overheating and thermal degradation of the Elium resin, which begins to decompose at temperatures above approximately 280°C with the evolution of methyl methacrylate monomer vapor that, if inhaled by personnel in the vicinity of the AFP machine, presents both a respiratory hazard and a flammability risk that the Mojave facility's ventilation and fire suppression systems are designed to mitigate through the continuous extraction of air from the AFP cell at a rate of 2,500 cubic meters per hour, maintaining the airborne monomer concentration below the OSHA permissible exposure limit of 100 parts per million as measured by a photoionization detector (PID) mounted in the exhaust duct and interlocked to the AFP machine's emergency stop circuit, such that any exceedance of the 100 ppm threshold automatically halts the laser heating, retracts the placement head to its home position, and activates the facility's audible and visual alarms.

The off-line programming (OLP) system that generates the toolpaths executed by the AFP machines is a critical component of the overall automation architecture, because the complexity of the Atlantis hull laminate — 48 plies in the cylindrical section, transitioning to 72 plies in the end caps, with tow paths that must follow geodesic or near-geodesic trajectories to minimize the shear deformation of the tow band that causes wrinkling, and with ply drop-offs in the transition zone between the cylinder and end cap where the laminate thickness increases to provide the additional bending stiffness required at the curvature discontinuity — exceeds the capability of manual teach-in programming by orders of magnitude, requiring instead a software system that can import the CAD model of the hull, apply the laminate stacking sequence definition, generate continuous tow paths for each ply while respecting the manufacturing constraints of minimum steering radius (2,000 millimeters for the 6.35-millimeter tows), maximum tow angle deviation from the geodesic path (±5 degrees, beyond which the tow's inherent bending stiffness causes it to lift from the mandrel surface and creates a bridging defect that the compaction roller cannot correct), and allowable gap and overlap tolerances (maximum gap of 1.0 millimeter between adjacent tow courses, maximum overlap of 2.0 millimeters, both verified by the in-process inspection system), and then post-process the resulting toolpaths into the machine-specific G-code that the Sinumerik 840D sl controller executes. The Mojave facility uses the Siemens Fibersim software suite for the composite design and toolpath generation workflow, with the following specific configuration: the CAD model of the Atlantis pod hull — created in Siemens NX and exported as a JT file with the laminate zone definitions, ply boundaries, and material orientation coordinate systems embedded as metadata — is imported into Fibersim's Composites Engineering Environment (CEE), where the laminate definition is applied as a series of plies, each with its specified material (LBFRP-001 UD tape for the 0-degree plies, LBFRP-001 fabric for the ±45-degree plies, both defined in the Fibersim material database with their full set of mechanical and processing properties), fiber orientation relative to the hull axis, ply boundary offset from the tool surface (accounting for the increasing laminate thickness as successive plies are added, which changes the effective tool surface radius and therefore the tow path geometry for each subsequent ply), and draping strategy (the algorithm that maps the flat tow band onto the doubly-curved mandrel surface, either by allowing the tow band to shear — changing its width and fiber orientation locally to conform to the surface — or by cutting the tow band into narrower segments that can be placed independently, a strategy that the Atlantis program uses for the end cap plies where the surface curvature is too severe for a single 50.8-millimeter-wide tow band to conform without wrinkling, requiring instead that the band be split into four 12.7-millimeter segments that are placed in parallel by the AFP head, which can independently control the feed rate of each segment to accommodate the different path lengths of the inner and outer segments as they traverse the curved surface). The toolpath generation algorithm in Fibersim's Automated Fiber Placement module then computes the continuous tow path for each ply, starting from a user-defined seed point and proceeding along a course that maintains the specified fiber orientation within the ±1-degree tolerance while respecting the minimum steering radius and maximum angle deviation constraints, using a proprietary path-planning algorithm that solves the constrained optimization problem of minimizing the deviation from the specified fiber angle while maintaining the tow path within the ply boundary and avoiding self-intersection of adjacent tow courses, a problem that for the Atlantis hull end caps — where the ply boundary is a complex three-dimensional curve on a doubly-curved surface — requires several hours of computation time on the Mojave facility's dedicated OLP workstation (a dual Intel Xeon Gold 6254 processor with 36 cores at 3.1 GHz, 384 GB of RAM, and an NVIDIA Quadro RTX 8000 GPU with 48 GB of VRAM, the GPU being used to accelerate the collision detection calculations that verify that the placement head does not collide with the mandrel, the tooling fixtures, or previously placed plies during the execution of the toolpath, a verification that must be performed for every point along every tow path — approximately 500,000 collision checks per ply for the 48-ply cylindrical section — and that would require days of CPU-only computation but completes in approximately 45 minutes with GPU acceleration). The post-processed G-code for a single Atlantis pod hull occupies approximately 18 gigabytes of storage, reflecting the approximately 6.2 million lines of machine code required to define the complete toolpath for all 48 to 72 plies across both AFP machines operating in coordinated fashion — one machine laying up the 0-degree and 90-degree plies while the other machine simultaneously lays up the ±45-degree plies, a parallel operation that reduces the total layup time by approximately 40 percent compared to a sequential operation in which a single machine lays up all plies, but that requires precise synchronization between the two machines to ensure that the ply boundaries align correctly at the overlap zones where the plies deposited by one machine must match the plies deposited by the other, a synchronization that is achieved through the CNC controller's distributed architecture, which connects the two Sinumerik 840D sl controllers via a deterministic PROFINET IRT (Isochronous Real-Time) network with a cycle time of 250 microseconds, ensuring that the two machines' motion commands are coordinated to within one control loop cycle — an orders-of-magnitude tighter synchronization than would be achievable through manual coordination or through a non-deterministic network protocol such as standard Ethernet TCP/IP, and a capability that is essential for the production of a laminate whose structural integrity depends on the precise alignment of plies across the full surface of the hull.

The mandrel tooling on which the AFP machines deposit the composite material is itself a precision-engineered component whose design significantly influences the quality of the finished laminate, because any dimensional inaccuracy, thermal distortion, or surface defect in the mandrel is directly transferred to the inner surface of the cured hull — the surface that forms the pressure boundary between the habitable interior and the external seawater environment, and that must therefore be free of any irregularities that could act as stress concentrators or crack initiation sites during the pressure vessel's fifty-year service life. The Atlantis hull mandrel is a steel weldment fabricated from A36 structural steel plates with a thickness of 25 millimeters, rolled and welded into the 3:1 prolate spheroid geometry described in Section 5 of the parent document — a shape that presents significant tooling challenges because it is not a surface of revolution (except in the cylindrical mid-section, which accounts for approximately 60 percent of the hull's length), meaning that the mandrel cannot be fabricated by simple rolling of flat plates into cylinders but instead requires the plates to be formed into compound curves using a combination of roll bending for the sections with single curvature (the cylindrical mid-body) and press brake forming with custom dies for the doubly-curved sections of the end caps, followed by welding of the formed plates along their edges and grinding of the weld beads flush with the parent metal to produce a continuous, smooth surface with a maximum allowable step or depression at any weld seam of 0.1 millimeters, verified by a coordinate measuring machine (CMM) survey of the completed mandrel at 500-millimeter intervals along its length and at 15-degree intervals around its circumference, generating approximately 2,400 measurement points whose deviations from the nominal CAD geometry are compiled into a surface profile error map that the AFP OLP system uses to adjust the tow path Z-coordinates, compensating for the measured mandrel surface profile errors by adding or subtracting an offset at each point along the tow path equal to the local mandrel surface deviation, such that the inner surface of the deposited laminate conforms to the nominal CAD geometry with a tolerance of ±0.5 millimeters regardless of the underlying mandrel imperfections — a compensation strategy that eliminates the need for the mandrel itself to be machined to sub-millimeter accuracy, which would be prohibitively expensive for a tool of this size, and instead relies on the metrology-to-OLP feedback loop to achieve the required laminate inner surface accuracy, a strategy that the Episode Twenty-One hulls validated by demonstrating that the finished hull inner surfaces deviated from the nominal geometry by less than ±0.3 millimeters at all measurement points, well within the ±0.5-millimeter tolerance required for the installation of the interior life support equipment modules, which are designed with standardized mounting interfaces that assume a known and consistent inner hull geometry and that would require costly custom shimming or rework if the hull's as-built geometry varied unpredictably from the design intent.

The mandrel incorporates an integral heating system that maintains the tool surface at 80 ± 5°C during the AFP layup process, a temperature that serves two essential functions: first, it reduces the cooling rate of the Elium resin after it passes through the laser-heated nip point, extending the time during which the resin remains above its glass transition temperature and can therefore continue to flow and wet out the fiber surfaces, improving the interlaminar bond quality by allowing more complete resin diffusion into the fiber interstices before the resin viscosity increases beyond the point of effective flow, which for the Elium resin occurs at approximately 120°C (the glass transition temperature of the cured resin, below which the polymer chains are effectively immobilized and further consolidation is impossible regardless of the applied compaction pressure); second, it reduces the thermal gradient through the laminate thickness during the layup of successive plies, because each newly deposited hot ply transfers heat not only to the previously deposited ply but also to the mandrel surface if the mandrel is cold, creating a steep thermal gradient that generates residual thermal stresses in the laminate as the plies cool at different rates — the ply closest to the cold mandrel cooling fastest and therefore contracting first, while the plies further from the mandrel remain hot and thermally expanded, a differential contraction that, in extreme cases, can cause delamination between the plies even before the laminate is removed from the mandrel, particularly in thick laminates such as the Atlantis hull with its 48 to 72 plies and a total cured thickness of 25 to 35 millimeters depending on the location, where the cumulative thermal strain across the laminate thickness can exceed the interlaminar tensile strength of the Elium matrix if the mandrel is not heated to maintain a uniform temperature through the laminate, a failure mode that the Mojave team observed during early development trials on an unheated mandrel and that motivated the retrofit of the mandrel heating system, which consists of 48 flexible silicone rubber heating pads (Omega Engineering SRFG series, 500 watts per pad, 120 VAC) bonded to the interior surface of the mandrel and arranged in six independently controlled zones — the forward end cap, the forward transition, the cylindrical section (subdivided into two zones, forward and aft, because the cylindrical section's 9-meter length exceeds the practical limit for a single heating zone), the aft transition, and the aft end cap — each zone controlled by a dedicated PID temperature controller receiving feedback from three type-K thermocouples embedded in the mandrel wall, with the setpoint for each zone individually adjustable to compensate for the different heat loss rates in different sections of the mandrel — the end caps losing heat more rapidly than the cylindrical section because of their higher surface-area-to-volume ratio — and to maintain the specified 80 ± 5°C surface temperature at all points on the mandrel regardless of the ambient air temperature in the AFP cell, which varies seasonally from approximately 15°C in winter (the Mojave Desert's nighttime temperatures can drop below freezing, but the AFP cell's HVAC system maintains a minimum of 15°C during production operations) to approximately 35°C in summer (the HVAC system's maximum cooling capacity limits the cell temperature to approximately 35°C during the hottest summer days, when the external temperature can exceed 45°C), a seasonal variation that would cause unacceptable variation in laminate quality if the mandrel heating system did not actively compensate for the changing ambient temperature by adjusting its heat output accordingly, a compensation that the PID controllers perform automatically based on the difference between the measured mandrel surface temperature and the setpoint, with no operator intervention required beyond the initial seasonal adjustment of the setpoint to account for the different optimal mandrel temperatures at different ambient conditions — a lower mandrel temperature of 75°C in summer, when the reduced temperature differential between the mandrel and the ambient air reduces the cooling rate of the deposited plies and allows the same interlaminar bond quality to be achieved with less mandrel heating, and a higher mandrel temperature of 85°C in winter, when the increased temperature differential requires more aggressive mandrel heating to maintain the same cooling rate.

The AFP cell environmental control system maintains the temperature, humidity, and particulate cleanliness of the air surrounding the AFP machines during operation, because any contamination of the laminate by airborne particles — dust, fiber fragments, or oil mist from the machine's mechanical components — creates inclusions in the laminate that act as stress concentrators and potential crack initiation sites, degrading the laminate's long-term fatigue resistance and increasing the probability of a pressure hull failure during the pod's fifty-year service life, a risk that is unacceptable for a habitation module that must protect human occupants at a depth where a hull breach would result in near-instantaneous fatal flooding. The AFP cell is a positive-pressure cleanroom maintained at Class 100,000 (ISO Class 8) per ISO 14644-1, with HEPA-filtered air supplied at a rate of 15 air changes per hour through ceiling-mounted diffusers and returned through low-level wall grilles, creating a downward airflow pattern that sweeps any airborne particles toward the floor and away from the laminate surface, where they would otherwise settle and become trapped between successive plies during the layup process. The cell's temperature is maintained at 23 ± 2°C by a chilled-water air handling unit with a cooling capacity of 150 kW, and the relative humidity is maintained at 35 ± 5 percent by a steam humidifier and a desiccant dehumidifier operating in tandem under the control of a central building management system that also monitors the cell's positive pressure differential relative to the adjacent factory spaces — maintained at a minimum of 15 pascals — to prevent the infiltration of unfiltered air through door seals and other leakage paths, a pressure differential that is especially important in the Mojave Desert environment, where the external air frequently contains high concentrations of wind-blown dust that would rapidly contaminate the laminate if allowed to enter the AFP cell, potentially causing rejection of an entire hull section and inflicting a production delay of weeks while a replacement section is fabricated, a risk that the facility's design mitigates through the combination of the positive-pressure cleanroom, the airlock entry vestibule with interlocked doors that prevent both doors from being open simultaneously, and the requirement that all personnel entering the AFP cell wear cleanroom garments (Tyvek coveralls, hairnets, beard covers, and shoe covers) and pass through an air shower that removes loose particles from their garments before they enter the cell proper, a protocol that has been validated through continuous particle counting at multiple locations within the cell, with the particle counters confirming that the ISO Class 8 cleanliness standard is maintained during all production operations and that excursions above the standard are limited to brief periods following maintenance activities, which are scheduled to occur during planned production downtime to allow the cell's filtration system to recover to the specified cleanliness level before AFP operations resume.

The tow cutting and restart system — the subsystem that severs each tow at the end of a tow course and then re-initiates tow placement at the beginning of the next course — is a critical reliability component because any failure of this subsystem results in either a tow that is not cut cleanly and that drags across the laminate surface as the head moves to the next course start point, depositing resin and fiber fragments in locations where they do not belong and creating defects that must be removed by manual rework before the next ply can be deposited, or a tow that fails to restart properly, creating a gap in the laminate at the start of the new course that must be filled by a manual patch applied after the AFP machine has completed its program and been moved away from the mandrel, a rework operation that typically requires one to two hours per defect and that, if it occurs more than approximately once per hull section, would accumulate enough rework hours to jeopardize the production schedule. The cutting system employs a pneumatically actuated shear blade fabricated from hardened D2 tool steel (HRC 60-62) that severs the tow against a tungsten carbide anvil, a cutting mechanism that has been selected over the alternative methods of ultrasonic cutting (which generates excessive heat that can degrade the Elium resin in the vicinity of the cut, creating a resin-degraded zone that would be incorporated into the laminate at the tow end) and laser cutting (which, while capable of producing a cleaner cut, introduces the risk of igniting the Elium resin, which is flammable in its uncured state and which, if ignited at the cutting point, could propagate along the tow back to the creel cabinet, a fire scenario that the facility's risk assessment identified as a credible hazard and that the mechanical shear cutting system eliminates entirely by operating without any heat source at the cutting point), and is designed for a minimum of 500,000 cutting cycles between blade replacements — corresponding to approximately 6 months of continuous production at the Atlantis program's tow course count of approximately 2,800 courses per hull, equating to 28,000 cutting cycles per hull and 280,000 cutting cycles for the ten-hull demonstration program — with blade wear monitored by an automated vision system that images the blade edge at 10x magnification after every 10,000 cutting cycles and compares the edge profile to a reference image, flagging the blade for replacement when the edge radius exceeds 50 micrometers, which corresponds to the point at which the shear force required to cut a tow increases by more than 20 percent above the new-blade baseline and the probability of an incomplete cut — a tow that is partially severed but remains connected by a few intact filaments, which are then pulled from the laminate as the head moves away, creating a fiber-depleted zone and a stress concentration — exceeds 0.1 percent, a threshold that the Mojave quality team has established based on the defect criticality analysis documented in PART-088. The restart system feeds the leading end of each tow from the cutting module to the nip point via a pneumatically driven gripper that grips the tow end, accelerates it to the head's travel speed, and releases it precisely as the tow end enters the nip point and is captured between the compaction roller and the substrate, a sequence that is synchronized with the head motion by the CNC controller and that must execute within 50 milliseconds of the head reaching the programmed course start position — any longer delay and the head would have moved beyond the acceptable start-position tolerance of ±2 millimeters before the tow is captured, creating a misalignment between the tow course and the adjacent courses that the in-process inspection system would detect and flag as a defect requiring rework. The restart system's reliability is ensured by a preventive maintenance protocol that replaces the gripper mechanism's pneumatic seals every 50,000 cycles and the gripper jaws every 100,000 cycles, intervals that correspond to approximately 2 hulls and 4 hulls respectively for the Atlantis program, a maintenance burden that the production schedule accommodates through the planned maintenance downtime that is already factored into the 85 percent OEE target and that occurs during the tooling changeover between hull sections, when the AFP machines are idle for approximately 8 hours while the completed hull section is removed from the mandrel and the mandrel is prepared for the next layup cycle, a window during which the maintenance team performs all scheduled preventive maintenance activities simultaneously to minimize the incremental downtime attributable to maintenance beyond the tooling changeover itself.

The final element of the AFP machine specification that warrants detailed description is the quality data acquisition system that records every relevant process parameter during the layup of each hull, creating a comprehensive digital record — the "digital twin" of the fabrication process, in the terminology of the Industry 4.0 framework that the Mojave facility has implemented across all its composite manufacturing operations — that serves multiple purposes: real-time process monitoring and control, enabling the CNC controller to detect process excursions (such as a tow tension that exceeds the allowable range, a nip point temperature that deviates from the setpoint, or a compaction pressure that falls below the minimum threshold) and to alert the operator or automatically pause the layup before the excursion produces a defect in the laminate; post-production quality verification, enabling the quality assurance team to correlate any defects found during the ultrasonic inspection (PART-087) with the process parameters that were in effect at the time and location of the defect, providing the root-cause data needed to implement corrective actions that prevent recurrence of the same defect in subsequent hulls; traceability documentation for the certification process (PART-120), providing the independent third-party certifying authority (ABS, DNV, or Lloyds, as determined by the certification pathway selection process) with the evidence that each hull was fabricated in accordance with the approved process specification and that any process deviations were detected, documented, and dispositioned according to the approved quality management system; and long-term process improvement, enabling the manufacturing engineering team to analyze the accumulated process data across multiple hulls using statistical methods — control charts, process capability indices (Cpk), and machine learning algorithms trained to identify subtle correlations between process parameters and laminate quality that might not be apparent from examination of individual hull records — to continuously refine the AFP process parameters and improve the consistency and quality of the laminate, a continuous improvement feedback loop that is essential for the transition from the ten-hull demonstration program to the 1,000-hull-per-year production rate described in PART-095, where the economic viability of the production operation depends on achieving laminate quality levels that minimize the scrap and rework rate and maximize the throughput of the AFP cell. The data acquisition system records the following parameters at a sampling rate of 100 Hz (10 milliseconds per sample): the six-axis position of the placement head (from the linear encoder and rotary encoder feedback), the tow tension for each of the 8 active tows (from the load cell in each tow tension control module), the nip point temperature (from the infrared pyrometer), the laser output power (from the laser controller's internal power monitor), the compaction pressure (from the pressure transducer in the pneumatic actuator supply line), the mandrel surface temperature at each of the 18 thermocouple locations (from the mandrel heating system PID controllers), the ambient temperature and humidity in the AFP cell (from the cleanroom environmental monitoring system), and the status of all digital inputs and outputs on the CNC controller — emergency stop status, door interlock status, spool-low alarm status, and the status of the tow cutting and restart mechanisms. At the 100 Hz sampling rate, a single hull's fabrication generates approximately 180 gigabytes of process data, which is stored on a redundant RAID 6 storage array in the Mojave facility's server room and backed up nightly to an off-site cloud storage service (compliant with the Aegis C data architecture standards described in PART-118) to ensure that the fabrication records survive any single-site disaster — a precaution that is especially relevant for a facility located in the Mojave Desert, where the earthquake risk from the San Andreas and Garlock fault systems, while lower than at the coastal deployment sites, is not zero, and where the loss of fabrication records for hulls already deployed to the seabed would compromise the ability to assess the continued seaworthiness of those hulls in the event that a manufacturing defect is discovered years after deployment, a scenario that the Aegis C program's safety philosophy requires to be addressed through the preservation of complete fabrication records for the entire service life of each pod plus ten years, or a total of sixty years from the date of fabrication.

The AFP machine specification described in this document represents the culmination of a two-decade evolution in automated composite manufacturing technology, from the early gantry-style AFP machines developed for the aerospace industry's carbon-fiber fuselage programs of the 2000s — the Boeing 787 and Airbus A350 programs, which pioneered the use of AFP for large-scale composite structures and which established many of the machine architecture principles that the Atlantis AFP cell inherits, including the gantry configuration, the six-axis placement head kinematics, the closed-loop tension control, and the laser heating system — through the refinement of those technologies for the specific demands of thick-section, high-pressure, long-service-life composite pressure vessels, which impose requirements — interlaminar bond strength under decades of hydrostatic compression, resistance to moisture-induced degradation of the fiber-matrix interface, and tolerance of the thermal stresses generated by the through-thickness temperature gradients during thick-section lamination — that are more severe than the corresponding requirements for aircraft fuselage structures, which are designed for a service life of approximately 30 years in a relatively benign atmospheric environment rather than 50 years in continuous saltwater immersion at elevated hydrostatic pressure. The Atlantis AFP machines embody the lessons learned from the aerospace AFP experience, adapted and extended for the unique demands of the subsea habitation application, and their specification reflects the conviction — shared by the Kiddo, Torvalds, Citadel, Dolittle, and ZIRCONIA registers of the Episode Twenty-One verification protocol — that the quality of the fabrication process is the quality of the product, that automation is the necessary condition for achieving the repeatability and consistency that a safety-critical application demands, and that the capital investment in AFP technology is not an optional expense to be minimized but a foundational requirement of the Atlantis program's mission: to produce habitation modules whose structural integrity can be trusted with human lives at the bottom of the sea, beneath the path of a megatsunami, for half a century of continuous operation.
