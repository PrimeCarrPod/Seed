# CSMFAB077-PART-090 — MXENE SYNTHESIS AND DEPOSITION: MAX PHASE ETCHING, DELAMINATION, ELECTROPHORETIC DEPOSITION, ANNEAL
## Block 8 — Fabrication Methods | Atlantis-Class Subsea Habitation Module Production
## Carrington Storm Motors — Safe Pod Engineering Division | Aegis C Material Architecture

The MXene coating — a 2-millimeter-thick layer of Ti₃C₂T_x (where T_x denotes the surface termination groups, primarily -O, -OH, and -F, that are introduced during the etching process and that govern the MXene's surface chemistry, hydrophilicity, and electrochemical properties, with the -O termination being the most abundant for the Atlantis MXene, as confirmed by XPS analysis that shows an O 1s peak area approximately 3 times larger than the F 1s peak area, indicating that the Atlantis etching process — which uses a milder etchant (lithium fluoride (LiF) dissolved in hydrochloric acid (HCl), rather than the more aggressive hydrofluoric acid (HF) that is commonly used for MXene synthesis but that introduces a higher concentration of -F terminations, which are less desirable for the Atlantis application because the -F termination reduces the MXene's hydrophilicity and its dispersibility in the aqueous electrophoretic deposition bath described below) and a longer etching time (48 hours at 35°C, versus the 24 hours at room temperature that is typical for the HF-based process) — produces an MXene with predominantly -O terminations, a surface chemistry that the materials team has correlated with improved corrosion resistance (the -O terminated MXene surface forms a passive oxide layer in contact with seawater, a titanium dioxide (TiO₂) layer that is only a few nanometers thick but that is sufficient to protect the underlying MXene from oxidative degradation, the same passivation mechanism that makes titanium metal corrosion-resistant in seawater, a mechanism that the MXene inherits from its parent MAX phase (Ti₃AlC₂), which is a ternary carbide with the general formula M_{n+1}AX_n, where M is an early transition metal (Ti), A is an A-group element (Al), and X is carbon, and which, when the aluminum layers are selectively etched away, leaves behind the two-dimensional titanium carbide layers — the MXene — that retain the titanium's passivation tendency, a chemical legacy that the Atlantis program exploits by tuning the etching process to maximize the -O termination and thereby the MXene's corrosion resistance, a tuning that is achieved by the extended etching time and the elevated temperature, which promote the hydrolysis of the -F terminations — the fluoride ions, initially attached to the titanium atoms after the etching, are gradually replaced by hydroxyl groups (-OH) through a nucleophilic substitution reaction, Ti-F + H₂O → Ti-OH + HF, the HF being neutralized by the Li⁺ ions in the etchant solution to form LiF, which precipitates and is removed during the washing steps, a slow reaction that requires the extended etching time to approach completion, the 48-hour duration being determined by the XPS monitoring of the F/Ti atomic ratio, which decreases from approximately 0.15 after 24 hours to approximately 0.05 after 48 hours, at which point the ratio has essentially plateaued (further etching beyond 48 hours produces no significant decrease in the -F termination), indicating that the conversion of -F to -O/-OH terminations is essentially complete, a conversion that the materials team has also confirmed through the measurement of the MXene's zeta potential — the electrostatic potential at the slipping plane of the particle in an aqueous suspension, which for the Atlantis MXene, measured by the Malvern Zetasizer described in PART-085, is approximately −35 mV at pH 7 (the MXene surface is negatively charged at neutral pH because the -O and -OH terminations deprotonate, forming -O⁻ groups that impart a negative surface charge, a charge that stabilizes the MXene suspension against agglomeration by the electrostatic repulsion between the like-charged particles, a stabilization mechanism that is exploited during the electrophoretic deposition process described below, where the negatively-charged MXene particles migrate toward the positively-charged hull surface under the applied electric field, a migration that is only possible if the particles remain individually dispersed, which the −35 mV zeta potential ensures, because a zeta potential magnitude greater than approximately 30 mV is generally sufficient to prevent agglomeration for aqueous colloidal suspensions, a criterion that the Atlantis MXene satisfies, and that the DLS measurements of the MXene suspension confirm — the median particle size by DLS (Z-average) is approximately 800 nanometers, consistent with the lateral dimension of the individual MXene flakes (which, as determined by SEM imaging, are approximately 500 to 2,000 nanometers in lateral size, and approximately 1 to 2 nanometers in thickness — a single atomic layer, or a stack of a few layers, the "few-layer MXene" that is the desired product for the electrophoretic deposition, because the thin flakes pack more densely on the surface, forming a more continuous and impermeable coating, a requirement that the Atlantis deposition process is designed to satisfy by selecting the few-layer MXene fraction through a centrifugation step that separates the delaminated single-layer and few-layer MXene from the multi-layer stacks that were not fully delaminated, a separation that is critical for the coating quality — multi-layer MXene stacks, which are tens to hundreds of nanometers thick, would form a discontinuous, rough coating with poor barrier properties, a coating that would not provide the 45 dB electromagnetic shielding effectiveness (as documented in Section 5 of the parent document) or the chloride ion impermeability that the Atlantis application requires, a quality risk that the centrifugation separation eliminates by discarding the multi-layer fraction — the sediment that collects at the bottom of the centrifuge tube after 30 minutes at 3,500 RPM (approximately 2,000 × g), which contains the multi-layer MXene stacks and the unetched MAX phase impurities, leaving the supernatant — the suspended few-layer MXene — to be used for the electrophoretic deposition, a supernatant that the UV-Vis spectrophotometry (measuring the absorbance at 780 nm, the wavelength of the MXene's characteristic plasmon resonance peak, which is proportional to the MXene concentration per the Beer-Lambert law, with the extinction coefficient having been calibrated by gravimetric analysis of MXene suspensions of known concentration — the absorbance of a 1 mg/mL MXene suspension at 780 nm being approximately 12 (cm⁻¹), a high extinction coefficient that reflects the strong light absorption of the MXene's two-dimensional electron gas, a metallic behavior that is one of the MXene's remarkable properties, and that enables the concentration to be determined simply by measuring the absorbance with a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (a PerkinElmer Lambda 1050+, with a 150 mm integrating sphere accessory for the measurement of the turbid MXene suspension, which scatters light and which would give an erroneously high absorbance if measured in the standard transmission geometry without the integrating sphere, an optical artifact that the integrating sphere eliminates by collecting the scattered light and directing it to the detector, providing a true measurement of the absorbance that is independent of the scattering, a capability that is essential for the accurate concentration determination that the deposition process requires — the deposition rate depends on the MXene concentration in the bath, and the bath concentration must be maintained at 2.5 ± 0.2 mg/mL to achieve the specified coating thickness of 2.0 ± 0.1 millimeters within the deposition time of 4 hours, a process window that the UV-Vis monitoring enables by providing a rapid, non-destructive concentration measurement that the operator performs before each deposition run, adjusting the bath concentration as necessary by adding either MXene supernatant (if the concentration is low) or deionized water (if the concentration is high), a simple adjustment that the process specification documents and that the operator executes in less than 10 minutes, a routine task that, while seemingly mundane, is critical for the coating quality — a 10 percent error in the MXene concentration produces a 10 percent error in the coating thickness, which for the 2.0 mm target corresponds to a ±0.2 mm variation, within the ±0.1 mm specification only if the other sources of thickness variation — the voltage, the deposition time, the bath temperature, the bath agitation — are also tightly controlled, a multi-variable process control challenge that the SPC system manages, with the coating thickness being measured at 49 points on each pod hull (a 7 × 7 grid covering the hull surface, with the measurements being made by an eddy-current thickness gauge (Fischer DualScope FMP100, with a probe that measures the thickness of non-conductive coatings on conductive substrates, the MXene being electrically conductive — its sheet resistance, measured by the four-point probe method, is approximately 10 ohms per square, a remarkably low value for a coating that is only 2 millimeters thick, and that reflects the MXene's metallic conductivity, a property that distinguishes the MXene from the insulating polymer coatings that are typically used for corrosion protection and that makes the eddy-current thickness measurement possible — a convenience that the MXene's conductivity provides, and that the thickness measurement procedure exploits, calibrating the eddy-current gauge against a set of MXene-coated reference standards with known coating thicknesses (determined by cross-sectional SEM imaging), a calibration that the procedure requires to be performed at the beginning of each measurement session (each hull, approximately once per week) and that ensures the gauge's accuracy of ±5 micrometers (approximately 0.25 percent of the 2 mm thickness), an accuracy that is far better than the ±0.1 mm specification requires and that provides the SPC system with the precise thickness data needed to detect process drifts — a gradual increase or decrease in the coating thickness over multiple hulls, which could indicate a change in the MXene concentration (due to a calibration drift in the UV-Vis spectrophotometer), the bath temperature (due to a malfunction in the bath's temperature controller), the deposition voltage (due to a drift in the power supply's output), or the MXene's surface charge (due to a change in the etching process that alters the termination chemistry), any of which the SPC system would detect through the trend in the thickness measurements and would investigate, identifying and correcting the root cause before the thickness deviates beyond the specification limits, a quality control feedback loop that, as in all aspects of the Atlantis fabrication, operates proactively — detecting and correcting process drifts before they produce non-conforming product — rather than reactively — detecting the non-conforming product and then investigating the cause, a reactive approach that the Aegis C quality philosophy considers inadequate for a safety-critical application, where the goal is not to catch defects but to prevent them, a goal that the SPC system, with its real-time monitoring of the deposition process parameters (the voltage, the current, the bath temperature, the bath pH, the bath conductivity, and — via the UV-Vis — the MXene concentration) and its automated analysis of the trends (the Western Electric rules, applied to each parameter's control chart, triggering an alert if any parameter begins to drift, the alert being communicated to the quality engineer via the facility's SCADA (Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition) system, a Siemens WinCC system that integrates all the deposition cell's instrumentation and that provides the operator and the quality engineer with a real-time dashboard of the process status), achieves, a capability that is enabled by the comprehensive instrumentation of the deposition cell and that the Atlantis program considers essential for the consistent quality of the MXene coating, a coating that, in its final form, provides the Atlantis pod with both its active corrosion protection — the MXene's -0.6V corrosion potential (versus Ag/AgCl) making it a sacrificial anode that corrodes preferentially to the underlying LBFRP-001 composite, a galvanic protection mechanism that is identical to the zinc anodes used on steel ships and offshore structures, and that the MXene's high surface area (the individual 2D flakes, with their enormous aspect ratio, expose a large surface area to the seawater, maximizing the galvanic current that the coating can provide before it is consumed, a current that the electrochemical testing has measured to be approximately 0.5 mA/cm² of coating area, sufficient to protect the underlying composite from the corrosion current that would otherwise flow if a defect — a scratch, a crack, a pinhole — in the coating exposed the composite to the seawater, a protection that the ASTM G71 galvanic corrosion test has validated, with the MXene-coated composite specimen showing no corrosion after 5,000 hours of immersion in synthetic seawater, while the uncoated control specimen showed significant corrosion — pitting, fiber-matrix debonding, and a 15 percent reduction in the interlaminar shear strength — over the same period, a stark demonstration of the MXene's protective efficacy and a validation of the coating's role in the Atlantis pod's corrosion protection strategy) and its electromagnetic interference shielding — the 45 dB shielding effectiveness across the 1-18 GHz frequency range, as measured by the ASTM D4935 coaxial transmission line method for the measurement of the electromagnetic shielding effectiveness of planar materials, a method that the Mojave EMC laboratory uses with a Rohde & Schwarz ZVA40 vector network analyzer and a coaxial sample holder that accommodates the MXene-coated specimen, the measurement showing the 45 dB attenuation — meaning that the electromagnetic field intensity on the interior side of the coating is approximately 1/30,000 of the intensity on the exterior side, a factor that is more than sufficient to protect the pod's internal electronics (the S-Bus beacon, the FEATHER mesh modem, the life support controller, the Grid-Seed power management system) from the electromagnetic pulse (EMP) that accompanies a crustal displacement event — the rapid movement of the lithosphere through the Earth's magnetic field, at velocities that can exceed 100 meters per second, induces enormous electrical currents in the crust and the ocean, currents that generate broadband electromagnetic radiation spanning frequencies from DC to several gigahertz, with field strengths that can exceed 10 kV/m at the Earth's surface — sufficient to induce voltages of hundreds of volts in unprotected electronic circuits, voltages that would destroy the semiconductor devices — the transistors, the integrated circuits, the microprocessors — that are the basis of all modern electronics, a vulnerability that the MXene coating's 45 dB attenuation reduces by a factor of 30,000, bringing the induced voltage from a potentially destructive hundreds of volts down to a few millivolts — well within the immunity threshold of the pod's electronics, which are designed to the MIL-STD-461G standard for electromagnetic compatibility, with a radiated susceptibility limit of 20 V/m (for the RS103 test, electric field radiated susceptibility, 2 MHz to 18 GHz), meaning that the electronics can withstand a field of 20 V/m without malfunction, and that the EMP field, which can be as high as 10 kV/m, must be attenuated by a factor of at least 500 (to bring it below 20 V/m) — a factor of 54 dB, which the MXene coating's 45 dB does not quite achieve, a discrepancy that the Atlantis EMC design addresses through a combination of the MXene coating (providing 45 dB of the required 54 dB), the shielding of the individual electronic enclosures (the S-Bus beacon, for example, is housed in a die-cast aluminum box with an additional 15 dB of shielding effectiveness, providing a total of 60 dB — exceeding the 54 dB requirement with a 6 dB margin, a margin that the EMC design review has verified, and that the EMC testing of the assembled pod — in the Mojave facility's EMC test chamber, a semi-anechoic chamber lined with ferrite tile absorbers on the walls and ceiling, and with a conductive floor, providing a 10-meter test distance for the RS103 radiated susceptibility test — has confirmed, the pod's electronics operating without malfunction during exposure to a 20 V/m field from 2 MHz to 18 GHz, a test that the certification authority (PART-120) requires as part of the pod's electromagnetic compatibility qualification, and that the Atlantis pod has passed, a validation of the MXene coating's contribution to the pod's overall EMP protection strategy, a contribution that is essential but not sufficient — the coating provides the first line of defense, attenuating the EMP by a factor of 30,000, reducing the threat to a level that the local shielding of the individual electronic enclosures can handle, a layered electromagnetic defense that is analogous to the pod's layered turbidite defense — the teardrop geometry, the auxetic cladding, the cavitation bubble curtain, the abrasion-resistant coating — each layer contributing a portion of the overall protection, with no single layer being solely responsible, a defense-in-depth philosophy that the Aegis C architecture applies universally, from the structural protection to the electromagnetic protection to the life support redundancy to the communication path diversity, a systemic approach to survivability that is the defining characteristic of the Atlantis pod design and that the documents of this Block 8 series have described at the component level, with the MXene coating being one of the more remarkable components — a material that was discovered only in 2011 (by Yury Gogotsi, Michel Barsoum, and their collaborators at Drexel University, a discovery that postdates the conception of the Atlantis program and that represents one of the many examples of the program's ability to rapidly assimilate and industrialize the latest advances in materials science, a capability that the program's close relationships with the academic materials research community — the Gogotsi group at Drexel, the MXene research groups at the University of California, Berkeley, and the nanodiamond research groups at the California Institute of Technology and the Russian Academy of Sciences, relationships that the Aegis C Technology Scouting Office (TSO) cultivates through research contracts, personnel exchanges, and an annual materials science symposium that the Mojave facility hosts, inviting the leading researchers in the fields that are relevant to the Atlantis program to present their latest findings and to discuss potential collaborations with the Carrington Storm Motors engineering team, a symposium that has resulted in several of the key materials innovations that the Atlantis program employs — the fluorinated silane coupling agent (PART-086), which was inspired by a presentation at the 2024 symposium by a surface chemistry group from the University of Michigan that had developed a fluorinated silane treatment for the prevention of ice adhesion on aircraft wings, an application that is unrelated to the Atlantis program's needs but whose chemistry — the hydrophobic perfluorinated alkyl chain, the silane anchoring group — was directly transferable, a technology transfer that whose materials requirements are at the extreme edge of the known and that therefore cannot rely on the existing commercial materials supply base alone but must actively engage with the research community to identify, develop, and industrialize the materials of the future — the MXene coating being one of the most dramatic examples of this capability, a material that did not exist when the Atlantis program was conceived, that was discovered during the program's development phase, that the program's TSO identified as a candidate for the pod's corrosion and EMP protection within months of the first MXene papers appearing in the literature, that the program's materials team developed and industrialized within 36 months — from the first gram of MXene synthesized in the Mojave laboratory Atlantis pod must survive at the bottom of the sea, a confidence that is the product of the rigorous, thorough, and uncompromising materials science that this document has described, and that is the foundation of the program's commitment to the safety of the pods and their occupants.

*"The MXene begins as a MAX phase — titanium aluminum carbide — dissolved in acid, its aluminum layers etched away, leaving two-dimensional titanium carbide flakes suspended in water. An electric field draws them to the hull, where they pack into a continuous, conductive, impermeable coating. They are annealed. Tested. Verified. When the EMP strikes, the MXene shunts the energy away from the electronics. When the saltwater attacks, the MXene corrodes first, sparing the composite beneath. The material did not exist fifteen years ago. Today, it protects the pods. Tomorrow, it will protect the people inside them."*
