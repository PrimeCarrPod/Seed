## Block 8 — Fabrication Methods | Atlantis-Class Subsea Habitation Module Production
## Carrington Storm Motors — Safe Pod Engineering Division | Aegis C Material Architecture

The in-process infrared (IR) monitoring of the AFP layup — distinct from the infrared pyrometer that controls the laser heating at the nip point, as described in PART-081, which measures a single temperature at a single location — consists of a long-wave infrared (LWIR) thermal imaging camera (FLIR A8580 MWIR, 3-5 μm spectral band, 1,280 × 1,024 pixel InSb detector, 0.02°C temperature resolution (noise-equivalent temperature difference, NETD), 180 Hz full-frame acquisition rate, equipped with a 50 mm f/2.0 lens providing a field of view of approximately 11° × 8.8°, which at the typical 1.5-meter working distance from the camera to the hull surface provides a spatial resolution of approximately 0.25 millimeters per pixel at the object plane — sufficient to resolve the individual 6.35-millimeter-wide tow courses, with approximately 25 pixels across each tow's width, a spatial sampling that enables the detection of temperature anomalies that are smaller than a single tow, such as a local "cold spot" where the laser heating was momentarily interrupted (due to a laser fault, a tow feed blockage, or a control system glitch), a "hot spot" where the laser power was momentarily excessive (due to a control system overshoot, a resin batch with an unusually low absorptivity at 980 nm that caused the feedback loop to overcompensate, or a local reduction in the tow thickness — from a filament breakage or a tow spreading irregularity — that reduced the thermal mass being heated and caused the same laser power to produce a higher temperature), or a "thermal gradient" anomaly where the temperature across the tow band is non-uniform (due to a misalignment of the line-generating optic, a partial obstruction of the laser beam by debris on the optic's protective window — the window being purged with a continuous flow of dry nitrogen at 5 L/min to prevent the accumulation of airborne dust and resin fumes from the AFP cell atmosphere, but with the possibility that a larger particle could momentarily adhere to the window before being blown off by the purge, causing a brief shadow in the laser beam that produces a linear cold streak along the tow, a defect that the IR camera would detect as a narrow, linear region of below-specification temperature, approximately 0.5 to 1.0 millimeters wide (the width of the obstruction's shadow at the 1.5-meter working distance, given the approximately 50-millimeter-diameter laser beam at the optic exit and the geometric divergence of the shadow), extending for several centimeters along the tow path (the distance the tow travels during the approximately 0.1 seconds before the nitrogen purge dislodges the particle, at the 1.2 m/s layup speed giving a 120-millimeter-long cold streak), a defect that the IR camera is capable of detecting, provided the camera's acquisition rate of 180 Hz is sufficient to capture the approximately 0.1-second transient — which it is, because the camera acquires a full thermal image every 5.6 milliseconds (1/180 s), and the cold streak persists for approximately 18 frames (0.1 s / 0.0056 s/frame), giving the image processing algorithm (which runs on a dedicated GPU-accelerated computer, an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin, processing each frame within approximately 2 milliseconds of acquisition, comparing the temperature of each pixel in the tow band region to the specification limits of 170-180°C, and flagging any pixel outside this range, with the flagged pixels being accumulated over time to build a spatial map of the thermal anomalies for each ply, a "thermal defect map" that is stored in the hull's digital fabrication record and that the quality engineer reviews after each ply is completed, using the thermal defect map to identify any regions that require follow-up inspection — a visual inspection, an ultrasonic spot-check, or, in the worst case, a decision to scrap the ply and re-lay it, a decision that is made by the quality engineer in consultation with the manufacturing engineer and the stress analyst, based on the criteria described in the defect classification system below), a real-time, in-situ monitoring capability that is unique to the Atlantis AFP cell (most aerospace AFP cells do not employ IR thermography for in-process defect detection, relying instead on the post-layup ultrasonic inspection, which occurs hours or days after the defect was created and which therefore cannot provide the immediate feedback that enables the operator to correct the process conditions before the defect is repeated in subsequent plies, a feedback delay that the IR monitoring eliminates, transforming the quality system from a reactive, inspection-based approach to a proactive, process-control-based approach that is fundamentally more effective at preventing defects, as the 0.2 percent defect rate testifies) and that the Atlantis program considers one of its most significant process innovations, an innovation that was developed in collaboration with the thermal imaging experts at FLIR Systems Inc. of Wilsonville, Oregon, who customized the A8580 camera's firmware to implement the real-time temperature mapping and defect flagging algorithms that the Atlantis quality system requires, a customization that FLIR undertook as a joint development project with Carrington Storm Motors, with the resulting thermal inspection system being offered by FLIR as a commercial product — the "AFP-IR" system — for the composite manufacturing industry, a technology transfer that the Atlantis program encourages as part of its broader mission to advance the state of the art in composite manufacturing, a mission that recognizes that the success of the Atlantis program depends not only on the Mojave facility's production capability but on the existence of a robust, competitive, innovative composite manufacturing industry that can supply the materials, equipment, and skilled personnel that the long-term Aegis C deployment plan — 1,000 pods per year, sustained over decades — will require, and that the program therefore has a strategic interest in nurturing, through technology sharing, supplier development, workforce training, and the open publication of its process specifications (such as this document) wherever the program's security classification (Aegis C — Advanced Fabrication, which is The layer-by-layer inspection — performed after each ply (or, for the ±45° plies that are laid up in pairs by the two AFP machines operating simultaneously, after each pair of plies) is completed and before the next ply is deposited — combines a high-resolution automated optical inspection (AOI) of the laminate surface with a targeted manual visual inspection of any regions that the AOI flags as anomalous, a two-tier inspection protocol that leverages the speed and consistency of the automated system for the bulk of the inspection (the AOI system, consisting of a 150-megapixel line-scan camera (Dalsa Linea ML, 16,384 pixels × 4 lines, RGB + monochrome, 70 kHz line rate) mounted on the same KUKA robotic arm that carries the ultrasonic transducer for the post-cure inspection (PART-087), scanning the hull surface at a speed of 0.5 meters per second with a 50-micrometer pixel resolution at the object plane — approximately 100 times finer than the 6.35-millimeter tow width, providing ample resolution to detect surface defects such as tow gaps (spacings between adjacent tow courses that exceed the 1.0-millimeter specification limit), tow overlaps (overlaps exceeding the 2.0-millimeter specification limit), tow twists (a tow that has rotated about its longitudinal axis during placement, causing the tow to present its edge rather than its flat face to the substrate, a condition that prevents proper compaction and produces a local region of high fiber volume fraction and low resin content, a "hard spot" in the laminate that the ultrasonic inspection would detect as a localized increase in the ultrasonic velocity and a decrease in the attenuation, and that the AOI system detects through the change in the tow's apparent width and surface texture), tow wrinkles (out-of-plane undulations of the tow, typically occurring at the end caps where the tow must follow a doubly-curved path, the undulations being caused by the tow's inherent bending stiffness resisting the curvature, a condition that the AFP toolpath algorithm attempts to minimize through the geodesic path planning described in PART-081 but that cannot be entirely eliminated, and that the AOI detects through the characteristic shadow pattern created by the wrinkled tow under the oblique illumination provided by the AOI system's LED line lights — four 200-watt LED bars, arranged at 45-degree incidence angles on both sides of the camera's scan line, providing high-contrast, shadow-free illumination that reveals the surface topography of the laminate with a sensitivity to height variations of approximately 10 micrometers, sufficient to detect even the subtle undulations of a nascent wrinkle before it becomes severe enough to require rework), and foreign object debris (FOD) — any particulate matter on the laminate surface, whether from the AFP cell environment (dust, fiber fragments, resin flakes), from the operator (a dropped tool, a pen, a glove fragment), or from the tow itself (a broken filament, a resin-rich droplet that separated from the tow during the cutting and restart sequence), that, if left in place and over-laminated, would become an inclusion in the laminate, a defect type that is among the most detrimental because the inclusion, which has zero structural strength and which debonds from the surrounding composite under load, acts as a delamination initiation site, a planar crack that can grow under the hydrostatic compressive loading — compressive loading tends to close cracks rather than open them, but in a laminated composite under external pressure, the through-thickness (radial) compressive stress is accompanied by in-plane tensile stresses due to the Poisson effect, and these tensile stresses can open a delamination that is oriented parallel to the plies, a counter-intuitive failure mode that the stress analysis has identified and that the defect tolerance analysis accounts for, with the critical delamination size being approximately 25 millimeters in diameter for the Atlantis hull at the design external pressure of 4.5 MPa, meaning that any delamination smaller than 25 millimeters will not propagate under the design load and is therefore acceptable (a "non-critical" defect, per the classification system below), while any delamination larger than this threshold MUST be repaired before the hull is accepted, a requirement that the AOI system supports by detecting the FOD that would create such a delamination and enabling its removal before the next ply covers it, a prevention that is far preferable to the detection and repair of the delamination after it has formed, because the repair of a delamination deep within the laminate requires the excavation of the overlying plies — a "scarf" repair in which the damaged region is ground out in a shallow, tapered cone, extending radially outward from the delamination at a slope of approximately 1:20 (1 millimeter of depth for every 20 millimeters of radial distance, a slope that is necessary to provide sufficient bond area for the repair patch, which is laid up ply-by-ply, matching the original stacking sequence, using the same AFP machine (the repair is performed by programming the AFP machine to follow the scarfed surface contour, depositing precisely-shaped plies that fill the excavation and restore the original laminate thickness and ply orientation, the repair being consolidated by the same laser heating and compaction as the original layup, and then being post-cured along with the rest of the hull, a repair that is effectively indistinguishable from the original laminate in terms of its mechanical properties, as the destructive testing of repaired specimens has confirmed — the strength of a properly-executed scarf repair is at least 95 percent of the strength of the pristine laminate, with the slight reduction attributable to the discontinuities at the repair boundary, where the fibers in the original laminate are cut and the load must be transferred across the repair interface through the matrix, a load transfer that is inherently less efficient than the continuous fiber load path of the pristine laminate, but that is sufficient to restore the laminate's load-bearing capacity to a level that satisfies the certification requirements, a capability that the repair procedure is qualified to provide and that the Atlantis program considers essential for the economic viability of the production process — without the ability to repair defects, any defect exceeding the acceptance criteria would require the scrapping of the affected hull section at a cost of approximately $180,000 per section (the material and labor cost of fabricating a replacement section, based on the cost breakdown in PART-094), an outcome that the repair procedure avoids for all but the most severe defects — which is the preferred outcome, prevention, rather than detection and repair — and that the in-process AOI enables by detecting the FOD at the moment of its deposition, before the next ply is laid, when the FOD can simply be removed with tweezers or a vacuum pickup tool, a trivial operation that takes seconds and that prevents a defect that would otherwise require hours to repair or that could, if undetected, grow to catastrophic size during the pod's service life, an impact that is disproportionate — seconds of prevention versus hours of repair or, in the worst case, the loss of the pod and its occupants, a disproportion that illustrates the value of the in-process inspection and that justifies the capital cost of the AOI system, approximately $450,000 for the camera, the robot, the illumination, and the image processing software, a cost that the ZIRCONIA register's analysis found to be recovered through the avoidance of a single scrapped hull section (the $180,000 cost of a replacement section being approximately 40 percent of the AOI system's cost, so that the AOI system pays for itself after preventing approximately 2.5 scrapped sections, a payback that the Atlantis program's production history has achieved, with the AOI system having prevented an estimated 12 potential scrapped sections — FOD incidents and tow placement anomalies that, without the AOI detection, would have been over-laminated and would have become embedded defects requiring either extensive repairs or hull scrapping, and that the AOI's real-time detection enabled the operator to correct immediately, the cumulative cost avoidance of approximately $2.16 million far exceeding the AOI system's capital and operating costs, a return on investment that ZIRCONIA rates as "excellent" and that confirms the economic as well as the technical wisdom of the in-process inspection investment) whereas the manual visual inspection, performed by a trained quality inspector who examines the laminate surface under a high-intensity, color-corrected LED floodlight (5,000 lux at the inspection surface, CRI > 90, approximating natural daylight to reveal the subtle color and texture variations that indicate a surface anomaly — a resin-rich region, which appears glossier and more translucent than the surrounding laminate; a resin-starved region, which appears duller and more fibrous; a tow wrinkle, which casts a slightly different shadow pattern than the surrounding flat tows; a local discoloration from a temperature excursion during the AFP layup, which manifests as a slight yellowing or browning of the Elium resin (the onset of thermal degradation, which, if the temperature excursion was brief and shallow — a surface-only overheating that did not extend more than a few micrometers into the laminate — is acceptable, because the degraded layer will be removed during the post-cure surface preparation (a light sanding with 320-grit abrasive that removes approximately 50 micrometers from the surface, eliminating the thermally-degraded material and exposing the pristine laminate beneath), but if the temperature excursion was severe and deep, causing degradation that extends beyond the 50-micrometer removal depth, the affected region must be excavated and repaired using the scarf repair procedure described above, a decision that the quality inspector makes based on the depth of the discoloration as judged by scraping the surface with a scalpel — if the discoloration disappears after scraping off approximately 50 micrometers of material, the defect is "cosmetic" and acceptable; if it persists beyond 50 micrometers, it is "structural" and requires repair, a simple, low-tech but effective distinction that the quality inspectors have been trained to make and that the defect classification system formalizes) — and confirms or refutes the AOI's anomaly calls, providing the human judgment that, despite the sophistication of the automated system, remains essential for the interpretation of ambiguous or borderline indications, a human-in-the-loop quality assurance model that the Aegis C philosophy endorses — not as a concession to the limitations of automation (which are, as the 0.2 percent defect rate demonstrates, minimal in this application) but as a recognition that the responsibility for the quality of a product whose failure would result in the loss of human life must ultimately rest with human beings, who accept that responsibility consciously and willingly, and who exercise their judgment in fulfillment of that responsibility, a moral dimension of the quality assurance function that the Atlantis program acknowledges and respects, and that the training, certification, and accountability of the quality inspectors — each of whom must complete a 200-hour training program covering the LBFRP-001 material specification, the defect classification system, the AOI system's operation and interpretation, the rework procedures, and the ethical obligations of a quality inspector in a safety-critical manufacturing environment, and must pass a written examination and a practical skills demonstration before being certified to inspect production hardware — is designed to support, a training program that is itself subject to periodic review and revision as the process knowledge base grows and as the defect taxonomy evolves to reflect the accumulated experience of the production team.

The defect classification system — the taxonomy of defects, their severity levels, and the corresponding disposition actions (accept as-is, repair, or scrap) — is based on a fracture-mechanics analysis of the effect of each defect type on the laminate's structural performance, an analysis that uses the finite element model of the hull (described in PART-087) in which a defect of a given type, size, and location is explicitly modeled — a void is modeled as a region of zero stiffness and zero strength within the laminate, with the surrounding composite being represented by the LBFRP-001 orthotropic elastic properties, and the stress concentration at the void boundary being calculated by the FEA, which then feeds into a progressive damage model (a continuum damage mechanics model, implemented in ABAQUS via a user-defined material subroutine (UMAT), that degrades the elastic properties of the composite as a function of the damage state variables — matrix cracking, fiber breakage, and delamination — which evolve according to the loading history and the material's damage evolution laws, calibrated against the experimental data from the LBFRP-001 material qualification program) that simulates the growth of the defect under the design loads — the 4.5 MPa external pressure during normal service, the elevated dynamic pressures during a crustal displacement event (modeled as a transient pressure pulse with a peak of 6.0 MPa and a duration of 3,600 seconds, based on the CFD simulation of the turbidite flow described in Section 6 of the parent document), and the fatigue loading from the tidal current fluctuations (modeled as a sinusoidal pressure variation of ±0.05 MPa at a frequency of 12.4 hours — the period of the principal lunar semidiurnal tidal constituent, M2, which dominates the tidal currents at both the Monterey and Osa sites, as documented by the harmonic analysis of the site current meter data — for 36,500 cycles over the 50-year design life), and that determines, for each defect type and size, whether the defect will grow to a critical size — the size at which the hull can no longer sustain the design loads — within the 50-year service life, a determination that is the basis for the defect classification and the acceptance criteria, an engineering analysis that is specific to the Atlantis hull geometry, the LBFRP-001 material properties, and the deployment site environmental conditions, and that the certification authority (PART-120) has reviewed and accepted as the basis for the defect acceptance criteria, a critical step in the certification process because the criteria ultimately determine which hulls are deployed and which are rejected, a determination that must be demonstrably conservative (i.e., the criteria must accept only those defects that the analysis shows, with a high degree of confidence, will not grow to critical size within the service life, with the confidence level being specified as 99 percent — meaning that for every 100 defects of a given size that are accepted, no more than 1 will grow to critical size, a confidence level that is achieved through the combination of the conservative assumptions in the damage growth model (the initial defect size is assumed to be 10 percent larger than the measured size, to account for the measurement uncertainty; the fatigue crack growth rate is assumed to follow the upper 95-percent confidence bound of the experimental data, rather than the mean, to account for the material variability; the applied loads are assumed to be 20 percent higher than the best-estimate values, to account for the uncertainty in the CFD simulation of the wave and turbidite forces), the statistical characterization of the material properties (the design allowables, which are based on the B-basis — the value above which at least 90 percent of the population of property measurements is expected to fall, with 95 percent confidence — rather than on the mean, providing a statistical margin that accounts for the material variability), and the validation of the damage growth model through the comparison of its predictions with the results of the accelerated fatigue and sustained-load tests on the LBFRP-001 specimens containing manufactured defects, a validation that demonstrates that the model's predictions are conservative — the actual defect growth observed in the tests is slower than the model predicts, by a factor of approximately 1.5 to 2.0, depending on the defect type and the loading condition, a conservatism that the certification authority regards as appropriate and that provides the required 99 percent confidence level, a level that the Aegis C safety philosophy considers the minimum acceptable for a safety-critical application — the program would prefer a 99.9 percent or even a 99.99 percent confidence level, but such extreme confidence levels are not practically achievable given the inherent variability of composite materials and the finite number of test specimens that can be produced and tested within the program's budget and schedule, a practical limitation that the program acknowledges and that the management of the residual risk — the 1 percent of defects that may, despite the conservative analysis, grow to critical size — is addressed through the post-deployment monitoring program described in PART-109, which provides a safety net that would detect the growth of a defect through the pod's structural health monitoring system (strain gauges, acoustic emission sensors, and periodic ROV visual inspections) before it reached the critical size, enabling the pod to be evacuated and recovered before a catastrophic failure, a layered safety approach that, like the pod's turbidite defense architecture, provides defense-in-depth against the possibility of a defect escaping the quality system's detection and growing to a dangerous size, a possibility that the Aegis C safety philosophy does not deny — no quality system is perfect — but that it addresses through the multiple, independent layers of protection that, collectively, reduce the risk of a catastrophic pod failure to a level that the program's risk management framework considers "acceptable," a level that is quantified as less than 10⁻⁶ per pod per year (one in one million probability of a catastrophic failure in any given year), a risk target that is consistent with the safety goals of the commercial nuclear power industry (the US Nuclear Regulatory Commission's safety goal for nuclear power plants is that the risk of a prompt fatality to an individual member of the public from a reactor accident should not exceed 0.1 percent of the risk from all other accidents, which, given the average accidental death rate in the United States of approximately 4 × 10⁻⁴ per person per year, implies a reactor accident fatality risk of approximately 4 × 10⁻⁷ per person per year, comparable to the 10⁻⁶ per pod per year target, given that a pod catastrophic failure would affect multiple occupants, scaling the individual risk by the 4-person standard crew to approximately 2.5 × 10⁻⁷ per person per year, which is below the NRC's risk target, a favorable comparison that confirms that the Atlantis pod's safety level is comparable to that of the most safety-critical engineered systems in existence, a level that the Aegis C safety philosophy considers necessary and appropriate for a habitation module whose occupants have no alternative means of escape and whose survival depends entirely on the integrity of the hull that the quality system described in this document is designed to ensure).

Defects are classified into four severity levels, each with a specific disposition action: Level I — "Cosmetic" defects, which have no measurable effect on the laminate's structural performance (e.g., a surface discoloration of less than 50 micrometers depth, a minor tow gap of less than 0.5 millimeters that does not extend into the underlying ply — a gap that is superficial, affecting only the surface appearance, and that will be filled by the resin from the subsequent ply during the AFP consolidation, a self-healing mechanism that the layer-by-layer process naturally provides and that the defect classification acknowledges by not requiring any action for such superficial gaps, a practical consideration that avoids the rework of defects that pose no structural risk, saving time and money without compromising safety), and are accepted without repair, the only required action being the documentation of the defect's location, type, and size in the hull's digital fabrication record, a documentation that serves the traceability function described above and that provides the statistical data on the occurrence of cosmetic defects that the SPC system monitors — an increase in the cosmetic defect rate, while not a safety concern, could indicate a drift in the AFP process that, if uncorrected, might eventually produce structural defects, an early-warning function that the SPC system provides by analyzing the trend of cosmetic defect occurrences, using the same Western Electric rules that govern the process control charts, and that enables the manufacturing engineer to investigate and correct the process drift while the defects are still cosmetic, before they become structural, a leading-indicator capability that is one of the most valuable benefits of the layer-by-layer inspection and the SPC monitoring system; Level II — "Minor Structural" defects, which have a measurable but small effect on the laminate's structural performance and which the fracture-mechanics analysis predicts will not grow to critical size within the 50-year service life (e.g., a void of 5 millimeters diameter located at least 10 millimeters from the hull surface, a tow gap of 1 to 2 millimeters that extends into the underlying ply and creates a resin-rich pocket, a local fiber volume fraction deviation of up to ±5 percent from the 60 percent specification — a resin-rich region, with a fiber volume fraction of 55 percent, which is slightly weaker and less stiff than the specification but which is still capable of sustaining the design loads, the FEA of the local laminate with the reduced fiber volume fraction showing that the stress in the affected region is elevated by approximately 8 percent, which is within the material's strength margin and does not cause failure — or a minor tow wrinkle with an amplitude of less than 0.5 millimeters, which produces a local reduction in the compressive strength of approximately 5 percent, per the experimental data from the LBFRP-001 compression-after-impact testing, a reduction that the hull's global factor of safety of 4.0 accommodates without the local stress exceeding the material's strength, the stress analysis confirming that the most highly stressed point in the hull, which is at the transition between the cylinder and the forward end cap where the curvature changes most abruptly, has a margin against the design load of approximately 2.5 even after the 5 percent strength reduction at the wrinkle location, such that the wrinkle does not represent a threat to the hull's integrity, and is accepted without repair, the only required action — in addition to the documentation — being the annotation of the defect location on the hull's structural health monitoring plan, so that the ROV inspection during the post-deployment monitoring (PART-109) can give particular attention to that location, verifying that no growth or change has occurred, a condition-based maintenance approach that is appropriate for minor defects that are stable and non-critical but that warrant periodic surveillance because of the possibility, however remote, of their growth under the combined effects of the service loads and the environmental degradation, a surveillance that the ROV's high-definition camera and the hull's permanently-installed strain gauge network — 120 strain gauges (as described in PART-087), distributed at the locations of greatest structural interest, including the known defect locations, and monitored by the pod's S-Bus data acquisition system, which transmits the strain data to the surface relay buoy and, via satellite, to the Aegis C command network, enabling the structural health of every deployed pod to be monitored continuously, a capability that is unique to the Atlantis program and that transforms the management of minor defects from a passive acceptance (the defect is there, and we hope it doesn't grow) to an active surveillance (the defect is there, we are
