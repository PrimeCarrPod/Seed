# CSMFAB077-PART-083 — ELIUM THERMOPLASTIC RESIN: CHEMISTRY, POLYMERIZATION KINETICS, GLASS TRANSITION, MOISTURE ABSORPTION
## Block 8 — Fabrication Methods | Atlantis-Class Subsea Habitation Module Production
## Carrington Storm Motors — Safe Pod Engineering Division | Aegis C Material Architecture

---

The Elium thermoplastic resin system, developed by Arkema S.A. of Colombes, France and licensed to Carrington Storm Motors under an exclusive subsea habitation application agreement negotiated through the Aegis C material supply chain diversification program described in PART-092, represents a fundamental departure from the thermoset epoxy and vinyl ester resin systems that have dominated the marine composites industry for the past five decades — a departure that is not merely an incremental improvement in material properties but a paradigm shift in the manufacturing and in-service behavior of fiber-reinforced polymer composites, and one whose technical implications for the Atlantis program extend far beyond the obvious advantage of weldability (the ability to join cured composite components through local melting and fusion of the thermoplastic matrix, eliminating the need for adhesively-bonded or mechanically-fastened joints, with their attendant stress concentrations, long-term durability concerns, and inspection challenges) to encompass a comprehensive set of properties — low exothermic heat generation during polymerization (eliminating the risk of thermal runaway and charring that limits the thickness of thermoset composite laminates to approximately 50 millimeters in a single cure cycle, whereas the Atlantis hull's 35-millimeter laminate thickness is safely within the Elium system's processing window, and the AFP laser-heated layup described in PART-081, with its precise control of the heat input at each ply interface, eliminates the through-thickness thermal gradients that would otherwise accumulate in a thick thermoset laminate during an autoclave cure cycle, causing differential cure shrinkage between the hotter interior and cooler surface of the laminate that generates residual thermal stresses sufficient to cause micro-cracking of the matrix before the laminate is ever placed in service), reversibility (the capacity to re-melt and re-form the thermoplastic matrix — albeit with some degradation of the polymer's molecular weight due to chain scission at each melt cycle, a degradation that the Atlantis program manages by limiting the cumulative thermal history of the Elium resin to no more than three melt cycles, corresponding to the initial melt-blending of the lonsdaleite nanodiamond additive described in PART-085, the AFP layup described in PART-081, and a potential post-deployment repair scenario in which a damaged hull section could be locally re-melted using the same laser heating technology and re-consolidated to restore its structural integrity, a repair capability that is uniquely available to thermoplastic composites and that provides an operational flexibility in the deployed pod maintenance strategy that thermoset composites — which cannot be re-melted once cured, because the crosslinked polymer network decomposes before melting, typically at temperatures above 300°C, leaving a carbonaceous char that has no structural value — cannot offer), recyclability (the ability to recover the polymer matrix from end-of-life composite structures through a thermal depolymerization process that breaks the poly(methyl methacrylate) chains back to methyl methacrylate monomer with a yield of approximately 85 percent, a recycling pathway that is economically marginal at current virgin monomer prices but that the Atlantis program's life-cycle analysis identifies as a future benefit when the demonstration pods reach the end of their fifty-year design life and must be decommissioned, at which point the recovered monomer can be purified and re-polymerized into new Elium resin for the next generation of Atlantis pods, closing the material loop and reducing the program's long-term dependence on petrochemical feedstocks for the methyl methacrylate monomer production — a consideration that the Citadel register's long-horizon perspective identifies as strategically important, even if economically neutral under current market conditions) — that are uniquely suited to the Atlantis application and that, collectively, justify the significant investment in characterizing, qualifying, and industrializing the Elium system for the specific demands of subsea habitation module fabrication, a characterization effort that constitutes the subject of the present document, PART-083.

The Elium resin is a reactive thermoplastic based on poly(methyl methacrylate), or PMMA — the same polymer that is familiar to the public as Plexiglas or acrylic, but engineered through Arkema's proprietary reactive processing technology to overcome the fundamental limitation that has historically prevented PMMA from being used as a structural composite matrix: the extremely high melt viscosity of conventional PMMA, which at typical melt processing temperatures of 200-250°C exceeds 10,000 Pa·s (pascal-seconds), a viscosity that is approximately 100 times higher than the maximum viscosity at which a polymer melt can flow through a fiber bed and wet out the individual filaments — a process that requires the melt viscosity to be below approximately 100 Pa·s, such that the polymer can infiltrate the micron-scale interstices between adjacent filaments under the driving force of the compaction pressure applied by the AFP roller (typically 0.5 to 1.0 MPa as described in PART-081), a pressure that, by Darcy's law for flow through a porous medium, produces an infiltration velocity that is inversely proportional to the fluid viscosity, meaning that a 100-fold increase in viscosity produces a 100-fold decrease in infiltration velocity, reducing the resin flow rate to levels at which the AFP machine's layup speed would have to be reduced from 1.2 meters per second to approximately 0.012 meters per second — a reduction of 99 percent, rendering the process economically nonviable — to achieve the same degree of fiber wet-out and interlaminar consolidation that the Atlantis laminate quality specification demands. Arkema's innovation, which forms the chemical foundation of the Atlantis fabrication process, is to supply the Elium resin not as a fully-polymerized PMMA melt but as a reactive liquid mixture comprising methyl methacrylate (MMA) monomer — the low-molecular-weight building block of PMMA, a clear, volatile liquid with a viscosity of approximately 0.6 mPa·s at room temperature, approximately seven orders of magnitude lower than the melt viscosity of its fully-polymerized product — dissolved PMMA polymer at a concentration of approximately 20 to 25 percent by weight (the "syrup" that provides the reactive mixture with sufficient viscosity — approximately 500 to 1,500 mPa·s, depending on the dissolved polymer molecular weight and concentration — to remain in place on the basalt fiber tow during the prepregging process described in PART-086, rather than dripping from the tow before it can be consolidated into the laminate), a thermal polymerization initiator (typically a peroxide compound such as benzoyl peroxide or lauroyl peroxide, which decomposes at elevated temperature — the activation temperature depending on the specific peroxide, with lauroyl peroxide having a 10-hour half-life temperature of approximately 62°C and being the preferred initiator for the Elium formulation used in the Atlantis program because its relatively low activation temperature allows the polymerization to proceed at the 175°C nip point temperature achieved by the AFP laser heating system, while its decomposition kinetics are sufficiently slow at room temperature to provide an acceptable pot life — the time during which the reactive mixture remains fluid enough to be processed — of approximately 48 hours at 23°C, exceeding the maximum residence time of any batch of Elium resin in the Mojave facility's prepregging system, which operates on a just-in-time basis with a typical batch-to-consumption interval of approximately 8 hours, well within the 48-hour pot life), and a chain-transfer agent or inhibitor system that controls the molecular weight of the resulting PMMA polymer by terminating the growing polymer chains at a controlled average length, producing a polymer with a target number-average molecular weight (Mn) of approximately 80,000 to 120,000 grams per mole — a molecular weight range that balances the requirements for mechanical strength (which increases with molecular weight, because longer polymer chains provide more entanglements per chain, increasing the toughness and the resistance to slow crack growth under sustained load — the creep rupture resistance, which is the critical long-term failure mode for a pressure vessel subjected to continuous hydrostatic compression — but eventually reaches a plateau above approximately Mn = 100,000 g/mol, beyond which further molecular weight increases produce diminishing returns in mechanical properties while dramatically increasing the melt viscosity, making the polymer unprocessable by the AFP method), chemical resistance (which also increases with molecular weight, because the concentration of chain ends — which are the sites most susceptible to chemical attack by water, oxygen, and the dissolved salts in seawater — decreases as the chains become longer, reducing the rate of the chain-scission degradation reactions that cause the polymer to lose molecular weight over time in service, a degradation mode that the accelerated aging program described below is designed to characterize and quantify), and processability (which decreases with increasing molecular weight, because the time required for the polymer chains to diffuse across the interface between the incoming tow and the previously deposited ply — the reptation time, in the terminology of polymer physics, which scales with molecular weight to the 3.4 power according to the de Gennes-Doi-Edwards theory of polymer dynamics, such that a doubling of molecular weight increases the reptation time by a factor of approximately 10.6, and which must be shorter than the residence time of the tow in the laser-heated nip point zone — approximately 50 milliseconds as described in PART-081 — for the polymer chains from the two plies to interdiffuse sufficiently to form the entanglements that constitute the interlaminar bond, a constraint that places an upper limit on the Elium molecular weight that is approximately Mn = 150,000 g/mol, above which the reptation time exceeds the available diffusion time and the interlaminar bond strength degrades despite the adequate temperature and pressure conditions at the nip point, a phenomenon that the Atlantis process engineering team has characterized through a series of experiments in which Elium resin formulations with molecular weights ranging from Mn = 50,000 to Mn = 200,000 g/mol were used to fabricate LBFRP-001 laminates under identical AFP conditions, with the resulting interlaminar shear strength measured by the short-beam shear test (ASTM D2344) and found to peak at Mn ≈ 100,000 g/mol with a value of 68 MPa, declining to approximately 55 MPa at Mn = 200,000 g/mol — a 19 percent reduction — confirming the existence of the diffusion-limited interlaminar bonding mechanism and establishing the optimal molecular weight target for the Atlantis Elium formulation).

The polymerization of the Elium resin during the AFP layup process is a free-radical chain polymerization — one of the most thoroughly studied chemical reaction mechanisms in all of polymer science, but one whose kinetics in the specific context of the AFP laser-heated, thin-layer consolidation process are sufficiently complex to warrant the detailed modeling and experimental characterization that the Atlantis program's materials engineering team has performed, because the degree of monomer conversion achieved during the short residence time in the laser-heated zone — and the degree of additional conversion that occurs during the subsequent slow cooling of the deposited ply, as the residual heat in the laminate continues to drive the polymerization reaction at a progressively decreasing rate as the temperature declines toward the glass transition temperature (Tg), below which the polymer chains are immobilized in the glassy state and the polymerization reaction effectively ceases because the monomer molecules can no longer diffuse to the propagating radical chain ends — determines the residual monomer content of the cured laminate, which in turn determines the laminate's glass transition temperature (because residual monomer acts as a plasticizer, reducing the Tg by approximately 2°C per weight percent of residual monomer, an effect that is well-characterized for PMMA and that the Atlantis Elium formulation follows, as confirmed by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) measurements of laminate specimens with known residual monomer contents, prepared by interrupting the polymerization at various stages and measuring the residual monomer by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) — the monomer, having a boiling point of 100°C, evaporates at a temperature well below the PMMA decomposition temperature of approximately 280°C, producing a distinct mass loss step in the TGA curve that can be quantified and correlated with the measured Tg, yielding the relationship Tg = 120°C − 2.1 × (residual monomer weight percent) for the Atlantis Elium formulation, a relationship that the process control system uses to monitor the cure state of each production laminate by periodically extracting witness specimens from the laminate edge — material that is trimmed from the hull during the post-cure machining step and that would otherwise be discarded as scrap — and measuring the Tg by DSC (TA Instruments Q2000 differential scanning calorimeter, heating rate 10°C/min, nitrogen atmosphere, Tg determined as the midpoint of the heat capacity step in the second heating scan, after an initial scan to 150°C to erase the thermal history of the specimen and ensure a consistent, reproducible Tg measurement), with the measured Tg being compared to the specification minimum of 118°C — corresponding to a residual monomer content of approximately 1.0 weight percent — and any laminate with a measured Tg below this threshold being flagged for investigation and potential rework per the defect disposition procedures described in PART-088, a quality control protocol that ensures that the deployed hull laminate achieves the design Tg and therefore retains its full mechanical properties at the maximum service temperature of the pod's interior environment, which is maintained at 25 ± 3°C by the life support system but which could, under a failure scenario in which the life support cooling system is degraded, rise to as high as 40°C — still approximately 78°C below the design Tg, providing a comfortable margin against thermal softening and creep, but only if the actual Tg of the cured laminate meets the 118°C specification minimum, a requirement that the DSC monitoring program is designed to verify for each pod hull before it is accepted for deployment.

The glass transition temperature of the Elium resin — the temperature at which the polymer transitions from the rigid, glassy state that provides the composite's structural stiffness and load-bearing capacity to the rubbery, viscoelastic state in which the polymer chains have sufficient thermal energy to undergo large-scale segmental motions, resulting in a dramatic reduction in the elastic modulus (from approximately 3 GPa in the glassy state to approximately 3 MPa in the rubbery state, a factor of 1,000 decrease) and a corresponding loss of the composite's dimensional stability under load — is the single most critical thermal property for the Atlantis application, because the pod's pressure hull must maintain its structural integrity at the maximum service temperature it could encounter (approximately 40°C, as noted above) while also retaining adequate toughness at the minimum service temperature it could encounter during deployment operations (approximately 5°C, the ambient seawater temperature at the Monterey site during winter, as measured by the CTD profiles collected during the seasonal oceanographic surveys described in the site selection sections of the parent document, and the temperature at which the pod's exterior surface is in direct contact with the surrounding seawater, a thermal boundary condition that imposes a through-thickness temperature gradient across the hull laminate, with the interior surface at 25°C and the exterior surface at 5°C, a 20°C gradient that generates thermal stresses in the laminate due to the differential thermal expansion between the warmer interior plies and the cooler exterior plies, stresses that are additive to the hydrostatic compressive stress from the external water pressure and that must be accommodated within the laminate's design allowables without exceeding the matrix cracking threshold, a design constraint that the laminate's [0°/±45°/90°/±45°/0°]s stacking sequence, with its balanced distribution of plies in multiple orientations, addresses by distributing the thermal stresses across plies with different coefficients of thermal expansion (CTE) — the 0° plies, being oriented parallel to the hull axis, have a CTE of approximately 6 × 10⁻⁶ per °C in the axial direction (dominated by the low CTE of the basalt fiber, approximately 8 × 10⁻⁶ per °C, constrained by the higher CTE of the Elium matrix, approximately 70 × 10⁻⁶ per °C, through the fiber-matrix load transfer mechanism that determines the composite's effective CTE according to the rule-of-mixtures approximation) and approximately 30 × 10⁻⁶ per °C in the hoop direction (dominated by the high CTE of the Elium matrix, because the fibers in the 0° plies provide minimal constraint in the transverse direction), while the 90° plies reverse this anisotropy, providing axial constraint but hoop compliance, a cross-ply architecture that generates interlaminar shear stresses at the ply interfaces during thermal cycling — as the pod experiences the daily and seasonal temperature variations of the seawater environment — but that manages these stresses within the Elium matrix's interlaminar shear strength capacity of approximately 68 MPa at 5°C (a temperature at which the matrix is well within its glassy state and its shear strength is near its maximum, because the polymer chains are immobilized and resist shear deformation through the same intermolecular forces — van der Waals interactions and chain entanglements — that provide the glassy-state modulus, whereas at temperatures approaching the Tg, the onset of segmental chain motions reduces the shear strength, and at temperatures above the Tg, the shear strength collapses as the polymer transitions to the rubbery state, a failure mode that must be avoided by ensuring that the laminate's in-service temperature never approaches the Tg, a design requirement that is satisfied by the 118°C minimum Tg specification and the 40°C maximum service temperature, which together provide a margin of 78°C between the maximum service temperature and the Tg, a margin that the thermal and structural analysis of the hull confirms is more than adequate for the expected thermal environment, with the worst-case thermal stress — occurring during a winter deployment scenario in which the pod's interior is heated to 25°C by the life support system while the exterior is at 5°C, producing the maximum through-thickness temperature gradient — calculated using the classical lamination theory (CLT) thermal stress analysis implemented in the Siemens Fibersim composite design module, with the predicted interlaminar shear stress of 12 MPa at the critical ply interface being less than 18 percent of the matrix's shear strength at 5°C, a margin that the structural analysis team considers acceptable for continuous operation and that is further improved by the gradual thermal equilibration that occurs over the pod's first few days of submerged operation, as the heat from the interior environment warms the exterior hull surface through conduction, reducing the through-thickness gradient and the associated thermal stresses, a self-mitigating thermal behavior that the structural analysis accounts for through a transient thermal FEA model of the hull cross-section, which shows that the temperature gradient decays from the initial 20°C at deployment to a steady-state value of approximately 8°C after approximately 72 hours of operation, with the corresponding interlaminar shear stress decaying from 12 MPa to approximately 5 MPa, well within the matrix's capacity and confirming that thermal stresses are a deployment transient rather than a continuous service concern for the Atlantis hull).

The moisture absorption behavior of the Elium resin — and the associated degradation of the polymer's mechanical properties through the plasticization mechanism (the water molecules, penetrating the polymer network and occupying the free volume between polymer chains, increase the average inter-chain spacing and thereby reduce the intermolecular forces — primarily van der Waals dispersion forces — that provide the polymer's stiffness and strength, an effect that is quantified by the decrease in the glass transition temperature with increasing moisture content, analogous to the plasticization by residual monomer described above but occurring through a different mechanism — water plasticization is reversible upon drying, whereas residual monomer is gradually consumed by continued polymerization if the temperature is elevated above the Tg, which for a deployed pod at the seabed is never the case, so that the residual monomer remains indefinitely and its plasticizing effect is permanent, a distinction that has implications for the long-term prediction of the laminate's mechanical properties, because the residual monomer content is fixed at the time of fabrication and its plasticizing effect is therefore constant over the pod's service life, whereas the moisture content varies with the humidity of the pod's interior environment and the seawater exposure of the exterior surface, a spatial and temporal variation that the structural analysis must account for through a worst-case assumption — exterior surface saturated with seawater (approximately 3.5 percent moisture by weight for the Elium resin, based on the equilibrium moisture absorption measured in the laboratory immersion tests described below), interior surface at the equilibrium moisture content corresponding to the pod's interior relative humidity of 50 ± 10 percent (approximately 1.0 percent moisture by weight for the Elium resin, based on the moisture absorption isotherm measured by exposing Elium specimens to controlled humidity environments at 25°C and measuring the weight gain until equilibrium was reached, the resulting data being fitted to the Park model for moisture absorption in polymers, which accounts for both the Henry's law dissolution of water in the polymer matrix and the Langmuir-type adsorption of water at specific hydrophilic sites — in the case of Elium/PMMA, the carbonyl oxygen atoms of the ester side groups, which can hydrogen-bond with water molecules, providing approximately 30 percent of the total moisture absorption capacity, with the remaining 70 percent being the Henry's law dissolution in the polymer free volume — a model that the materials engineering team selected over the simpler Fickian diffusion model, which assumes a single moisture absorption mechanism and which underpredicts the equilibrium moisture content at high humidity levels because it does not account for the Langmuir adsorption sites) — with a linear moisture concentration gradient through the laminate thickness connecting the two surface values, a gradient that produces a corresponding gradient in the matrix mechanical properties — at the exterior surface, the Tg is reduced by approximately 8°C (from 120°C to 112°C) due to the 3.5 percent moisture content, while at the interior surface, the Tg reduction is approximately 2.5°C due to the 1.0 percent moisture content — and that the structural analysis addresses by using the degraded matrix properties at the worst-case (exterior) location for the buckling and collapse pressure calculations, a conservative assumption that ensures the hull's factor of safety against collapse remains above the design minimum of 4.0 even after the matrix has equilibrated with the seawater environment, as confirmed by the post-immersion pressure testing of sub-scale hull sections that were fabricated alongside the production hulls and that were immersed in synthetic seawater at 4.5 MPa and 15°C for 2,000 hours before being tested to collapse in the Mojave facility's hydrostatic pressure test chamber (a 5-meter-diameter, 20-meter-long cylindrical pressure vessel rated for 25 MPa internal pressure, capable of testing full-diameter hull sections up to 4 meters in length, and equipped with strain gauge instrumentation — 120 foil strain gauges per test section, distributed in rosette patterns at 12 axial stations and 8 circumferential positions around the hull, providing approximately 960 independent strain measurements during the pressure test — and acoustic emission sensors that detect the ultrasonic signals generated by matrix cracking and fiber breakage events during the pressure ramp, providing real-time indication of damage progression that complements the strain gauge data in identifying the pressure at which the laminate's damage tolerance limit is exceeded and the hull can no longer be considered safe for continued service, even if it has not yet collapsed, a damage-based acceptance criterion that the Aegis C certification philosophy described in PART-120 requires in addition to the ultimate collapse pressure criterion, consistent with the damage tolerance design philosophy that is standard in aerospace composite structures but that is more demanding than the deterministic collapse pressure criterion typically used in submarine pressure hull design, reflecting the Atlantis program's commitment to the highest standard of structural safety for a habitation module that cannot be evacuated, inspected, or repaired once deployed to the seabed — there is no "safe abort" procedure for a subsea habitation pod at 45 meters depth; either the hull maintains its integrity, or the occupants are lost, a binary outcome that justifies the conservative certification approach).

The moisture absorption kinetics of the Elium resin — the rate at which water penetrates the polymer and the laminate, establishing the moisture concentration profile as a function of time and position — is governed by Fick's second law of diffusion, which for the one-dimensional case of moisture diffusion through the hull laminate thickness (the primary diffusion direction, because the laminate's length and circumference are large relative to its thickness, and the edges are sealed by the end cap geometry, such that moisture can only enter through the interior and exterior surfaces) predicts a moisture concentration profile that evolves with the square root of time, with the characteristic diffusion time being τ = L² / D, where L is the laminate thickness (35 millimeters for the cylindrical hull section, 25 millimeters for the end caps) and D is the diffusion coefficient of water in the Elium matrix, which has been measured through the standard gravimetric sorption experiment — specimens of neat Elium resin (i.e., without basalt fiber reinforcement), machined into plaques of dimensions 50 mm × 50 mm × 2 mm and dried to constant weight in a vacuum oven at 60°C for 48 hours before being immersed in distilled water at four temperatures (25, 40, 60, and 80°C) — and found to follow an Arrhenius temperature dependence, D = D₀ × exp(−Ea / RT), with D₀ = 3.2 × 10⁻³ cm²/s and Ea = 42 kJ/mol, yielding D ≈ 8.5 × 10⁻⁹ cm²/s at the 15°C seawater temperature of the Monterey deployment site, which for a diffusion path length of L = 1.75 cm (half the laminate thickness, corresponding to diffusion from one surface, the other surface being at the interior of the pod and therefore not in contact with seawater) produces a characteristic diffusion time of τ = (1.75 cm)² / (8.5 × 10⁻⁹ cm²/s) = 3.6 × 10⁸ seconds ≈ 11.4 years, meaning that the moisture concentration at the laminate's mid-plane — the point farthest from either surface, and therefore the last point in the laminate to reach moisture equilibrium — will approach but not fully reach its equilibrium value during the pod's fifty-year design life, and that the moisture concentration profile in the laminate is never steady-state but is continuously evolving, albeit at an ever-decreasing rate as the gradient between the surface and the interior decreases, a temporal evolution that the structural analysis accounts for by modeling the moisture-dependent matrix properties as a function of the time-dependent moisture concentration profile, rather than assuming the worst-case equilibrium moisture profile throughout the service life — an assumption that would be excessively conservative for the early and middle decades of service, when much of the laminate thickness is still at a low moisture content and the matrix properties are correspondingly higher, and that the Atlantis structural analysis avoids through the use of a coupled diffusion-stress finite element model (implemented in Abaqus with a user-defined material subroutine, UMAT, that defines the Elium matrix's elastic properties as a function of the local moisture concentration, using the experimentally-measured relationship between moisture content, Tg, and elastic modulus — specifically, the elastic modulus is modeled as E(T, c) = E₀(T) × [1 − β × (c / c_sat)^n], where E₀(T) is the dry-state modulus at temperature T (which itself decreases slightly with increasing temperature, from 3.2 GPa at 5°C to 2.8 GPa at 40°C, a decrease of approximately 12.5 percent that is typical for glassy polymers in this temperature range, reflecting the increasing thermal energy of the polymer chains and the corresponding reduction in the effective stiffness of the intermolecular bonds), β = 0.25 is an empirical constant determined from the correlation between the measured modulus of moisture-conditioned Elium specimens and their moisture content, c is the local moisture concentration, c_sat = 3.5 weight percent is the saturation moisture content at the exterior surface, and n = 0.5 corresponds to the square-root dependence of the plasticization effect on moisture content that has been observed for PMMA and other glassy polymers, consistent with the free-volume theory of plasticization in which each water molecule increases the fractional free volume by an amount proportional to the water molecule's volume, and the glass transition temperature decrease is proportional to the increase in fractional free volume according to the Fox equation, Tg = [w₁/Tg₁ + w₂/Tg₂]⁻¹ where w₁ and w₂ are the weight fractions of the polymer and the absorbed water respectively, Tg₁ = 120°C is the dry-state glass transition temperature, and Tg₂ = −137°C is the glass transition temperature of water (extrapolated from the measured Tg of hyperquenched amorphous solid water, a value that is physically meaningful because water, when uniformly dispersed in a polymer matrix at the molecular level, acts to depress the polymer's Tg according to the same thermodynamic principles that govern the plasticization of polymers by low-molecular-weight diluents), yielding a Tg reduction of approximately 2.3°C per weight percent of absorbed water, consistent with the experimentally-observed relationship for the Elium system — a materials model that, while more complex than the simple assumption of saturated matrix properties, provides a more accurate — and in most cases, less conservative — prediction of the laminate's structural behavior over its fifty-year service life, and that the ABS/DNV/Lloyds certification review has accepted as a valid basis for the structural analysis, subject to the confirmation of the model's predictions through the post-immersion testing of hull witness specimens as described above.

The Elium resin's polymerization kinetics — the rate at which the MMA monomer is converted to PMMA polymer as a function of temperature, initiator concentration, and the concentrations of any inhibitors, chain-transfer agents, or other additives in the reactive mixture — governs both the processing window for the AFP layup (the time available between the deposition of successive plies, during which the previously deposited ply must remain sufficiently fluid to form an interlaminar bond with the incoming tow) and the ultimate degree of monomer conversion (and hence the residual monomer content, which as discussed above affects the Tg and the long-term mechanical properties), and has been characterized for the Atlantis Elium formulation through a combination of isothermal and non-isothermal differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) experiments, in which small samples (approximately 10 mg) of the Elium reactive mixture were sealed in aluminum DSC pans and subjected to controlled temperature programs — either isothermal holds at temperatures ranging from 60°C to 140°C (covering the range from the onset of thermally-initiated polymerization at approximately 60°C — the temperature at which the lauroyl peroxide initiator begins to decompose at a measurable rate, generating free radicals that initiate the polymerization — to the onset of thermal degradation at approximately 200°C, where the PMMA begins to depolymerize through an unzipping mechanism that regenerates MMA monomer, a competing reaction that reduces the net monomer conversion and that must be avoided during processing by limiting the AFP laser temperature to 175°C, as specified in PART-081, leaving a 25°C margin below the degradation onset temperature) or constant-rate temperature ramps at rates of 2, 5, 10, and 20°C per minute (covering the range of heating rates that the Elium resin experiences during the AFP laser heating cycle — a heating rate that, at the maximum AFP layup speed of 1.2 m/s and a laser-heated zone length of 8 mm, corresponds to a temperature increase from the mandrel temperature of 80°C to the nip point temperature of 175°C over a time of approximately 6.7 milliseconds, or a heating rate of approximately 14,000°C per second, a rate that is far beyond the capability of any commercial DSC instrument and that must be extrapolated from the lower-rate DSC data using the isoconversional kinetic analysis method — the methods of Kissinger, Flynn-Wall-Ozawa, and Friedman — which assumes that the reaction rate at any given degree of conversion depends only on the temperature, not on the thermal history, and that the apparent activation energy of the reaction can be determined from the shift in the reaction rate curves measured at different heating rates, a methodology that is standard in the thermal analysis of polymer cure kinetics and that the Atlantis materials engineering team has applied to the Elium system, determining that the apparent activation energy for the Elium polymerization is 95 ± 6 kJ/mol — slightly higher than the activation energy for the thermal decomposition of lauroyl peroxide, approximately 128 kJ/mol, because the overall polymerization rate is determined not only by the initiator decomposition rate but also by the propagation and termination rate constants, which have different activation energies and whose combined effect on the overall polymerization rate produces the observed apparent activation energy, a value that the process model uses to extrapolate the DSC-measured kinetics to the AFP heating rates, confirming that the Elium resin achieves approximately 92 percent monomer conversion in the 50-millisecond residence time in the laser-heated nip point zone — corresponding to a residual monomer content of approximately 8 percent immediately after deposition — and that the remaining 8 percent of monomer converts over the subsequent approximately 30 minutes as the laminate cools slowly from the 175°C nip point temperature to the 80°C mandrel temperature, with the polymerization rate during this cooling phase being controlled by the temperature-dependent rate constant at the progressively decreasing temperature, ultimately achieving a final monomer conversion of approximately 99.0 ± 0.5 percent and a residual monomer content of approximately 1.0 ± 0.5 weight percent, values that are consistent with the DSC-measured Tg of the production laminate specimens and that confirm the adequacy of the Elium polymerization kinetics for the Atlantis AFP process, with no modification to the resin formulation required beyond the addition of the lonsdaleite nanodiamond dispersion described in PART-085, which does not affect the polymerization kinetics because the nanodiamond particles, at the 3-5 weight percent loading used in the Atlantis laminate, are chemically inert with respect to the free-radical polymerization mechanism — they neither initiate nor inhibit the reaction, and their effect on the polymerization is limited to a slight increase in the viscosity of the reactive mixture (approximately 15 percent increase at 5 wt% nanodiamond loading, measured by rheometry using a TA Instruments ARES-G2 rheometer with a parallel-plate geometry, 25 mm diameter plates, 1 mm gap, oscillatory shear at a frequency of 1 Hz and a strain amplitude of 1 percent, measuring the complex viscosity of the nanodiamond-containing Elium mixture at temperatures from 25°C to 120°C to characterize the effect of the nanodiamond on the flow behavior during the prepregging and AFP processes, confirming that the viscosity increase is within the acceptable range for the tow impregnation process described in PART-086 and does not require any modification to the prepregging equipment or process parameters) and a slight nucleating effect on the polymer crystallization — Elium/PMMA is an amorphous polymer under normal processing conditions and does not crystallize, but the presence of nanodiamond particles provides heterogeneous nucleation sites that could, in principle, induce a small degree of crystallinity if the polymer were cooled very slowly from the melt, a phenomenon that the materials engineering team has investigated through wide-angle X-ray scattering (WAXS) analysis of nanodiamond-containing Elium specimens and that has been found to produce no detectable crystallinity at the cooling rates characteristic of the AFP process (approximately 100-200°C per minute during the initial cooling phase, far too rapid for crystallization of PMMA, which is a notoriously slow-crystallizing polymer even under optimal conditions of high temperature and long annealing time, with the maximum reported crystallinity for PMMA being approximately 5 percent after annealing for hundreds of hours at temperatures near the Tg, conditions that are never approached in the AFP process and that confirm that the Elium matrix in the Atlantis laminate remains fully amorphous, with its mechanical properties governed by the glassy-state behavior of the amorphous chains rather than by any crystalline phase that might be present, a simplification that benefits the structural analysis by eliminating the complexity of a two-phase (amorphous + crystalline) matrix morphology).

The Elium resin specification developed for the Atlantis program — and documented in the Carrington Storm Motors Material Specification CSM-MAT-001, "Elium Thermoplastic Resin for LBFRP-001 Composite Fabrication," which is maintained under the configuration control procedures described in the Aegis C quality management system — defines the following acceptance criteria for each batch of Elium resin received at the Mojave facility, based on the characterization described above: MMA monomer purity ≥ 99.5 percent by gas chromatography (GC), with the identity and concentration of any impurities — which could affect the polymerization kinetics by acting as chain-transfer agents, inhibitors, or retarders, altering the molecular weight distribution and the residual monomer content — determined by GC-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) and compared to the impurity profile of the qualification batch that was used for the LBFRP-001 material qualification testing, with any new impurity exceeding 0.1 percent by weight triggering a material review board (MRB) evaluation to determine whether the impurity is acceptable for use in production laminates; dissolved PMMA polymer concentration 20.0 ± 2.0 weight percent, as measured by the gravimetric method of precipitating the polymer from a sample of the Elium reactive mixture by addition of methanol (a non-solvent for PMMA), filtering, drying, and weighing the precipitated polymer; initiator (lauroyl peroxide) concentration 1.5 ± 0.1 weight percent, measured by iodometric titration of the peroxide content per ASTM E298; inhibitor (hydroquinone monomethyl ether, MEHQ) concentration 50 ± 15 ppm, measured by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with UV detection at 280 nm, the inhibitor being added by Arkema at the monomer production stage to prevent premature polymerization during storage and transport, with the concentration being a compromise between the need for adequate shelf-life stability — the inhibited monomer must remain unpolymerized for at least 6 months when stored at temperatures below 25°C, a requirement that the Arkema supply chain meets through the use of refrigerated transport and the Mojave facility's climate-controlled resin storage area, maintained at 15 ± 2°C — and the need to minimize the inhibitor's effect on the polymerization kinetics during AFP processing, because the inhibitor must be consumed by the initiator-derived free radicals before polymerization can proceed, effectively creating an induction period during which no polymerization occurs, an induction period that at the 50 ppm MEHQ concentration and the 175°C AFP nip point temperature is approximately 1.5 milliseconds — negligible relative to the 50-millisecond residence time and therefore not a significant factor in the AFP process, but that at higher MEHQ concentrations would become a concern by consuming a larger fraction of the initiator and reducing the ultimate monomer conversion, a trade-off that the current specification optimizes; and the rheological properties of the reactive mixture — specifically, the complex viscosity at 25°C and 1 Hz, which must be between 500 and 1,500 Pa·s, a range that is determined by the requirements of the tow prepregging process described in PART-086 (the lower bound ensuring that the mixture has sufficient viscosity to remain on the tow during prepregging and not drip from the tow before the AFP layup, the upper bound ensuring that the mixture can be pumped, metered, and applied by the prepregging equipment without excessive pressure drop or equipment wear) — each batch being sampled upon receipt, tested against these criteria, and either accepted into the Mojave facility's resin inventory (stored under the controlled temperature and humidity conditions described above), conditionally accepted with restrictions on its use (e.g., a batch with a slightly low dissolved polymer concentration, producing a lower-than-specified viscosity, might be accepted for use in non-structural components that do not require the full laminate quality, such as the interior partition walls or the cable tray supports, components whose failure would not compromise the pressure hull integrity and for which the reduced resin viscosity would not affect the component's fitness for its intended function), or rejected and returned to the supplier, with the rejection rate across the first 24 Elium batches received for the Atlantis program being 0 out of 24 — a perfect acceptance record that testifies to Arkema's quality control of the Elium production process, which is performed at Arkema's Lacq, France manufacturing facility under ISO 9001:2015 and ISO 14001:2015 certified quality and environmental management systems, and that provides the Mojave production team with the confidence that the Elium resin supply will not be a source of production delays or quality variability for the Atlantis demonstration program.

The Elium resin system, as characterized and specified in this document, provides the Atlantis program with a composite matrix that is uniquely suited to the demands of subsea habitation module fabrication: processable by the AFP method at economically viable production rates, curable to a high degree of monomer conversion within the short thermal cycle of the laser-heated layup, resistant to moisture-induced degradation of its mechanical properties over fifty years of continuous seawater immersion, and — through the reversibility inherent to the thermoplastic nature of the PMMA matrix — repairable in the event of localized damage, a capability that, while not intended to be exercised during the demonstration program (which plans for no damage to the deployed hulls), provides an operational option for the long-term deployment scenario in which a pod hull might sustain damage from a turbidite boulder impact that was not fully mitigated by the layered defense architecture described in Section 6 of the parent document, and in which a remotely-operated underwater repair — using a subsea version of the AFP laser heating system, deployed from a surface vessel and operated via the ROV's manipulator arm — could restore the hull's structural integrity without requiring the pod to be recovered to the surface, a capability that is unique to thermoplastic composites, that derives directly from the Elium chemistry, and that represents one of the principal justifications for the Atlantis program'S selection of the Elium system over the more conventional thermoset epoxy and vinyl ester alternatives, which have longer track records in marine applications but which lack the weldability and reparability that the thermoplastic Elium matrix provides — properties that, in the final analysis, are not merely manufacturing conveniences but survival features for a habitation module that must protect its occupants at the bottom of the sea for half a century, a duration that exceeds the typical service life of any actively-maintained marine structure and that demands a material system whose inherent properties, not merely the diligence of its fabrication, guarantee its continued integrity.
