# CSMFAB077-PART-086 — RESIN IMPREGNATION: TOW PREPREGGING, FLUORINATED SILANE COUPLING AGENT, MOISTURE ABSORPTION REDUCTION
## Block 8 — Fabrication Methods | Atlantis-Class Subsea Habitation Module Production
## Carrington Storm Motors — Safe Pod Engineering Division | Aegis C Material Architecture

---

The resin impregnation process — the step in which the dispersed nanodiamond-Elium resin mixture described in PART-085 is applied to the continuous basalt fiber tow described in PART-082, producing the pre-impregnated tow ("prepreg") that is wound onto spools and delivered to the AFP cell's creel cabinet as documented in PART-081 — occupies the critical interface position between the materials production chain (basalt fiber drawing, lonsdaleite synthesis, nanodiamond dispersion, Elium resin formulation) and the composite fabrication chain (automated fiber placement, in-process inspection, curing, post-cure, and quality assurance), and the quality of the prepreg — measured by the uniformity of the resin distribution along and across the tow, the degree of fiber wet-out (the extent to which the resin has penetrated the inter-filament spaces within the tow, coating every individual 9-micrometer basalt filament with a continuous layer of resin and eliminating any dry, resin-starved regions where filaments are in direct contact with each other without the intervening resin layer that transfers load between filaments and protects the filament surfaces from mechanical abrasion and environmental degradation), the resin content (the mass fraction of resin in the prepreg, which must be 35 ± 2 percent by weight to achieve the specified fiber volume fraction of 60 ± 2 percent in the cured laminate, the relationship between prepreg resin content and cured laminate fiber volume fraction being determined by the consolidation process — during the AFP layup, the compaction roller squeezes excess resin from the tow, reducing the resin content from the prepreg value to the cured laminate value, a reduction that must be precisely controlled, because too much resin in the prepreg results in a resin-rich cured laminate with a lower-than-specified fiber volume fraction, reducing the laminate's strength and stiffness, while too little resin results in a resin-starved laminate with dry fibers and voids that degrade the interlaminar bond strength and the fatigue life, a narrow process window that the impregnation process must hit with high consistency, as verified by the resin content measurement described below), and the chemical state of the fiber-matrix interface — the fluorinated silane coupling agent described in detail below must be chemically bonded to the basalt fiber surface (through the silanol condensation reaction described in PART-082, where the APTES silane was applied to the freshly-drawn fiber at 250°C, promoting the formation of covalent Si-O-Si bonds between the silane and the fiber) AND to the Elium resin matrix (through the reaction of the amine group on the APTES molecule with the methacrylate groups of the MMA monomer — a reaction that is not a covalent bond formation but a physical interaction, specifically hydrogen bonding between the amine hydrogens and the carbonyl oxygens of the methacrylate ester groups, which, while weaker than a covalent bond, is sufficient to provide the ~65 MPa interlaminar shear strength that the LBFRP-001 specification requires, and that has the advantage of being reversible — the hydrogen bonds can break and re-form, allowing the interface to accommodate the differential thermal expansion between the basalt fiber and the Elium matrix (their coefficients of thermal expansion differ by a factor of approximately 10, as documented in PART-083) without accumulating the irreversible damage — the broken covalent bonds — that a covalently-bonded interface would suffer under thermal cycling, a damage tolerance mechanism that the materials team refers to as "sacrificial hydrogen bonding" and that contributes to the exceptional fatigue resistance of the LBFRP-001 composite, as documented by the fatigue test data that shows a fatigue life of greater than 10⁷ cycles at 60 percent of the ultimate compressive strength — the design stress level for the Atlantis hull under the 4.5 MPa external pressure, with a factor of safety of 4.0, corresponding to a service stress of approximately 1.1 MPa (4.5 MPa / 4.0), which is less than 2 percent of the laminate's ultimate compressive strength of approximately 65 MPa, and therefore a stress level at which fatigue is not a concern for the Atlantis application, a comfortable margin that the fatigue test data supports, with no fatigue failures observed at stress levels below approximately 40 percent of the ultimate strength, confirming that the service stress of 1.7 percent of the ultimate strength is well within the infinite-life regime — in the event that a future Atlantis pod design, perhaps at a greater deployment depth (the Aegis C long-term deployment plan includes sites at depths up to 100 meters, where the hydrostatic pressure of 10 MPa would increase the service stress to approximately 3.8 percent of the ultimate strength, still within the infinite-life regime, but with a reduced margin, and the fatigue characterization of the Elium-silane-basalt interface would become correspondingly more important, a future need that the present characterization, while conservative for the Monterey and Osa sites, is designed to support) — to ensure that the fiber-matrix interface, and therefore the composite's load-bearing capacity, is preserved intact throughout the pod's fifty-year service life, a requirement that the fluorinated silane coupling agent described below is specifically designed to satisfy by reducing the moisture-induced degradation of the interface that is the dominant long-term failure mechanism for fiber-reinforced polymer composites in marine environments.

The impregnation process is performed on a custom-designed prepreg line, configured as a continuous, in-line process that receives the basalt fiber tow directly from the fiber production line's winding station (eliminating the need for intermediate spooling, storage, and unspooling of the dry fiber, each of which would introduce opportunities for fiber damage — abrasion against guide surfaces, contamination by airborne dust, moisture absorption by the hygroscopic basalt fiber surface — and would add process steps, cycle time, and cost, all of which the in-line configuration avoids, a process integration decision that the Mojave production engineering team made early in the facility design phase, based on the principle that every material handling step between fiber production and composite fabrication is an opportunity for quality degradation and should be eliminated if technically feasible, a principle that the team refers to as "the shortest path from melt to laminate" and that embodies the just-in-time, integrated manufacturing philosophy that the Aegis C production architecture is built upon) and processes it through the following sequence of operations: tow spreading (in which the as-drawn tow, consisting of 800 individual basalt filaments bundled together with a small amount of twist — approximately 1 turn per meter, introduced by the winding process to provide sufficient cohesion for handling without excessive twist that would prevent the filaments from spreading apart during impregnation, the twist being a compromise between handleability (higher twist = better cohesion, less tendency for the tow to splay apart during handling, but also less ability for the resin to penetrate the tow interior, because the twisted filaments are pressed together and the inter-filament spaces are constricted, impeding resin flow) and impregnatability (lower twist = better resin penetration but more difficult handling), with the 1 turn per meter representing the optimal balance determined by a designed experiment (DOE) that varied the twist from 0 to 5 turns per meter, measured the resulting prepreg resin distribution uniformity by the resin content measurement described below, and correlated the uniformity with the interlaminar shear strength of laminates fabricated from prepreg produced at each twist level, finding that the interlaminar shear strength decreased approximately linearly with increasing twist, from 68 MPa at 0 turns/m to 55 MPa at 5 turns/m, a 19 percent decrease over the twist range, and that the 1 turn/m level achieved a shear strength of 66 MPa — a 3 percent decrease from the zero-twist value, which was deemed an acceptable trade-off for the improved handleability that the 1 turn/m twist provides, and that the process specification documents accordingly), is pulled through a series of cylindrical spreader bars — polished stainless steel rods of progressively decreasing diameter (from 20 millimeters to 10 millimeters to 5 millimeters), arranged in a zigzag pattern that forces the tow to follow a serpentine path, the repeated bending over the spreader bars causing the filaments to slide relative to each other, overcoming the inter-filament friction and spreading the tow from its as-drawn width of approximately 2 millimeters to a flat ribbon of approximately 8 to 10 millimeters width, a spreading ratio of approximately 4:1 to 5:1 that increases the tow surface area accessible to the resin and reduces the maximum diffusion distance from the tow surface to the interior filaments — for a circular tow of 2 mm diameter, the center filament is approximately 1 mm from the surface, and for a resin with a viscosity of approximately 2,000 mPa·s, the time required for the resin to infiltrate from the surface to the center under capillary pressure alone is prohibitively long (hours, not the seconds available in the continuous impregnation process), whereas for a flat ribbon of 100 micrometers thickness (the thickness of the spread tow, corresponding to approximately 10 to 12 filaments through the thickness, each filament being 9 micrometers in diameter), the maximum diffusion distance is only 50 micrometers (half the ribbon thickness), and the resin infiltration time, calculated from the Darcy's law model for flow through a porous medium — t ≈ (φ × η × L²) / (2 × k × ΔP), where φ ≈ 0.4 is the porosity of the spread tow (the volume fraction of the inter-filament space, approximately 0.4 for a tow with a fiber volume fraction of 60 percent, the remaining 40 percent being the space that the resin must fill), η ≈ 2.0 Pa·s is the resin viscosity, L = 50 × 10⁻⁶ m is the infiltration distance, k ≈ 10⁻¹⁴ m² is the permeability of the fiber bed (estimated from the Blake-Kozeny equation for flow through a packed bed of cylindrical particles, k = (d_f² × φ³) / (150 × (1 − φ)²), where d_f = 9 × 10⁻⁶ m is the filament diameter, giving k ≈ (81 × 10⁻¹² × 0.4³) / (150 × 0.6²) ≈ 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁴ m², a permeability that is consistent with the experimentally-measured values for carbon and glass fiber tows with similar filament diameters and fiber volume fractions), and ΔP ≈ 10⁵ Pa is the pressure differential driving the infiltration — the atmospheric pressure plus the pressure generated by the impregnation die described below, approximately 0.1 MPa above atmospheric — is approximately t ≈ (0.4 × 2.0 × (50 × 10⁻⁶)²) / (2 × 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁴ × 10⁵) ≈ (0.4 × 2.0 × 2,500 × 10⁻¹²) / (2.4 × 10⁻⁹) ≈ (2,000 × 10⁻¹²) / (2.4 × 10⁻⁹) ≈ 0.83 seconds, a dwell time that is readily achievable in the continuous impregnation process, which operates at a tow speed of approximately 0.5 meters per second (the maximum speed at which the spread tow can be drawn through the impregnation die without excessive tension that would break the filaments, a limit that the process development team determined empirically by operating the prepreg line at progressively higher speeds and monitoring the filament breakage rate via an optical filament counter — a laser-based system that detects the interruption of a laser beam by a broken filament end as it passes through the sensor zone, counting the number of breaks per unit length of tow, with the breakage rate increasing from approximately 0.01 breaks per meter at 0.5 m/s to approximately 0.5 breaks per meter at 1.0 m/s, a 50-fold increase that reflects the increasing tension — approximately proportional to the square of the speed, per the viscous drag model of a tow moving through a liquid-filled die — and that the process specification limits to the 0.5 m/s maximum, at which the breakage rate is negligible and the productivity is approximately 36 kilograms of prepreg per hour — 0.5 m/s × 140 tex (the linear density of the tow in grams per 1,000 meters) × 3,600 s/hr / 1,000 g/kg × (1 / 0.65) (the factor accounting for the resin mass, which is 35 percent of the total prepreg mass, such that the fiber mass flow rate of 0.5 × 140 × 3,600 / 1,000,000 = 0.252 kg/hr of fiber corresponds to a prepreg mass flow rate of 0.252 / 0.65 = 0.388 kg/hr per tow, and with 8 tows being impregnated in parallel on the prepreg line — one impregnation die per tow, arranged side-by-side, each fed from its own tow supply and producing its own prepreg tow, which are then wound onto individual spools — the total prepreg production rate is approximately 8 × 0.388 = 3.1 kg/hr, a rate that is approximately 3.1 percent of the AFP cell's tow placement rate of 100 kg/hr (as documented in PART-081), meaning that the prepreg line must operate for approximately 32 hours to produce the prepreg required for 1 hour of AFP operation — a seemingly large imbalance that reflects the fact that the AFP cell places 8 tows simultaneously at a speed of 1.2 m/s, consuming prepreg at a rate that is approximately 32 times faster than the prepreg line can produce it, and that therefore requires the prepreg line to operate continuously, ahead of the AFP schedule, to build an inventory of prepreg spools that the AFP cell then consumes at its higher rate, an inventory that, for the Atlantis demonstration program, is stored in the climate-controlled creel cabinet described in PART-081, within the 48-hour maximum storage time specified in PART-085, a just-in-time buffer that decouples the prepreg production rate from the AFP consumption rate while ensuring that the prepreg is consumed before the nanodiamond dispersion quality degrades — a production scheduling optimization that the operations research group at the Mojave facility solved using a discrete-event simulation model of the prepreg line and the AFP cell, with the model predicting that an inventory buffer of 8 to 12 hours of AFP prepreg consumption (corresponding to 800 to 1,200 kg of prepreg, or approximately 260 to 390 spools, each containing 8,200 meters of tow and weighing approximately 3.1 kg — the prepreg spool mass being larger than the dry fiber spool mass because of the 35 percent resin content, as documented in PART-082) is sufficient to maintain uninterrupted AFP operation despite the prepreg line's lower production rate, and that this buffer can be maintained by operating the prepreg line for two 8-hour shifts per day, five days per week, with the third shift reserved for equipment maintenance and process troubleshooting, a production schedule that the Mojave facility has implemented and that has successfully supported the Atlantis hull production to date.

The impregnation die — the component in which the spread tow contacts the liquid nanodiamond-Elium resin mixture and in which the resin infiltrates the inter-filament spaces — is the core of the prepreg line and the component whose design most directly determines the quality of the resulting prepreg. The die is a heated, pressurized chamber machined from stainless steel (316L grade, selected for its corrosion resistance to the Elium resin mixture, which, while not particularly corrosive to metals at room temperature, contains the lauroyl peroxide initiator, a mild oxidizing agent, and the residual sulfuric and nitric acids from the nanodiamond purification process (the acids having been washed to below the detection limit as documented in PART-084, but with the possibility of a trace residue that, over months of continuous contact, could cause pitting corrosion of a less-resistant stainless steel grade such as 304, which is susceptible to chloride-induced stress corrosion cracking — a risk that the Mojave facility's water chemistry, which is not chlorinated but which does contain dissolved salts from the aquifer that supplies the facility's process water, would exacerbate, and that the 316L grade's molybdenum content, approximately 2 to 3 percent by weight, mitigates through the well-known effect of molybdenum in increasing the pitting resistance of stainless steels, as measured by the pitting resistance equivalent number, PREN = %Cr + 3.3 × %Mo + 16 × %N, which for 316L is approximately 24 to 26, compared to approximately 18 to 20 for 304, a difference that, while modest, is sufficient to provide reliable corrosion resistance in the mildly oxidizing, ambient-temperature environment of the prepreg impregration die, as confirmed by the absence of any corrosion — no pitting, no crevice corrosion at the die joints, no stress corrosion cracking — after 5,000 hours of continuous operation, a service life that exceeds the expected operating time for the Atlantis demonstration program's prepreg production (approximately 3,600 hours, corresponding to 18 months of two-shift operation), providing confidence that the dies will not require replacement during the program), the interior surfaces polished to a mirror finish (Ra < 0.1 micrometers, the arithmetic average roughness, achieved by progressive polishing with diamond pastes down to 0.25-micrometer grit size, a surface finish that minimizes the frictional drag of the tow as it slides through the die and that prevents the accumulation of resin deposits — the Elium resin, being a reactive mixture that slowly polymerizes even at room temperature (albeit at the negligible rate discussed in PART-085, with a pot life of 48 hours), can form a thin, adherent polymer film on the die surfaces if the surface roughness provides nucleation sites for the polymerization, a fouling mechanism that the mirror-polished finish reduces by eliminating the surface irregularities that would otherwise anchor the polymer film, and that the process specification maintains by requiring the dies to be disassembled, cleaned, and re-polished every 500 hours of operation — a preventive maintenance interval that the production schedule accommodates during the third-shift maintenance window and that has prevented any die fouling or prepreg quality degradation attributable to die surface condition throughout the Atlantis production history) and comprising three sections: the entrance section, where the spread tow enters the die and is pre-wetted by a curtain of resin that flows across the tow's path — the "resin waterfall" — a continuous stream of the nanodiamond-Elium mixture, pumped from the holding tank described in PART-085 at a flow rate of approximately 100 milliliters per minute (controlled by a gear pump with a flow meter feedback loop, ensuring a constant, metered resin supply to the die, independent of the tow speed or the resin viscosity, which varies with the ambient temperature and the age of the resin batch), distributed across the full width of the spread tow by a slot die — a narrow slit, 12 millimeters wide and 0.5 millimeters high, machined into the top plate of the entrance section, through which the resin flows under the pressure generated by the gear pump, forming a continuous, uniform film that coats the upper surface of the tow as it passes beneath the slot, the resin flow rate of 100 mL/min being calculated to provide a resin coating on the tow surface that, at the 0.5 m/s tow speed, corresponds to an average resin film thickness of approximately (100 × 10⁻⁶ m³/min) / (0.5 m/s × 60 s/min × 0.01 m tow width) ≈ 333 × 10⁻⁶ m (333 micrometers), a thickness that is far greater than the 100-micrometer tow thickness, ensuring that the tow is completely immersed in resin and that every filament has access to the resin, the excess resin being collected at the die exit, filtered (through a 50-micrometer stainless steel mesh to remove any fiber fragments or other particulate that might have been generated during the tow's passage through the die, particulate that, if recirculated, could clog the slot die or contaminate the prepreg), and returned to the holding tank, a recirculation loop that minimizes the resin waste — approximately 95 percent of the resin that enters the die is recirculated, with only the approximately 5 percent that is actually incorporated into the prepreg being consumed, an efficiency that the process engineering team considers acceptable, and that could be improved through the optimization of the resin flow rate and the die geometry (a narrower slot die, or a roller-type applicator that meters the resin onto the tow more precisely, would reduce the excess resin and increase the utilization efficiency), but that the current design, with its generous excess resin, provides a process margin that ensures consistent, complete fiber wet-out even if the tow spreading is slightly non-uniform or if the resin viscosity varies from batch to batch, a margin that the team values more highly than a few percentage points of resin utilization efficiency, given the criticality of the impregnation quality to the final laminate performance; the impregnation section, where the tow, now fully immersed in resin, passes through a series of four heated, polished stainless steel pins — 10 millimeters diameter, arranged in a zigzag pattern with a separation of 50 millimeters between adjacent pins, heated to 80 ± 2°C by internal electric cartridge heaters controlled by PID temperature controllers with feedback from thermocouples embedded in the pins at the mid-point of the tow contact zone, the heating serving to reduce the resin viscosity at the tow surface from approximately 2,000 mPa·s at 25°C to approximately 200 mPa·s at 80°C (the temperature-viscosity relationship for the Elium resin mixture having been characterized by rheometry as described in PART-083, and following an Arrhenius dependence, η(T) = η₀ × exp(Ea/RT), with an apparent activation energy Ea ≈ 35 kJ/mol, such that the temperature increase from 25°C (298 K) to 80°C (353 K) reduces the viscosity by a factor of exp[(35,000 J/mol / 8.314 J/(mol·K)) × (1/353 K − 1/298 K)] ≈ exp[4,210 × (−0.00052)] ≈ exp(−2.19) ≈ 0.112, or an 89 percent reduction — from 2,000 mPa·s to approximately 224 mPa·s, consistent with the 200 mPa·s cited above), a viscosity reduction that dramatically accelerates the resin infiltration into the tow interior, reducing the infiltration time by the same factor of approximately 9 according to Darcy's law (t ∝ η), such that the residence time in the impregnation section — approximately 0.5 seconds, corresponding to the 0.5 m/s tow speed and the approximately 250-millimeter path length through the four pins — is comfortably sufficient for complete infiltration, a conclusion that the process validation data confirms through cross-sectional microscopy of prepreg samples, which show no dry filament bundles or resin-starved regions when the pins are heated to 80°C, but which show occasional dry spots when the pin heaters are turned off, confirming the necessity and effectiveness of the heated impregnation section, and the repeated bending of the tow over the pins, in addition to guiding the tow through the die, serves to mechanically work the resin into the tow — the bending causes the filaments on the outer radius of each pin to slide relative to each other, opening and closing the inter-filament spaces in a cyclic "pumping" action that forces the resin to flow through the tow thickness, a mechanism that is analogous to the "squeegee" effect in hand lay-up of composite laminates, where the roller forces resin through the fiber bed by locally compressing and releasing the fibers, and that the process engineering team has found to be essential for achieving consistent impregnation of the 800-filament tow — without the pin-induced fiber movement, the resin would infiltrate the tow by capillary action and pressure-driven flow alone, which would be slower and less uniform, particularly in the interior of the tow where the resin must travel the greatest distance and where the fibers, if not mechanically agitated, tend to remain in their as-spread configuration, creating a gradient in resin content from the tow surface (resin-rich) to the tow interior (resin-poor), a gradient that the pin-induced fiber movement eliminates by continuously redistributing the fibers and exposing new surfaces to the resin, ensuring that every filament, regardless of its initial position within the tow, spends a portion of its residence time in the die at or near the tow surface, where the resin is most abundant and the infiltration is most rapid; and the exit section, where the impregnated tow is drawn through a calibrated metering orifice — a precision-ground slot, 10 millimeters wide (slightly wider than the spread tow width to prevent edge damage) and 0.15 millimeters high, machined into a replaceable tungsten carbide insert (the extreme hardness of tungsten carbide, approximately 2,200 HV (Vickers hardness), being necessary to resist the abrasive wear from the basalt fiber, which, although not as abrasive as glass fiber — glass fibers are notoriously abrasive because of their high hardness and their tendency to fracture into sharp fragments that score the guide surfaces — is still sufficiently abrasive, particularly if a filament breaks and the broken end, which is sharp, drags along the die surface, that a hardened steel die would wear measurably within a few hundred hours of operation, changing the metering orifice dimensions and therefore the resin content of the prepreg, a drift that would violate the ±2 percent resin content tolerance and that the tungsten carbide insert, with its far greater wear resistance, prevents, the insert being replaced only after approximately 5,000 hours of operation, at which point the orifice width has increased by approximately 0.005 millimeters due to wear, corresponding to a resin content increase of approximately 0.5 percent — still within the ±2 percent tolerance, but with a trend toward the upper tolerance limit that motivates the scheduled replacement, a preventive maintenance philosophy that, once again, aims to replace the component before it causes a quality deviation rather than waiting for the deviation to occur and then reacting, a proactive approach that the SPC system monitors by tracking the prepreg resin content as a function of the die operating hours, with the control chart showing a gradual upward trend that is detected by the Western Electric rules (specifically, the rule of eight consecutive points on one side of the center line) well before the resin content approaches the specification limit, triggering the die replacement at a point when the resin content is still comfortably within the specification, a leading-indicator maintenance strategy that minimizes the risk of producing out-of-specification prepreg) — that scrapes the excess resin from the tow surfaces and controls the final prepreg resin content to the target of 35 ± 2 percent by weight, the orifice height being the critical dimension that determines the amount of resin retained on the tow after the scraping action, and having been determined through a calibration experiment in which orifices of various heights (from 0.10 mm to 0.25 mm in 0.01 mm increments) were tested, the resulting prepreg resin content being measured by the burn-off method described below, and the data being fitted to a linear regression, resin content (%) = 15.2 + 132 × (orifice height in mm), with an R² of 0.97, indicating that the orifice height is a strong, linear predictor of the resin content and that the 0.15 mm orifice height produces a resin content of 15.2 + 132 × 0.15 = 35.0 percent, exactly at the target, a calibration that is re-verified after each orifice insert replacement by producing a short length of prepreg, measuring its resin content, and adjusting the orifice height (by shimming the insert up or down, changing the effective gap by a few micrometers) if the measured resin content deviates from the target by more than 1 percent — a fine-tuning capability that the adjustable orifice design provides and that the fixed-orifice designs of many commercial prepreg lines lack, a flexibility that the Mojave team incorporated into the die design at the cost of additional mechanical complexity but with the benefit of precise, responsive control over the prepreg resin content, a control that is essential for the consistent laminate quality that the Atlantis program demands.

The fluorinated silane coupling agent represents the Atlantis program's principal chemical defense against the moisture-induced degradation of the fiber-matrix interface that is the Achilles' heel of fiber-reinforced polymer composites in marine environments. While the baseline APTES silane applied during fiber drawing (PART-082) provides the covalent bond between the basalt fiber and the Elium matrix that is the basis of the composite's interlaminar shear strength, the APTES molecule, being a small, hydrophilic organic compound (the amine group, -NH₂, is polar and can hydrogen-bond with water molecules, attracting moisture to the interface), does not, by itself, provide a barrier against the ingress of water molecules to the fiber surface — water molecules, being small (approximately 0.275 nanometers in diameter, comparable to the inter-chain spacing in the PMMA matrix) and highly mobile (the diffusion coefficient of water in PMMA being approximately 8.5 × 10⁻⁹ cm²/s at 15°C, as documented in PART-083), can diffuse through the Elium matrix and reach the fiber surface, where they hydrolyze the siloxane (Si-O-Si) bonds that attach the silane to the fiber, a hydrolysis reaction that is chemically reversible (Si-O-Si + H₂O ⇌ Si-OH + HO-Si) but that, over time, progressively detaches the silane molecules from the fiber surface, reducing the effective silane coverage and therefore the fiber-matrix adhesion, a slow degradation that the accelerated aging tests described in PART-082 and PART-083 have characterized and that the fluorinated silane is designed to slow by a factor of approximately 3 to 5 — extending the time for the fiber-matrix adhesion to degrade to the critical level (below which the composite's interlaminar shear strength falls below the design minimum of 65 MPa, at which point the laminate can no longer sustain the design loads without risk of delamination) from approximately 75 years (for the unmodified APTES silane, as estimated by the Arrhenius extrapolation of the accelerated aging data) to approximately 225 to 375 years — far beyond the pod's fifty-year design life and therefore providing a comfortable margin of safety against interface degradation, a margin that is not strictly necessary (the pod is designed for 50 years, not 375) but that the Aegis C safety philosophy values, because it provides robustness against the uncertainties inherent in the Arrhenius extrapolation — the extrapolation from 60°C (the accelerated aging temperature) to 15°C (the service temperature) involves an acceleration factor of approximately 100 to 200 (depending on the activation energy, which itself has an uncertainty of approximately ±5 kJ/mol, translating to an uncertainty in the acceleration factor of approximately ±50 percent), and the extrapolated lifetime must therefore be interpreted as an estimate with a wide confidence interval, not as a precise prediction, a statistical reality that the Aegis C safety philosophy addresses not by demanding greater precision in the lifetime prediction (which is fundamentally limited by the variability of the material properties and the testing conditions) but by building in generous margins — the factor of 4.5 to 7.5 between the 50-year design life and the predicted 225 to 375-year interface degradation time provides confidence that, even if the Arrhenius extrapolation overestimates the acceleration factor by a factor of 2 (i.e., if the actual activation energy for the interface hydrolysis reaction is lower than the measured value, such that the reaction proceeds faster than predicted at the service temperature), the interface will still survive the fifty-year design life, a margin that is prudent, cost-effective (the fluorinated silane adds approximately $120 to the material cost of each pod hull — a trivial fraction of the $2.24 million total fabrication cost per pod as documented in PART-094), and consistent with the program's overall safety philosophy of designing for the worst credible case, not the most likely one.

The fluorinated silane — specifically, (tridecafluoro-1,1,2,2-tetrahydrooctyl)triethoxysilane, a molecule consisting of a perfluorinated octyl chain (C₈F₁₃, the fluorinated carbon "tail" that provides the hydrophobicity) attached to a silicon atom via a short hydrocarbon linker (-CH₂CH₂-, the "spacer" that separates the fluorinated tail from the silane group, preventing the fluorinated chain from interfering with the silane condensation reaction on the fiber surface), with three ethoxy (-OCH₂CH₃) groups also bonded to the silicon atom (the "head" groups that hydrolyze to silanol (-Si-OH) and then condense with the hydroxyl groups on the basalt fiber surface, chemically bonding the fluorinated silane to the fiber, the same reaction mechanism as for the APTES silane, and one that is compatible with the APTES chemistry — the fluorinated silane does not displace the APTES but co-deposits with it, the two silanes occupying adjacent sites on the fiber surface and forming a mixed monolayer in which the APTES provides the adhesion to the Elium matrix and the fluorinated silane provides the hydrophobic barrier that protects the APTES from hydrolytic attack, a cooperative chemical system that the materials team refers to as a "molecular shield" and that the XPS analysis of the fiber surface confirms — the XPS spectrum shows the characteristic fluorine 1s peak at a binding energy of approximately 689 eV, the nitrogen 1s peak from the APTES amine at 399.5 eV, and the silicon 2p peak from both silanes at approximately 102 eV, the relative intensities of the fluorine and nitrogen peaks corresponding to the 0.5 weight percent fluorinated silane and 1.0 weight percent APTES loadings, respectively) — is applied as a co-treatment with the APTES during the fiber drawing process, not as a separate post-treatment, because the silanol condensation reaction that bonds both silanes to the fiber surface is most efficient at the elevated fiber temperature (approximately 250°C) immediately after fiber drawing, and a separate, room-temperature post-treatment would require either a thermal activation step (which would add process complexity, cost, and the risk of fiber damage from the additional handling and heating) or a longer reaction time (which would be incompatible with the continuous, high-speed fiber production process), neither of which is as efficient or effective as the co-deposition approach that the Mojave team has implemented, in which an aqueous solution containing both the APTES (1.0 weight percent) and the fluorinated silane (0.5 weight percent, the 0.5 weight percent being determined by the same designed experiment (DOE) methodology that was used to optimize the nanodiamond loading — the fluorinated silane concentration was varied from 0 to 1.0 weight percent in 0.1-weight-percent increments, and the resulting basalt fibers were embedded in Elium resin, cured into composite coupons, and subjected to the accelerated moisture aging protocol described in PART-082 (synthetic seawater at 60°C for 5,000 hours), with the interlaminar shear strength measured before and after aging, the retention of shear strength (the ratio of the aged strength to the unaged strength, expressed as a percentage) being the response variable, and the data showing that the retention increased from approximately 82 percent for the unmodified APTES alone (0 percent fluorinated silane) to approximately 92 percent at 0.5 weight percent fluorinated silane, with little further improvement at higher concentrations — the retention reaching a plateau of approximately 93 percent at 1.0 weight percent — indicating that 0.5 weight percent is the optimal loading, providing most of the hydrophobic protection benefit with minimal use The resin content of the prepreg is verified by the burn-off method, a simple, robust gravimetric technique in which a sample of the prepreg tow — approximately 10 grams, cut from the spool at the beginning, middle, and end of each spool's winding (to detect any systematic variation in resin content along the tow length, which could indicate a problem with the impregnation die, such as a die atmospheric oxygen, leaving only the incombustible basalt fiber and lonsdaleite nanodiamond, both of which are stable at 600°C — the basalt fiber, being a silicate glass, does not oxidize, and the lonsdaleite, being diamond, would oxidize in air at temperatures above approximately 600°C, but the 2-hour dwell at 600°C, while sufficient to oxidize the amorphous and graphitic carbon that may be present as contaminants, does not significantly oxidize the diamond because the oxidation rate of diamond, even in air, is negligible at 600°C — the oxidation of diamond in air begins to be measurable
