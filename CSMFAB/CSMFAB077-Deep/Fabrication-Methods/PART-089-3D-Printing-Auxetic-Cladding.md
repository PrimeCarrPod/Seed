# CSMFAB077-PART-089 — 3D PRINTING FOR AUXETIC CLADDING: TPU FILAMENT, RE-ENTRANT HONEYCOMB TOOLPATH, BUILD VOLUME
## Block 8 — Fabrication Methods | Atlantis-Class Subsea Habitation Module Production
## Carrington Storm Motors — Safe Pod Engineering Division | Aegis C Material Architecture

---

The auxetic metamaterial cladding that constitutes the second layer of the Atlantis pod's turbidite defense architecture — the 150-millimeter-thick sacrificial panel that absorbs boulder impact energy through its negative Poisson's ratio behavior, drawing material inward toward the impact point rather than extruding it outward as a conventional material would, a counter-intuitive mechanical response that increases the effective density at the impact zone by a factor of approximately 3.5 and the specific energy absorption by a factor of approximately 4.2 compared to a solid thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) panel of equivalent thickness, as documented in Section 6 of the parent document CSMFAB000000000077 — is fabricated using a large-format fused filament fabrication (FFF) 3D printing process that deposits TPU filament in a precisely-controlled re-entrant honeycomb toolpath, a toolpath that generates the negative Poisson's ratio geometry — a periodic array of inward-pointing "bow-tie" cells, in which each cell consists of four inclined struts meeting at a central node, the struts being angled inward (toward each other) rather than outward as in a conventional hexagonal honeycomb, such that when the structure is compressed in one direction, the inclined struts rotate and the cell collapses inward in the orthogonal direction as well, producing a simultaneous contraction in both directions — a macroscopic manifestation of the negative Poisson's ratio that, at the mesoscale of the individual cell (approximately 25 millimeters cell width, 50 millimeters cell length, with strut thicknesses of 3 millimeters and strut inclination angles of 20 degrees from the normal, giving a Poisson's ratio of approximately -0.4, as verified by the digital image correlation (DIC) measurements during the compressive testing of the printed panels, in which a speckle pattern was applied to the panel surface, high-resolution cameras tracked the displacement of the speckles during the compression test, and the DIC software (Correlated Solutions Vic-3D) calculated the strain field from the displacement data, confirming the negative Poisson's ratio and the auxetic deformation pattern that is the basis of the impact energy absorption mechanism), and that requires the FFF printer to deposit the TPU filament along complex, three-dimensional paths that cannot be generated by the simple, layer-by-layer slicing algorithms that are standard in consumer and most industrial 3D printing software, but that instead require a custom toolpath generation algorithm — developed by the Carrington Storm Motors additive manufacturing team in collaboration with the computational geometry group at the California Institute of Technology, under a research contract funded by the Aegis C program's advanced manufacturing development budget — that models the re-entrant honeycomb geometry as a conformal lattice mapped onto the curved surface of the Atlantis pod's teardrop hull, with the lattice's axes aligned to the hull's principal curvature directions to ensure that the auxetic behavior is oriented correctly — the negative Poisson's ratio direction being normal to the hull surface, so that the boulder impact, which is directed approximately normal to the hull (the turbidite boulders approach from the flow direction, which is approximately parallel to the hull's longitudinal axis as described in the CFD analysis of Section 6, and the teardrop geometry deflects the flow around the hull, such that boulders that do impact the hull do so at a glancing angle that is approximately normal to the local hull surface — the impact being approximately perpendicular to the surface, which is the optimal orientation for the auxetic cladding, because the negative Poisson's ratio effect is most pronounced when the impact is normal to the auxetic structure's plane, the inclined struts rotating inward and densifying the material directly beneath the impact point, absorbing the majority of the impact energy through the plastic deformation of the TPU struts — an energy absorption mechanism that would be less effective if the impact were at an oblique angle, because the struts would experience a combination of compression and shear, and the auxetic collapse would be less complete, a directionality that the lattice orientation alignment is specifically designed to optimize, based on the CFD-predicted boulder impact angle distribution, which shows that approximately 85 percent of impacts are within ±30 degrees of the local surface normal, a distribution that the auxetic cladding's orientation sensitivity accommodates, with the energy absorption remaining above 80 percent of the maximum for impact angles up to 30 degrees from the normal, as confirmed by the drop-weight impact testing of inclined cladding specimens, a testing program that validated the lattice orientation strategy and informed the design of the attachment system — the frangible fasteners described in Section 6 — that must transmit the impact load from the cladding panel to the hull while accommodating the angular variability of the impacts, a multibody dynamic simulation of the panel-fastener-hull system (using MSC Adams, a commercial multibody dynamics code, with the TPU material model calibrated from the quasi-static and dynamic (Instron VHS 160/100-20 high-rate servohydraulic test machine, strain rates of 10⁻⁴ to 10³ s⁻¹, covering the quasi-static to impact-relevant range) compression test data and represented as a hyperelastic-viscoplastic constitutive model — the Bergström-Boyce model, which captures the rate-dependent, large-strain behavior of thermoplastic elastomers, including the Mullins effect (the stress-softening that occurs during the first loading cycle, as the polymer chains are stretched beyond their initial equilibrium configuration and do not fully recover, a phenomenon that is particularly pronounced in the 3D-printed TPU because the layer-by-layer deposition process introduces a preferential orientation of the polymer chains along the print direction, an anisotropy that the Bergström-Boyce model accounts for through its tensorial representation of the polymer network and that the cladding design exploits by orienting the print direction parallel to the expected impact direction, aligning the strongest direction of the TPU with the direction of the largest impact stress, a bio-inspired design principle that the Dolittle register identifies as analogous to the orientation of collagen fibers in bone — the fibers are aligned along the principal stress trajectories, maximizing the bone's strength in the directions where it is most needed, a principle that the auxetic cladding adopts, with the FFF toolpath being generated to orient the printed "roads" (the individual extruded filaments, approximately 0.8 millimeters wide and 0.3 millimeters tall, which bond to each other during the printing process to form the continuous TPU structure) along the axes of the re-entrant honeycomb struts, such that each strut consists of several parallel printed roads running along the strut's length, providing the maximum tensile strength along the strut axis — approximately 45 MPa ultimate tensile strength, measured per ASTM D638 on printed TPU dog-bone specimens with the roads oriented parallel to the loading direction, compared to approximately 25 MPa for specimens with the roads oriented perpendicular to the loading direction, a factor of 1.8 anisotropy that the toolpath design exploits — and that confirmed the adequacy of the frangible fastener design, the fasteners shearing at the predetermined load (approximately 15 kN per fastener, corresponding to an impact force that exceeds the cladding panel's energy absorption capacity and that would, if transmitted to the hull, risk damaging the underlying composite laminate, a damage that the fastener shear is specifically designed to prevent, sacrificing the cladding panel — a replaceable component — to protect the hull — the irreplaceable pressure boundary, a design trade-off that the Kiddo register's risk analysis evaluated and approved, based on the estimated repair cost of approximately $12,000 per panel (materials + ROV labor + vessel time) versus the replacement cost of the hull — effectively infinite, because the hull cannot be replaced once deployed, a cost asymmetry that makes the sacrificial panel design overwhelmingly favorable from a risk-management perspective).

The FFF printer is a custom-built, large-format machine — designated the "Mojave Mega-Printer" (MMP) — with a build volume of 4.0 meters (X) × 2.0 meters (Y) × 1.5 meters (Z), a volume that is sufficient to print the largest single cladding panel — the forward end cap panel, which covers the curved nose of the teardrop hull and which measures approximately 3.5 meters along its longest dimension, 1.8 meters wide, and 0.15 meters thick (the 150-millimeter cladding thickness), requiring a build volume that is larger than any commercially-available FFF printer (the largest commercially available FFF printers — the BigRep PRO, the Modix BIG-180X, the 3D Platform 400 Series — have build volumes of approximately 1.0 to 1.5 meters in the largest dimension, far smaller than the Atlantis cladding panels require), a capability gap that motivated the development of the MMP in-house, a development that leveraged the Mojave facility's extensive experience with large-format CNC machine design (the AFP gantry, described in PART-081, provided the structural and motion-control architecture that the MMP adapted for the FFF application, the similarities between the two machines — a gantry structure, linear motor-driven axes, a deposition head that moves in multiple degrees of freedom over a stationary workpiece — being sufficient that the MMP's design was essentially a derivative of the AFP machine, with the fiber placement head replaced by a thermoplastic extrusion head, a design approach that reduced the MMP's development time from an estimated 24 months to approximately 9 months, and its development cost from an estimated $3.2 million to approximately $1.4 million, a saving that ZIRCONIA welcomed but that the engineering team valued more for the schedule acceleration than for the cost reduction, because the auxetic cladding was on the critical path for the first pod hull — the hull could be fabricated, post-cured, and inspected, but it could not be deployed without its cladding, and any delay in the cladding fabrication would delay the deployment, potentially pushing the Monterey demonstration past the seasonal weather window (April through October, as described in PART-107), a delay that would cost the program an additional six months and approximately $4.8 million in standing-army costs (the salaries and overhead of the Mojave production team, the Charlemagne fleet crew, and the deployment contractors, who would be idle during the delay), a cost that the MMP's accelerated development avoided, and that the team considers one of the most impactful schedule-risk mitigation actions taken during the Atlantis program, a mitigation that was possible only because the program's integrated design and manufacturing team — the engineers who designed the Atlantis pod also designed the machines that build it, a vertical integration that is unusual in the composite manufacturing industry, where the design and the manufacturing are typically performed by separate organizations, with the resulting inefficiencies of communication, coordination, and schedule alignment that the Atlantis program's integrated approach avoids, a competitive advantage that the Aegis C program explicitly cultivates through its policy of maintaining all critical fabrication capabilities in-house, at the Mojave facility, under the direct control of the program management, a policy that increases the program's fixed costs (the Mojave facility's overhead is approximately $512,000 per pod as documented in PART-094, a significant cost that the integrated approach incurs) but that the program considers justified by the schedule certainty, the quality control, and the intellectual property protection that the in-house manufacturing provides, a strategic calculus that ZIRCONIA has validated through a comparison of the Atlantis program's cost and schedule performance against the industry benchmarks for aerospace composite manufacturing programs, a comparison that shows the Atlantis program to be approximately 40 percent faster and approximately 25 percent less expensive per unit mass of composite structure than the industry average, a performance advantage that ZIRCONIA attributes largely to the integrated design-manufacturing approach and to the elimination of the contractual interfaces, the profit margins, and the communication delays that plague the traditional prime contractor-subcontractor model, a model that the Atlantis program deliberately rejects in favor of the integrated approach, an approach that is, in ZIRCONIA's assessment, a key enabler of the program's technical and economic success). The extrusion head on the MMP is a custom-designed, liquid-cooled hot end capable of processing TPU filament at rates up to 5 kilograms per hour — approximately 10 times the deposition rate of a typical desktop FFF printer (approximately 0.5 kg/hr), a rate that is necessary because the largest cladding panel requires approximately 450 kilograms of TPU (the panel's volume, approximately 3.5 m × 1.8 m × 0.15 m = 0.945 m³, multiplied by the auxetic lattice's relative density — the fraction of the panel's volume that is occupied by the TPU material, as opposed to the voids within the re-entrant honeycomb cells — which is approximately 0.45 (i.e., 55 percent void fraction, 45 percent solid fraction, a relatively dense lattice that reflects the high energy absorption requirement — a less dense lattice would absorb less energy because there would be less material to deform plastically, and the lattice's density is a design parameter that the impact analysis optimized, trading off the energy absorption (which increases with density) against the panel's mass (which increases with density) and the printer's build time (which increases with density), the optimal density of 0.45 being the result of this multi-objective optimization), giving a solid TPU mass of approximately 450 kg (0.945 m³ × 1.2 g/cm³ (the density of TPU) × 0.45 = 510 kg, approximately 450 kg after accounting for the non-lattice borders and attachment features that are solid rather than lattice and that consume additional material), a mass that, at the 5 kg/hr deposition rate, requires approximately 90 hours of continuous printing — nearly 4 days — for the largest panel, a build time that the production schedule accommodates by printing the panels in parallel with the AFP layup of the hull, such that the panels are complete and ready for installation — the attachment to the hull via the frangible fasteners, a process that is performed after the hull is post-cured and before the pod is transported to the deployment site, as described in the Block 9 deployment documents — when the hull emerges from the post-cure oven, a just-in-time production sequence that the integrated scheduling system (an enterprise resource planning (ERP) system, SAP S/4HANA, customized for the Atlantis program's specific production workflow) coordinates, ensuring that the cladding panels, the MXene coating, the ALON viewports, the life support equipment, the Grid-Seed power modules, the S-Bus beacons, and all the other pod components are available when the hull is ready to receive them, with no component waiting for the hull and no hull waiting for a component, a synchronized production flow that the integrated facility design and the ERP system enable and that the production team's daily stand-up meetings monitor, adjusting the schedule as necessary to accommodate the inevitable minor disruptions — a machine breakdown, a material delivery delay, a quality hold — that any manufacturing operation experiences, disruptions that the integrated approach manages through the flexibility that the in-house control of all production resources provides, a flexibility that a distributed, subcontractor-based supply chain would lack, and that has enabled the Atlantis program to maintain its production schedule with zero critical-path delays attributable to supply chain disruptions, a record that the logistics team, whose Block 9 documents will describe the equally-critical deployment logistics, considers a model for the management of complex, integrated manufacturing operations, and that the Aegis C long-term deployment plan — the 1,000-pod-per-year production rate described in PART-095 — will require to be replicated at the expanded Mojave facility and at the additional production sites that the scalability analysis identifies as necessary.

The TPU filament — the feedstock for the MMP — is a custom-formulated thermoplastic polyurethane, BASF Ultramid TPU 95A, selected for its combination of high elasticity (the elongation at break exceeds 500 percent, providing the large strains that the auxetic lattice undergoes during impact without fracturing — the lattice is designed to absorb energy through plastic deformation, not through brittle fracture, because a brittle material would shatter on impact, producing fragments that could damage the underlying hull or clog the cavitation bubble curtain system described in Section 6, whereas a ductile, elastic-plastic material deforms, absorbs energy, and remains in place as a coherent — if damaged — panel that continues to provide some degree of protection even after the initial impact, a graceful degradation that the Kiddo register's systems engineering philosophy values highly and that the TPU material provides), high abrasion resistance (the Taber abrasion test per ASTM D4060, CS-17 wheel, 1,000 g load, shows a mass loss of less than 20 milligrams per 1,000 cycles, a value that is approximately 5 times better than the abrasion resistance of the Elium resin matrix and that ensures the cladding panel's surface, which is directly exposed to the sediment-laden turbidite flow, will survive the abrasive environment of the flow passage without significant erosion, a survivability that the abrasion testing validated by exposing cladding panel specimens to a slurry of synthetic seawater and silica sand (particle size 50-500 micrometers, concentration 10 percent by weight) in a recirculating erosion test loop, with the specimens being weighed before and after 100 hours of exposure (corresponding to approximately 25 kilometers of slurry flow over the specimen surface, far exceeding the estimated turbidite flow duration), the mass loss being less than 0.5 percent, indicating that erosive wear is not a significant concern for the cladding panels over the pod's 50-year service life — the panels are expected to survive multiple turbidite events before requiring replacement, a durability that the Atlantis program's maintenance plan assumes, with the cladding panels being replaced on a condition-based schedule, triggered by the ROV inspection during the post-deployment monitoring (PART-109) that assesses the panel condition and determines whether replacement is necessary, a decision that is expected to occur no more than once per decade based on the erosion rate data), and good interlayer adhesion in the FFF process (the Z-direction tensile strength — the strength across the layer boundaries, which is the weakest direction in any FFF-printed part because the bond between layers is formed by the thermal welding of the newly-deposited hot filament to the previously-deposited, partially-cooled layer, a weld whose strength depends on the interface temperature, the contact pressure, and the time available for the polymer chains to diffuse across the interface — a diffusion process that is analogous to the interlaminar bond formation in the AFP laminate, as described in PART-087, but which occurs at the much lower temperature of the TPU extrusion (approximately 220°C, the TPU's recommended extrusion temperature, at which the polymer viscosity is low enough for extrusion but the chain mobility is significantly lower than at the Elium resin's 175°C nip point temperature, resulting in slower interdiffusion and a weaker bond, a limitation that the MMP's heated build chamber — maintained at 60°C, approximately 10°C below the TPU's glass transition temperature of approximately 70°C, the highest temperature that the build chamber can sustain without causing the printed part to sag under its own weight, a constraint that limits the degree of interlayer bonding that can be achieved during the printing process — partially overcomes by keeping the previously-deposited layers warm, reducing the temperature differential between the new filament and the existing surface and thereby increasing the diffusion rate, a process optimization that the materials team confirmed through the measurement of the Z-direction tensile strength as a function of the build chamber temperature, the strength increasing from approximately 12 MPa at 25°C (unheated chamber) to approximately 22 MPa at 60°C (heated chamber), an 83 percent improvement that justifies the heated chamber's additional cost and complexity — approximately $85,000 for the chamber's insulation, the heating elements (18 kW total, distributed across six zones to maintain temperature uniformity within ±2°C), and the temperature control system, a cost that is amortized across the 10-pod demonstration program and the subsequent production pods — and that is essential for the cladding panels to withstand the impact loads, which generate tensile stresses in the Z-direction as the auxetic lattice deforms and the individual struts are pulled apart, the Z-direction strength being the limiting factor for the panel's impact resistance, a factor that the heated chamber's improvement of the Z-strength directly enhances, reducing the required strut thickness — and therefore the panel's mass and the printer's build time — for a given impact energy absorption capacity), and is supplied in the form of 2.85 mm diameter filament wound onto 10 kg spools (the MMP's filament delivery system uses a direct-drive extruder — a Bondtech BMG dual-drive extruder, modified for the MMP's higher throughput with a larger drive gear and a more powerful stepper motor (NEMA 23, 2.8 N·m holding torque, sufficient to push the 2.85 mm filament through the 1.2 mm nozzle at the 5 kg/hr deposition rate without slipping or grinding the filament, a common failure mode of FFF extruders when the extrusion force exceeds the drive gear's traction on the filament, a failure that the Bondtech's dual-drive design — which grips the filament from both sides, doubling the contact area and the traction — and the NEMA 23 motor's torque margin prevent, with the extrusion force at the 5 kg/hr rate being approximately 80 N, and the Bondtech's traction limit being approximately 200 N, a margin of 2.5 that the extruder's reliability testing — 500 hours of continuous extrusion at the maximum rate, zero filament slip events — has validated), a delivery system that minimizes the distance between the filament spool and the extruder — the spool is mounted on a rotary bearing directly above the extruder, with the filament path being a simple, straight vertical drop of approximately 300 millimeters, eliminating the bends, the friction, and the elastic energy storage in the filament that would occur if the filament were routed through a Bowden tube (a flexible conduit that guides the filament from a remote spool to the extruder, a common configuration in desktop FFF printers but one that introduces a significant pressure drop — the filament rubbing against the tube's interior wall, particularly when the tube is curved, consumes a substantial fraction of the extruder's drive force, reducing the maximum extrusion rate and increasing the risk of filament buckling or jamming — a trade-off that the MMP's design avoids by mounting the spool directly above the extruder, a configuration that is only possible because the MMP's build volume is not enclosed on the top — the gantry structure provides the X-Y motion, and the Z-axis raises and lowers the build platform, leaving the space above the extruder free for the spool, a design that is specific to the large-format, open-frame architecture of the MMP and that would not be practical for a smaller, enclosed printer, where the spool must be located outside the enclosure to avoid the elevated temperature of the build chamber — the 60°C chamber temperature being too high for the TPU filament, which would soften and deform, causing feeding problems, if stored at that temperature, a thermal constraint that the MMP's design satisfies by locating the spool above the chamber, where the ambient temperature is approximately 30°C, well below the filament's softening temperature, and by thermally insulating the spool from the chamber via a 25 mm thick ceramic fiber blanket that lines the top of the build chamber, a simple but effective thermal barrier that the temperature measurements confirm maintains the spool temperature at less than 35°C even when the chamber is at 60°C, a separation that is essential for the reliable feeding of the flexible TPU filament, which is more prone to buckling and jamming than rigid filaments such as PLA or ABS, because the TPU's low elastic modulus — approximately 30 MPa, compared to approximately 3,000 MPa for PLA — allows the filament to compress elastically under the extruder's drive force rather than transmitting that force to the hot end, a compliance that, if the filament path contains any unsupported length — any gap between the extruder's drive gear and the hot end's entrance where the filament is not constrained within a close-fitting guide tube — would allow the filament to buckle sideways, jamming the extruder and stopping the print, a failure that the MMP's design prevents by constraining the filament within a close-fitting (2.90 mm ID, for the 2.85 mm filament, providing a clearance of only 0.05 mm — enough for the filament to slide freely but not enough for it to buckle) PTFE guide tube that extends from the drive gear's exit to the hot end's entrance, a distance of approximately 40 millimeters, short enough that the unsupported length is too small for buckling to occur at the 80 N extrusion force — the Euler buckling load for a 2.85 mm diameter TPU filament with a 40 mm unsupported length, P_crit = π² × E × I / L², with E ≈ 30 MPa and I = π × d⁴ / 64 ≈ π × (2.85 × 10⁻³ m)⁴ / 64 ≈ 3.2 × 10⁻¹² m⁴, giving P_crit = π² × 30 × 10⁶ Pa × 3.2 × 10⁻¹² m⁴ / (0.04 m)² ≈ 593 N, far exceeding the 80 N extrusion force, a comfortable margin that the PTFE guide tube maintains even if the tube wears slightly, increasing the clearance — a wear that the preventive maintenance program monitors by measuring the tube's internal diameter every 500 print hours, replacing the tube when the clearance exceeds 0.2 mm, at which point the Euler buckling load would be approximately 350 N — still more than sufficient, but approaching the regime where the margin is less comfortable, a maintenance threshold that the program's conservatism sets well before the buckling load approaches the extrusion force, a proactive replacement strategy that, as in all aspects of the Atlantis fabrication process, aims to prevent failures rather than react to them. The MMP operates continuously during the print, with the operator monitoring the print via a webcam (a Axis Q6155-E PTZ network camera, providing a live video feed of the print bed, with the operator able to remotely pause, adjust, or abort the print if an anomaly — a filament break, a layer shift, a nozzle clog — is detected, a remote monitoring capability that reduces the operator's workload (the operator can monitor multiple printers simultaneously, rather than being dedicated to a single machine) and improves the operator's safety (the operator is not required to be in close proximity to the printer during its operation, reducing the exposure to the TPU's fumes — the extrusion of TPU at 220°C generates a small amount of volatile organic compounds (VOCs), primarily the polyol and isocyanate monomers that are released from the TPU's thermal decomposition, a decomposition that is minimal at the recommended extrusion temperature (the TPU's thermal stability, as measured by TGA, shows a weight loss of less than 0.1 percent after 1 hour at 220°C, indicating that the decomposition is negligible) but that, over the many hours of a single print, could accumulate to a concentration that exceeds the OSHA permissible exposure limit (PEL) for isocyanates — 0.02 ppm, a very low limit that reflects the respiratory sensitization hazard of isocyanates, which can cause occupational asthma even at low concentrations, a hazard that the MMP's exhaust system — a 1,500 m³/hr fan that draws air from the build chamber through a activated carbon filter (which adsorbs the organic vapors) and exhausts it to the outside atmosphere, maintaining the isocyanate concentration in the operator's breathing zone below 0.005 ppm, as measured by the facility's real-time isocyanate monitor (a Thermo Fisher Scientific iQ Series gas analyzer, using Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy to measure the isocyanate concentration with a detection limit of 0.001 ppm, a sensitivity that is more than sufficient to verify compliance with the 0.02 ppm PEL), a respiratory protection system that the facility's industrial hygienist has validated through the personal exposure monitoring of the MMP operators, with the monitoring results showing that the operators' daily isocyanate exposure is below the detection limit of the personal sampling method (a glass fiber filter coated with 1-(2-methoxyphenyl)piperazine, which reacts with the isocyanates to form stable urea derivatives, which are then desorbed and analyzed by HPLC, the standard NIOSH 5525 method for isocyanate exposure assessment, a method that the Mojave facility's on-site industrial hygiene laboratory performs, providing the data that the facility's safety manager reviews monthly to confirm that the respiratory protection measures are effective and that the operators are not being exposed to hazardous concentrations of isocyanates — a confirmation that, to date, has been consistently positive, with zero exceedances of the PEL or of the facility's internal action limit of 0.01 ppm, a safety record that the facility considers a prerequisite for the MMP's continued operation and that the program's safety culture, which extends the Aegis C safety philosophy from the deployed pods to the factory floor, insists upon, a commitment to occupational safety that is as integral to the Atlantis program as the commitment to pod safety, because the program's mission — to protect human lives — begins not at the seabed but at the factory gate, with the protection of the workers whose hands and minds create the pods, a principle that the Dolittle register articulates as "the unity of protection" — the same ethical imperative that demands the pod's structural integrity also demands the factory's respiratory protection, the operator's ergonomic workstation, the safety interlocks on the AFP machines, and the blast containment of the LPC, a comprehensive approach to safety that is, in the Dolittle register's philosophical framework, the foundation of the program's moral legitimacy, a legitimacy that the program earns not through its intentions but through its actions — the actions, documented in this and the other Block 8 documents, that protect the people who build the pods, so that they may, in turn, protect the people who will live in them.
