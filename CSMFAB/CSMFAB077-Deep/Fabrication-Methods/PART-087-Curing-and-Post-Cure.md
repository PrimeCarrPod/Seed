# CSMFAB077-PART-087 — CURING AND POST-CURE: LASER HEATING, INTERLAMINAR BOND STRENGTH, ULTRASONIC INSPECTION
## Block 8 — Fabrication Methods | Atlantis-Class Subsea Habitation Module Production
## Carrington Storm Motors — Safe Pod Engineering Division | Aegis C Material Architecture

---

The curing and post-cure process for the Atlantis pod hull laminate — the thermal treatment that transforms the individual plies of nanodiamond-Elium-basalt prepreg, deposited layer-by-layer by the AFP machines as described in PART-081, into a monolithic, fully-consolidated composite structure whose interlaminar bond strength, residual monomer content, and void distribution have been driven to their optimal values through the precise control of the time-temperature-pressure history that each microscopic volume of the laminate experiences from the moment the laser-heated compaction roller presses the incoming tow onto the previously deposited substrate until the moment, approximately 30 minutes later for any given point on the hull surface, when the temperature at that point has decayed below the glass transition temperature of the Elium matrix (approximately 120°C for the fully-cured, dry-state resin as documented in PART-083, or approximately 112°C at the exterior surface where the moisture-saturated matrix has a slightly lower Tg as described in PART-083's moisture absorption analysis) and the polymer chains are immobilized, the polymerization reaction has effectively ceased (residual monomer content having reached its asymptotic value of approximately 1.0 ± 0.5 weight percent, the asymptotic conversion being determined by the competition between the polymerization rate, which decreases with temperature, and the time available at each temperature during the cooling transient, a competition that the process model described below quantifies and that the post-cure treatment is designed to drive to completion), and the laminate has developed its full complement of mechanical properties — strength, stiffness, toughness, and resistance to the gradual environmental degradation that the Atlantis pod will experience over fifty years at the seabed — that the structural design assumes and that the certification basis (PART-120) requires to be demonstrated for each production hull. This document, PART-087, provides the complete specification of the curing and post-cure process, from the laser heating at the AFP deposition point through the post-cure thermal treatment, and including the ultrasonic inspection that verifies the resulting laminate quality with a spatial resolution and a defect detection sensitivity that enable the detection of individual voids smaller than 1 millimeter in diameter and interlaminar disbonds as small as 5 millimeters in diameter, a capability that exceeds the requirements of the defect tolerance analysis described in PART-088 and that provides the quantitative evidence — the C-scan maps of ultrasonic attenuation, the A-scan waveforms showing the reflection amplitudes from each ply interface, and the statistical summaries of the detected defect population for each hull — that the laminate meets the LBFRP-001 acceptance criteria and is therefore fit for deployment to the seabed, a fitness-for-service determination that is the culmination of the entire fabrication process and the final quality gate before the pod is released from the Mojave facility to the transport, deployment, and commissioning phases described in Block 9 (PARTS 096-110).

The laser heating at the AFP deposition point — the 980-nanometer diode laser, 4-kilowatt maximum power, line-generating optic producing a 52 mm × 8 mm rectangular beam as described in PART-081 — provides the thermal energy that melts the Elium resin on the incoming tow and on the surface of the previously deposited ply, raising the temperature at the nip point (the line of contact between the compaction roller, the incoming tow, and the substrate) to the setpoint of 175 ± 5°C, as measured by the infrared pyrometer and controlled by the closed-loop laser power modulation system described in PART-081, a temperature that is approximately 15°C above the resin's nominal melting temperature of 160°C (the temperature at which the PMMA crystalline phase, if any were present — and as discussed in PART-083, there is none, because the Elium/PMMA is amorphous, but the "melting" temperature in the context of an amorphous thermoplastic refers to the temperature at which the viscosity has decreased sufficiently for the polymer to flow under the compaction pressure, approximately 160°C corresponding to a viscosity of approximately 100 Pa·s, the threshold for effective flow and consolidation), and that provides the thermal driving force for three simultaneous physical and chemical processes: first, the flow of the molten resin from the incoming tow into the interstices between the adjacent tows and into any surface irregularities on the previously deposited ply, filling the gaps and creating a continuous resin layer between the successive plies that, upon cooling, forms the interlaminar bond, a flow process that is governed by the Darcy's law model described in PART-086 but with the important difference that, in the AFP process, the resin flows across the tow boundaries (transverse to the tow direction) rather than into the tow interior (transverse to the filament direction), the transverse flow being driven by the compaction pressure — approximately 0.5 to 1.0 MPa as described in PART-081 — and being resisted by the viscosity of the molten resin, which at 175°C is approximately 50 Pa·s (extrapolated from the rheometry data described in PART-083, using the Arrhenius relationship with the activation energy of 35 kJ/mol, giving η(175°C) = η(120°C) × exp[(Ea/R) × (1/175+273 − 1/120+273)] ≈ 200 Pa·s × exp[(35,000/8.314) × (1/448 − 1/393)] ≈ 200 Pa·s × exp[4,210 × (0.002232 − 0.002545)] ≈ 200 Pa·s × exp[4,210 × (−0.000313)] ≈ 200 × exp(−1.318) ≈ 200 × 0.268 ≈ 53.5 Pa·s, consistent with the 50 Pa·s estimate), and with the flow distance being approximately 1 millimeter (the width of the gap between adjacent tow courses, which is typically 0 to 1 millimeter as controlled by the AFP toolpath generation algorithm described in PART-081, the gap being the space that the resin must fill to achieve a continuous interlaminar bond), such that the flow time required for the resin to traverse this gap, from the Darcy's law model with the appropriate parameters (permeability of the gap — which for a 1 mm wide, 35 μm thick (one ply thickness) gap bounded by parallel walls is approximately k = h²/12 = (35 × 10⁻⁶ m)² / 12 ≈ 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ m², several orders of magnitude higher than the tow permeability, reflecting the fact that the gap is an unobstructed channel rather than a porous fiber bed, and the resin flows through it as a viscous fluid in a narrow channel, not as a fluid percolating through a porous medium, a flow regime transition that occurs when the gap width exceeds approximately 10 times the filament diameter (90 micrometers), at which point the fiber bed can no longer be treated as a homogeneous porous medium and the flow becomes a channel flow, with the appropriate model being the Poiseuille flow between parallel plates, v = (ΔP × h²) / (12 × η × L), where ΔP is the pressure drop driving the flow (the compaction pressure, approximately 1 MPa), h = 35 μm is the channel height, η ≈ 50 Pa·s is the resin viscosity, and L ≈ 1 mm is the flow distance, giving a flow velocity of v ≈ (10⁶ Pa × (35 × 10⁻⁶ m)²) / (12 × 50 Pa·s × 10⁻³ m) ≈ (10⁶ × 1.225 × 10⁻⁹) / (0.6) ≈ 2.04 × 10⁻³ m/s ≈ 2 millimeters per second, and a flow time of L / v ≈ 1 mm / 2 mm/s ≈ 0.5 seconds, which is approximately 10 times longer than the 50-millisecond residence time of the tow in the laser-heated zone as described in PART-081, meaning that the resin does NOT have time, during the brief passage through the nip point, to flow completely across the gap and fill it — the gap is only partially filled during the AFP layup, and the complete filling occurs during the subsequent post-cure treatment described below, when the entire laminate is heated to a temperature that reduces the resin viscosity and allows the residual consolidation to proceed over a timescale of hours rather than milliseconds, a post-cure consolidation that the AFP process is deliberately designed to leave incomplete — the AFP's role is to place the tows in the correct position and orientation, to achieve a partial consolidation that locks the tows in place and prevents them from shifting during the subsequent handling of the laminate, and to initiate the polymerization that converts the liquid monomer into a solid polymer with sufficient green strength (the strength of the uncured or partially-cured laminate, which for the Elium resin at the 175°C nip point temperature corresponds to a monomer conversion of approximately 92 percent as described in PART-083, and which is sufficient to allow the laminate to be handled, trimmed, and transferred to the post-cure oven without delamination or distortion) to survive the transfer to the post-cure oven, a division of labor between the AFP process (positioning and partial consolidation) and the post-cure process (complete consolidation and final polymerization) that is the fundamental process architecture for thermoplastic AFP composites and that distinguishes them from thermoset AFP composites, where the AFP process must achieve complete consolidation because the crosslinking reaction that solidifies the thermoset matrix is irreversible and cannot be reactivated by a subsequent heating cycle, a fundamental limitation of thermoset composites that the thermoplastic Elium system elegantly overcomes, enabling the two-stage AFP + post-cure process architecture that the Atlantis program employs; second, the interdiffusion of the PMMA polymer chains across the ply interface — the polymer chains on the surface of the previously deposited ply, which are in the solid, glassy state at the mandrel temperature of 80°C, are heated by the laser to the 175°C nip point temperature, at which they become mobile and begin to diffuse into the molten resin on the incoming tow, a diffusion process that is governed by the reptation model of polymer dynamics described in PART-083, with the characteristic diffusion time — the time required for a polymer chain to diffuse a distance equal to its own radius of gyration, approximately 10 to 20 nanometers for a PMMA chain with a molecular weight of Mn ≈ 100,000 g/mol, a distance that, if traversed, indicates that the chains from the two plies have interpenetrated sufficiently to form the entanglements that constitute the interlaminar bond — being approximately 5 to 10 milliseconds at 175°C, as calculated from the reptation time formula, τ_rep ≈ (ζ × N³ × b²) / (kT), where ζ is the monomeric friction coefficient (approximately 10⁻¹⁰ N·s/m for PMMA at 175°C, estimated from the viscosity-molecular weight relationship, η ∝ M^(3.4), and the known values for PMMA from the polymer physics literature), N ≈ 1,000 is the number of monomer units per chain (for Mn ≈ 100,000 g/mol and a monomer molecular weight of approximately 100 g/mol for MMA), b ≈ 0.25 nm is the statistical segment length of PMMA (a measure of the chain stiffness, approximately 0.25 nm for PMMA based on its characteristic ratio C∞ ≈ 9, which relates the mean-square end-to-end distance of the chain to the number of bonds, <R²> = C∞ × n × l², where n ≈ 2,000 is the number of backbone bonds per chain and l ≈ 0.154 nm is the carbon-carbon bond length, giving <R²> = 9 × 2,000 × (0.154)² ≈ 426 nm², and the radius of gyration Rg ≈ (<R²>/6)^(1/2) ≈ (71)^(1/2) ≈ 8.4 nm, consistent with the 10-20 nm estimate above), k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K is Boltzmann's constant, and T = 448 K (175°C), giving τ_rep ≈ (10⁻¹⁰ × 1,000³ × (0.25 × 10⁻⁹)²) / (1.38 × 10⁻²³ × 448) ≈ (10⁻¹⁰ × 10⁹ × 6.25 × 10⁻²⁰) / (6.18 × 10⁻²¹) ≈ (6.25 × 10⁻²¹) / (6.18 × 10⁻²¹) ≈ 1.0 second, which is significantly longer than the 50-millisecond residence time, meaning that the chain interdiffusion is far from complete during the AFP layup — only the very beginnings of the interdiffusion process occur at the nip point, with the chains penetrating only a few nanometers into the adjacent ply, forming a weak, incipient interlaminar bond that is sufficient for the green strength requirement but that accounts for only a fraction of the ultimate interlaminar bond strength that is achieved after the post-cure treatment, when the laminate is maintained at a temperature above the Tg for a duration sufficient for the polymer chains to complete their interdiffusion and establish the full entanglement network, a process that the post-cure treatment described below is specifically designed to accomplish, and that the Atlantis program has characterized through the preparation of laminate specimens that were subjected to various post-cure durations and then tested for interlaminar shear strength, the data showing that the shear strength increases from approximately 35 MPa for the as-laid (AFP-only, no post-cure) laminate to approximately 68 MPa after 4 hours of post-cure at 140°C (the post-cure temperature specified below), a near-doubling of the shear strength that testifies to the importance of the post-cure treatment and that confirms the reptation-based model of the interlaminar bond formation, a model that provides the physical foundation for the post-cure process specification and that the process engineering team has validated through the quantitative agreement between the model's predictions and the measured interlaminar shear strength as a function of post-cure temperature and duration; and third, the continuation of the MMA monomer polymerization — at the 175°C nip point temperature, the lauroyl peroxide initiator decomposes extremely rapidly (the half-life at 175°C being approximately 0.01 seconds, as estimated from the Arrhenius parameters for lauroyl peroxide decomposition — the pre-exponential factor A ≈ 10¹⁶ s⁻¹ and the activation energy Ea ≈ 128 kJ/mol, giving the rate constant k = A × exp(−Ea/RT) = 10¹⁶ × exp[−128,000 / (8.314 × 448)] = 10¹⁶ × exp(−34.4) = 10¹⁶ × 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ = 12 s⁻¹, and the half-life t₁/₂ = ln(2) / k = 0.693 / 12 ≈ 0.058 seconds, approximately consistent with the 0.01-second estimate given the approximations in the Arrhenius parameters and the fact that the peroxide decomposition kinetics in the Elium resin mixture, which contains the dissolved PMMA polymer, the nanodiamond particles, and various additives, differ from the kinetics in a simple solvent due to the cage effect — the tendency of the initially-formed free radical pairs to recombine within the solvent cage before they can diffuse apart and initiate polymerization, an effect that reduces the effective initiator efficiency to approximately 50 to 70 percent and that extends the effective half-life by the inverse of the efficiency factor), generating a burst of free radicals that drive the monomer conversion from the 92 percent achieved during the brief laser heating to the 99 percent achieved asymptotically as the laminate cools, a conversion that is essential for achieving the target residual monomer content of 1.0 ± 0.5 weight percent and the corresponding Tg of 118°C (dry state), as documented in PART-083, and that the post-cure treatment described below drives to completion for any regions of the laminate where the AFP heating was, for whatever reason — a local variation in the laser power, a temporary drop in the resin temperature due to a fluctuation in the tow feed rate, a particularly thick section of the laminate where the laser energy was insufficient to heat the entire thickness — insufficient to achieve the target conversion, a "leveling" effect that the post-cure treatment provides by uniformly heating the entire laminate to a temperature that reactivates the polymerization and allows any under-cured regions to catch up to the target conversion, a process uniformity benefit that the SPC data on the residual monomer content (measured by the DSC method described in PART-083 on witness specimens extracted from each hull) confirms, with the within-hull variability in residual monomer content being less than ±0.3 percent — a remarkable uniformity for a structure of this size and complexity, and a testament to the effectiveness of the post-cure treatment in homogenizing the cure state of the laminate, a homogenization that is essential for the consistent mechanical properties that the structural analysis assumes and that the certification basis requires.

The post-cure thermal treatment — in which the completed hull laminate, immediately after the conclusion of the AFP layup and while still on its mandrel (the mandrel serving as the dimensional fixture that maintains the hull's shape during the post-cure, preventing the distortion — the spring-back and warpage — that would occur if the laminate were removed from the mandrel before the post-cure, because the Elium matrix, being a thermoplastic, has no crosslinked network to lock in the shape, and the laminate, at the post-cure temperature, is sufficiently compliant that it would deform under its own weight if not supported by the mandrel, a deformation that would alter the hull's geometry, increase the geometric imperfections that govern the buckling pressure (the buckling of a cylindrical shell under external pressure is notoriously sensitive to geometric imperfections, with the buckling pressure decreasing by as much as 50 percent for an imperfection amplitude of only 0.1 times the shell thickness, per the classical Koiter imperfection sensitivity theory for cylindrical shells, a sensitivity that the Atlantis hull's structural analysis accounts for through a nonlinear finite element analysis (ABAQUS with the Riks arc-length method for post-buckling analysis) that applies an initial geometric imperfection in the shape of the first buckling mode, with an amplitude equal to the measured imperfection of the as-built mandrel plus the documented distortion allowance of ±3 millimeters, and that verifies that the buckling pressure with this imperfection exceeds the design collapse pressure of 18 MPa (4.5 MPa external pressure × factor of safety 4.0) by a margin of at least 15 percent, a margin that the hull's dimensional control — maintained by the post-cure on the mandrel — is essential for preserving, and that any post-demolding distortion would reduce, potentially below the certification-required margin, a manufacturing risk that the on-mandrel post-cure eliminates) — is performed in a custom-built, electrically-heated industrial oven, a walk-in style enclosure with internal dimensions of 20 meters length × 8 meters width × 6 meters height (sufficient to accommodate the entire 15-meter-long Atlantis pod hull on its mandrel, with clearance for the air circulation fans and the temperature sensors), lined with stainless steel panels backed by 150 millimeters of mineral wool insulation (thermal conductivity approximately 0.04 W/(m·K) at 100°C, providing a heat loss rate of approximately 30 kW at the 140°C post-cure temperature, which the oven's heating elements — 18 individual 15-kilowatt finned tubular heaters, arranged in six independently-controlled zones (forward end cap, forward transition, forward cylinder, aft cylinder, aft transition, aft end cap, mirroring the six-zone configuration of the mandrel heating system described in PART-081, a commonality that simplifies the control system integration and the operator training, and that allows the mandrel heating system, which is permanently embedded in the mandrel, to supplement the oven heaters during the post-cure, providing heat directly to the laminate from the interior as well as from the exterior via the oven air, a dual-sided heating arrangement that reduces the through-thickness temperature gradient in the laminate — the hottest region being the surfaces that are in contact with the mandrel (interior) and the oven air (exterior), and the coolest region being the mid-plane of the laminate, which is farthest from both heat sources and which, without the interior mandrel heating, would lag significantly behind the surface temperature, prolonging the time required for the mid-plane to reach the post-cure temperature and thereby extending the total post-cure cycle time, a throughput penalty that the mandrel heating system reduces by approximately 40 percent (from a total cycle time of approximately 10 hours without mandrel heating to approximately 6 hours with it), a productivity improvement that the ZIRCONIA register's cost analysis valued at approximately $18,000 per hull (the cost of the additional oven occupancy time, including the electricity, the operator labor, and the opportunity cost of the oven being unavailable for the next hull), and that justified the $45,000 incremental cost of integrating the mandrel heating system with the post-cure oven, a cost that the analysis amortized over the ten-hull demonstration program, yielding a net present value of approximately $135,000 (the $180,000 in savings minus the $45,000 cost, approximately $135,000 positive), a favorable return on investment that ZIRCONIA approved — exceed, with a comfortable margin, the heating capacity of 270 kW total) is designed to counter, with the six-zone configuration allowing the temperature in each zone to be individually controlled to maintain the specified temperature uniformity of ±3°C across the entire hull surface, a uniformity that the oven's commissioning test — in which 72 calibrated type-K thermocouples were temporarily bonded to the surface of the first hull during its post-cure, distributed at 12 axial stations and 6 circumferential positions — demonstrated, with the measured temperatures at all 72 locations being within the ±3°C band throughout the 4-hour dwell at the 140°C post-cure temperature, a result that verified the oven's thermal design and that the production team monitors continuously during each hull's post-cure via a permanent array of 18 thermocouples (3 per zone, providing the feedback for the zone PID controllers, with the temperature data being logged at 1-minute intervals to the hull's digital fabrication record, a record that the quality assurance team reviews after each post-cure to confirm that the temperature-time profile met the specification and that any deviations — a temporary temperature drop due to a power interruption, for example, which occurred once during the hull #3 post-cure when a Mojave Desert thunderstorm caused a 3-second utility power sag that the oven's uninterruptible power supply (UPS) corrected within 5 seconds, a transient that the thermal mass of the hull and the mandrel (approximately 25,000 kg combined) absorbed with a maximum temperature drop of less than 0.5°C, a negligible perturbation that the post-cure model confirms had no detectable effect on the laminate's final properties, as confirmed by the DSC Tg measurements on the hull #3 witness specimens, which were statistically indistinguishable from the hulls that experienced no power interruptions — are documented and dispositioned per the non-conformance procedure described in PART-088).

The post-cure temperature profile — the time-temperature history that each point on the hull surface experiences during the post-cure — is specified as follows: ramp from the mandrel temperature of 80°C (the temperature at which the AFP layup concluded and at which the laminate has been maintained, via the mandrel heaters, during the approximately 2-hour period between the AFP completion and the post-cure start, a "hold" period during which the polymerization continues slowly, the residual monomer being consumed at the decreasing rate corresponding to the decreasing temperature, such that the monomer conversion upon entry to the post-cure oven is approximately 96 to 97 percent — higher than the 92 percent at the moment of AFP deposition, because the 2-hour hold at 80°C, while too low a temperature for rapid polymerization, is sufficient for the slow, diffusion-controlled polymerization of the remaining monomer, a process that is analogous to the "vitrification" stage in thermoset cure, in which the polymer network becomes so dense — or, in the case of the thermoplastic Elium, the polymer chains become so entangled and the free volume so reduced — that the monomer molecules can no longer diffuse to the propagating radical sites, and the polymerization rate becomes diffusion-limited, a kinetic regime that the 80°C mandrel temperature supports by providing sufficient thermal energy for the monomer diffusion, albeit at a greatly reduced rate compared to the 175°C nip point temperature, and that the 2-hour hold exploits to increase the monomer conversion and thereby reduce the burden on the post-cure treatment, the 2-hour duration being determined by the AFP machine's cycle time — the time between the completion of the last ply and the moment the AFP machines have been retracted, the laminate has been trimmed of its edge flash (the excess material that extends beyond the trim line, approximately 50 millimeters on each end, which is cut away using an ultrasonic knife — a hand-held tool with a titanium blade vibrating at 20 kHz, which slices through the partially-cured composite without generating the dust and fiber fragments that a conventional saw would produce, a cleanliness consideration that is important in the AFP cell's controlled environment — and discarded as scrap, a loss of approximately 5 percent of the laminate material that the production planning accounts for and that the cost analysis (PART-094) includes), the in-process inspection described in PART-088 has been performed, and any identified defects have been reworked, a period that typically takes 2 hours and that the production scheduling uses as the "hold" at 80°C before the post-cure ramp begins) to the post-cure temperature of 140°C at a ramp rate of 1°C per minute, a slow ramp that is necessary to prevent the generation of thermal stresses in the laminate — as the temperature increases, the Elium matrix expands thermally (its CTE being approximately 70 × 10⁻⁶ per °C, as documented in PART-083), while the basalt fiber, with its CTE of approximately 8 × 10⁻⁶ per °C, expands much less, a differential expansion that generates tensile stresses in the matrix (the fiber restrains the matrix's expansion, putting the matrix in a state of tension in the fiber direction) and compressive stresses in the fibers (the matrix pulls on the fibers, putting them in compression), stresses that, if the temperature ramp is too rapid, can exceed the matrix's tensile strength at the elevated temperature and cause micro-cracking — fine, distributed cracks in the matrix that, while not immediately catastrophic (they do not cause the laminate to fail under the post-cure loads, which are essentially zero — the laminate is unloaded during the post-cure, supported by the mandrel, and subjected only to its own weight and the thermal stresses), would act as initiation sites for the environmental degradation over the pod's service life, providing pathways for the ingress of seawater and initiating the hydrolytic attack on the fiber-matrix interface that the fluorinated silane is designed to resist but that is best avoided entirely by preventing the micro-cracks from forming in the first place, a prevention that the 1°C/min ramp rate achieves by limiting the thermal stress to below approximately 5 MPa — well within the matrix's tensile strength of approximately 40 MPa at 80°C (the matrix strength decreases with increasing temperature, but remains above 20 MPa even at 140°C, as documented in the Elium mechanical property data from the materials qualification program), and therefore safe from micro-cracking, a margin that the 1°C/min ramp rate provides and that a faster ramp rate — e.g., the 5°C/min that the oven is capable of, but that the process specification deliberately avoids — would reduce, potentially approaching the matrix strength and risking the micro-cracking that the process specification is designed to prevent, a conservative choice that the Aegis C safety philosophy endorses as appropriate for the criticality of the laminate's long-term integrity — a 1-hour post-cure cycle extension (the difference between the 60-minute ramp at 1°C/min from 80°C to 140°C, versus the 12-minute ramp at 5°C/min) is a small price to pay for the elimination of a known degradation mechanism. The laminate is then held at 140°C for a duration of 4 hours, a dwell period during which three essential processes occur — the completion of the monomer polymerization (the residual monomer content reducing from the approximately 3 to 4 percent at the beginning of the dwell to the final 1.0 ± 0.5 percent, a reduction that the DSC Tg measurements on the witness specimens confirm, with the Tg reaching the asymptotic value of approximately 118°C (dry state) by the end of the 4-hour dwell, and with no further increase in Tg if the dwell is extended beyond 4 hours, indicating that the polymerization is essentially complete, the remaining 1 percent of monomer being trapped in isolated domains within the glassy matrix and unable to diffuse to the active radical sites, a "kinetic trapping" phenomenon that is common in free-radical polymerizations conducted below the Tg, and that is acceptable for the Atlantis application because the 1 percent residual monomer, as discussed in PART-083, depresses the Tg by approximately 2°C and reduces the matrix elastic modulus by approximately 1 percent, effects that are within the design allowables and that do not compromise the hull's structural integrity), the completion of the polymer chain interdiffusion across the ply interfaces (the reptation time at 140°C being approximately exp[(35,000/8.314) × (1/353 − 1/413)] ≈ exp[4,210 × (0.002833 − 0.002421)] ≈ exp(1.73) ≈ 5.6 times longer than the reptation time at 175°C, as calculated previously, giving τ_rep(140°C) ≈ 5.6 × 1.0 s ≈ 5.6 seconds, which is far shorter than the 4-hour (14,400-second) dwell, meaning that the chain interdiffusion has ample time to complete, the polymer chains from adjacent plies fully interpenetrating and forming the entanglement network that provides the maximum interlaminar bond strength, a completion that the interlaminar shear strength data confirms — the shear strength of specimens subjected to the 4-hour, 140°C dwell is 68 ± 2 MPa, statistically indistinguishable from the shear strength of specimens subjected to longer dwells of 8, 16, or 24 hours, indicating that the interlaminar bond formation is complete within the 4-hour dwell and that no further improvement occurs with extended post-curing, a saturation behavior that is consistent with the reptation model and that defines the optimal post-cure duration), and the completion of the resin flow into the gaps between adjacent tow courses (the Poiseuille flow analysis from earlier in this document indicates that the flow time for the gap filling at 175°C is approximately 0.5 seconds, but at 140°C, the resin viscosity is approximately η(140°C) = 200 Pa·s × exp[(35,000/8.314) × (1/413 − 1/393)] ≈ 200 × exp[4,210 × (0.002421 − 0.002545)] ≈ 200 × exp(−0.522) ≈ 200 × 0.593 ≈ 119 Pa·s (interpolated between the 50 Pa·s at 175°C and the 200 Pa·s at 120°C, the range over which the Arrhenius model is valid), and the flow time for gap filling at this higher viscosity is approximately (119/50) × 0.5 s ≈ 1.2 seconds, still far shorter than the 4-hour dwell, so the gap filling is also complete, with the resin having had ample time to flow across the 1-millimeter gaps and establish a continuous, void-free interlaminar resin layer, a completion that the ultrasonic inspection data (described below) confirms, with the C-scan images showing no systematic indications of incomplete gap filling — no linear indications along the tow boundaries that would correspond to the unfilled gaps — in any of the hulls produced to date, a validation of the post-cure gap-filling mechanism and the adequacy of the 4-hour dwell duration). Following the dwell, the laminate is cooled to ambient temperature (approximately 25°C) at a controlled rate of 1°C per minute — symmetric with the heating ramp, and for the same reason: the slow cooling minimizes the thermal stresses that develop as the laminate contracts from the 140°C dwell temperature to the ambient temperature, a contraction that, because of the CTE mismatch between the fiber and the matrix, would generate residual stresses in the laminate — the matrix would be in a state of residual tension (having contracted more than the fiber and being restrained by it), and the fiber would be in a state of residual compression (having contracted less than the matrix and being compressed by it), a residual stress state that, while not large enough to cause immediate damage (the matrix tensile strength, even at ambient temperature, is approximately 80 MPa, and the residual thermal stress is estimated to be approximately 15 to 20 MPa based on the classical lamination theory calculation of the thermal stress in a cross-ply laminate subjected to a 115°C temperature drop — the difference between the stress-free temperature, which is approximately the Tg of 120°C (above which the matrix can relax any thermal stresses by viscous flow, because the polymer chains are mobile and can rearrange to accommodate the differential expansion, a stress relaxation mechanism that operates on the timescale of the reptation time — approximately 1 second at 140°C, as calculated above — and that is therefore more than fast enough to prevent the accumulation of thermal stresses during the 1°C/min cooling ramp, until the temperature drops below the Tg, at which point the chains are immobilized and the stresses begin to accumulate, the stress-free temperature being identified with the Tg, a standard assumption in the thermal stress analysis of polymer composites, and a reasonable one for the Elium system given its amorphous, non-crystallizing, non-crosslinking nature — and the ambient temperature of 25°C, a temperature drop of 95°C, which, combined with the CTE mismatch and the laminate's elastic properties, produces the 15 to 20 MPa residual stress estimate), and therefore not a threat to the laminate's immediate integrity, but which, over decades, could contribute to the slow accumulation of creep damage — the residual tensile stress in the matrix, sustained for fifty years, drives the viscoelastic creep of the PMMA, causing the matrix to gradually elongate in the direction of the tensile stress, a time-dependent deformation that, if it accumulates sufficiently, could transfer additional load to the basalt fibers, increasing the fiber stress and potentially causing fiber breakage, a slow damage mechanism that the structural analysis accounts for through a viscoelastic creep model of the Elium matrix (using the time-temperature superposition principle, as is standard for the creep analysis of polymers, with the creep compliance master curve being constructed from short-term creep tests at multiple temperatures — 25, 40, 60, and 80°C — and shifted to the 15°C service temperature using the Williams-Landel-Ferry (WLF) shift factor, with the WLF constants for PMMA — C₁ = 8.86, C₂ = 101.6 K at the reference temperature of the Tg (393 K for the Elium PMMA) — providing the temperature dependence of the shift factor, log(a_T) = −C₁ × (T − T_ref) / (C₂ + T − T_ref), which for T = 15°C (288 K) and T_ref = 120°C (393 K) gives log(a_T) = −8.86 × (288 − 393) / (101.6 + 288 − 393) = −8.86 × (−105) / (−3.4) ≈ −8.86 × 30.9 ≈ −273.7, indicating that the creep at 15°C is approximately 10²⁷⁴ times slower than the creep at the Tg — an absurdly large retardation that, in practice, means that the creep of the Elium matrix at 15°C is negligible over any timescale of human interest, including the fifty-year design life, and that the residual thermal stress, once established, remains essentially constant for the service life, neither relaxing nor causing progressive damage, a favorable conclusion that the creep analysis confirms and that removes the residual thermal stress as a concern for the long-term integrity of the Atlantis hull — a concern that the slow cooling ramp, by minimizing the magnitude of the residual stress, further reduces, providing an additional margin of safety that the structural analysis conservatively assumes is not needed but that the Aegis C safety philosophy, with its preference for margins over minimalism, welcomes as a contribution to the overall structural reliability.

The ultrasonic inspection of the cured hull laminate is the primary non-destructive evaluation (NDE) method for verifying the quality of the Atlantis pod's pressure hull, and its technical specification — the ultrasonic frequency, the transducer configuration, the scanning mechanism, the data acquisition and processing, and the defect detection and sizing algorithms — is informed by the specific defect types (voids, delaminations, disbonds, porosity, foreign object inclusions, and variations in fiber volume fraction or fiber orientation) that the LBFRP-001 composite may contain and that the defect tolerance analysis (PART-088) has determined are critical (i.e., they degrade the laminate's structural performance below the design allowables and must therefore be detected and either accepted, if their size and location are within the allowable limits, or repaired, if they exceed those limits, or rejected, if they are not repairable, in which case the affected hull section must be scrapped and replaced, a drastic outcome that the quality system is designed to prevent by detecting and correcting the process conditions that cause defects before they are incorporated into the laminate, as described in PART-088, rather than relying on the NDE to identify defects in the finished laminate, which, while necessary as a final verification, is not the primary defect prevention strategy — the primary strategy is process control, and the NDE is the independent verification that the process control has succeeded, a "trust but verify" philosophy that is consistent with the Aegis C quality management system and with the regulatory requirements for pressure vessel fabrication). The ultrasonic inspection is performed using a custom-built, 7-axis robotic scanning system — a KUKA KR 1000 titan heavy-payload industrial robot (1,000 kg payload capacity, 3,200 mm reach, ±0.1 mm positioning repeatability) equipped with a phased-array ultrasonic transducer (Olympus 5L64-A2, 64 elements, 5 MHz center frequency, 0.6 mm element pitch, 38.4 mm total aperture, capable of electronic beam steering and focusing over an angular range of ±45 degrees from the transducer normal, enabling the inspection of the curved hull surface without requiring the transducer to be mechanically tilted to maintain normal incidence — the phased-array beam steering automatically adjusts the focal laws (the time delays applied to each element's transmission and reception) to compensate for the local surface curvature, ensuring that the ultrasonic beam enters the laminate at the optimal angle (perpendicular to the surface, for maximum transmission through the water-laminate interface, which for the basalt-Elium composite has an acoustic impedance of approximately 3.5 × 10⁶ kg/(m²·s) (the product of the density, approximately 2,100 kg/m³, and the longitudinal wave speed, approximately 1,670 m/s for the quasi-isotropic laminate), significantly different from the acoustic impedance of water, approximately 1.5 × 10⁶ kg/(m²·s), such that only approximately 64 percent of the incident ultrasonic energy is transmitted into the laminate (the remainder being reflected at the water-laminate interface, a reflection that is actually useful, because it provides a reference echo against which the echoes from internal defects can be compared, a technique known as "interface-gated gain control" in which the gain of the ultrasonic receiver is adjusted dynamically based on the amplitude of the front-surface echo, compensating for variations in the coupling efficiency — the water gap thickness, the surface roughness, the curvature-induced beam spread — that would otherwise cause the defect echo amplitudes to vary from point to point, a variation that the gain control eliminates, ensuring that a defect of a given size produces the same echo amplitude regardless of its location on the hull, a critical requirement for the quantitative defect sizing that the acceptance criteria demand), a reflection coefficient that the Olympus phased-array system's high dynamic range (80 dB) and its powerful transmitter (100 V peak-to-peak excitation) are designed to overcome, providing sufficient signal-to-noise ratio to detect the small echoes from the internal defects even after the 64 percent transmission loss at the water-laminate interface), the transducer being coupled to the hull surface through a water column — the hull is immersed in a water-filled inspection tank (the same tank used for the hydrostatic pressure testing of the hull sections, as described in PART-083, its dimensions — 20 meters length × 8 meters width × 6 meters depth — accommodating the entire 15-meter hull with clearance for the robotic scanner, the water serving triple duty: as the ultrasonic couplant, as the medium that supports the hull during inspection (the buoyancy of the hull in water reduces the support loads and prevents the distortion that would occur if the hull were supported on mechanical cradles, which would concentrate the support forces at a few points and potentially cause local deformation of the hull, a deformation that, while elastic and recoverable, would alter the geometry during the inspection and could produce false indications — the water immersion eliminates this source of inspection error), and as the medium that provides the temperature control for the inspection — the water temperature is maintained at 25 ± 1°C by a recirculating chiller, the same temperature as the ambient environment, minimizing the thermal gradients that could distort the hull and ensuring that the ultrasonic velocity in the water, which is temperature-dependent — v_water = 1,402 + 5.03 × T − 0.058 × T² + 3.34 × 10⁻⁴ × T³ (m/s, with T in °C), per the empirical formula from the ultrasonic literature, such that a 1°C temperature change produces a velocity change of approximately 2.5 m/s, or about 0.17 percent, a change that, if uncorrected, would introduce a systematic error in the depth measurement of the defects, because the ultrasonic system calculates the defect depth from the time-of-flight of the echo, assuming a known velocity in the water and in the laminate, and any error in the velocity assumption produces a proportional error in the calculated depth — a 0.17 percent velocity error corresponds to a depth error of approximately 0.015 millimeters for a defect at the mid-plane of the 35-millimeter-thick laminate, an error that is negligible relative to the defect sizing tolerance of ±0.5 millimeters and that the 25 ± 1°C water temperature control effectively eliminates as a source of inspection uncertainty — is known and constant) that is maintained at a temperature that is uniform and stable, a temperature control that the inspection procedure verifies by measuring the water temperature at four locations (the corners of the tank) before and after each hull inspection, with the temperature variation being required to be within ±1°C of the nominal 25°C, a requirement that the tank's recirculation system, with its 50-kilowatt heating/cooling capacity and its 500-liter-per-minute recirculation rate (which exchanges the tank's 960,000-liter volume approximately once per 32 hours — sufficient to maintain the temperature uniformity, given the large thermal mass of the water and the aggressive mixing provided by the recirculation flow, which is distributed through a manifold of 24 nozzles arranged around the tank perimeter, each directing a jet of temperature-conditioned water into the tank, creating a turbulent mixing pattern that the computational fluid dynamics model of the tank confirms is sufficient to eliminate any stagnant zones where the temperature could deviate from the setpoint, a model that the commissioning test validated by deploying an array of 48 temperature sensors throughout the tank volume and recording the temperatures over a 24-hour period, confirming that all sensors remained within the ±1°C band, a result that verified the tank's thermal design and that the production team references as the basis for the inspection's temperature control assumption), satisfies. The robotic scanner moves the phased-array transducer over the hull surface in a raster pattern with a 1-millimeter scan index (the spacing between adjacent scan lines, corresponding to the Nyquist sampling criterion for the detection of defects that are at least 2 millimeters in diameter, because a defect must be sampled by at least 2 adjacent scan lines to be reliably detected, per the Shannon-Nyquist sampling theorem as applied to ultrasonic imaging, a criterion that the 1-millimeter scan index satisfies for defects down to 2 millimeters, which is below the minimum defect size of 3 millimeters that the defect tolerance analysis has determined is the smallest defect that could degrade the laminate's structural performance, meaning that all critical defects will be detected by the ultrasonic inspection, a detection capability that the inspection system's probability of detection (POD) analysis has quantified — the POD for a 3-millimeter-diameter void is 99.5 percent with 95 percent confidence, as determined by the standard "â vs. a" POD methodology (MIL-HDBK-1823A), in which a set of 29 laminate specimens, each containing a manufactured defect of known size (ranging from 1 mm to 10 mm diameter), were inspected by the ultrasonic system under the same conditions as the production hull inspection, and the inspector's calls (defect detected or not) were recorded, the resulting binary data being fitted to a log-logistic regression model, POD(a) = 1 / [1 + exp(−(ln(a) − μ)/σ)], with the parameters μ (the log of the median detectable defect size) and σ (the width of the POD transition) being estimated by maximum likelihood, and the POD curve being plotted, showing that the POD exceeds 90 percent for defect sizes above 2.3 millimeters, and reaches the 99.5 percent level at 3 millimeters, a POD that is acceptable for the Atlantis application per the certification basis (PART-120), which requires a POD of at least 90 percent for the critical defect size of 5 millimeters (the size at which a defect must be detected to ensure structural safety, as determined by the defect tolerance analysis) — the Atlantis inspection system significantly exceeds this requirement, providing a margin of detection capability that the certification authority has accepted as compensating for any unforeseen variables (surface roughness variations, operator fatigue, ambient electromagnetic interference from the robotic scanner motors) that could degrade the POD in production use, a margin that the quality system protects by performing the POD verification annually, using a new set of manufactured-defect specimens, to confirm that the system's detection capability has not degraded, and that, if the POD were to fall below the 90 percent threshold, would trigger the system's recalibration, the operator's retraining, or the replacement of the transducer — a sustainment program that ensures the ultrasonic inspection system remains capable throughout the Atlantis production run, a capability that is essential because the NDE is the final verification of the hull's structural integrity before deployment, and any defect that escapes detection becomes a latent flaw in the deployed pod, a flaw that, depending on its size and location, could grow — through the mechanisms of fatigue (if the pod experiences cyclic loading from the tidal currents and the turbidite flow vibrations), creep (if the stress concentration at the defect causes a local elevation of the matrix stress above the creep threshold, a threshold that, as discussed above, is effectively infinite at the 15°C service temperature, such that creep is not a credible growth mechanism for defects in the Atlantis hull under normal service conditions, and only becomes a concern during a crustal displacement event, when the dynamic loads temporarily elevate the stresses above the creep threshold for a duration that is too brief — hours to days — for significant creep deformation to occur, a conclusion that the creep analysis confirms and that simplifies the defect growth analysis by eliminating creep as a growth mechanism, leaving fatigue as the only credible mechanism for the growth of defects during the pod's service life), or the slow degradation of the fiber-matrix interface by moisture (which could extend the effective size of a defect by degrading the composite in the vicinity of the defect, a damage zone growth that the moisture diffusion analysis indicates is limited to a few millimeters over the fifty-year service life, as the moisture penetrates the laminate from the surfaces and the diffusion front advances at the rate of approximately (D × t)^(1/2) ≈ (8.5 × 10⁻⁹ cm²/s × 1.58 × 10⁹ s)^(1/2) ≈ (13.4 cm²)^(1/2) ≈ 3.7 cm, so that only defects within a few centimeters of the hull surfaces are exposed to moisture during the service life, while defects in the interior of the laminate remain dry and do not experience moisture-induced degradation, a spatial distribution of degradation that the defect location analysis in PART-088 accounts for, classifying defects based on their depth from the surface — surface-breaking defects are the most critical, because they provide a direct pathway for moisture ingress regardless of the diffusion distance, and the ultrasonic inspection specifically detects surface-breaking defects through the characteristic loss of the front-surface echo — and applying different acceptance criteria for surface-breaking, near-surface (within 10 mm of the surface), and interior defects, a graded acceptance approach that reflects the different degradation susceptibilities and that the certification authority has reviewed and accepted) — during the service life, eventually reaching a critical size that would cause the hull to fail, a scenario that the defect tolerance analysis prevents by requiring that all defects be below the size that could grow to the critical size within the service life, given the credible growth mechanisms, a requirement that the ultrasonic inspection's POD and sizing accuracy are designed to satisfy, and that the remaining documents of Block 8 — PARTS 088 and 089 — will elaborate in the context of the overall quality assurance and defect management system.
