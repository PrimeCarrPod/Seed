# CSMFAB077-PART-082 — BASALT FIBER PRODUCTION: 1400°C MELT, 9-MICRON FILAMENT, SOVIET HERITAGE, CSM ADVANCEMENT
## Block 8 — Fabrication Methods | Atlantis-Class Subsea Habitation Module Production
## Carrington Storm Motors — Safe Pod Engineering Division | Aegis C Material Architecture

---

The basalt fiber that constitutes the load-bearing reinforcement of the LBFRP-001 composite — and therefore the literal backbone of every Atlantis-class subsea habitation module — is produced through a manufacturing process whose fundamental principles were developed in the Soviet Union during the 1960s and 1970s at the Ukrainian Scientific Research Institute for Glass and Ceramics in Kiev, where Soviet materials scientists, operating under the direction of the Ministry of Defense and motivated by the strategic imperative of developing domestically-sourced alternatives to the carbon and aramid fibers that were subject to Western export controls under the CoCom (Coordinating Committee for Multilateral Export Controls) regime, established the fundamental process parameters — melt temperature, bushing design, filament drawing speed, and sizing chemistry — that remain the basis of basalt fiber production to the present day, a continuity of technology that the Atlantis program has exploited by drawing upon sixty years of accumulated industrial experience while simultaneously advancing the state of the art through three process innovations that are specific to the Atlantis application and that elevate the resulting basalt fiber from a generic industrial material to an aerospace-grade structural reinforcement suitable for the extreme performance demands of a subsea pressure vessel intended to protect human occupants at 45 meters depth through crustal displacement events, megatsunamis, and turbidite impacts over a fifty-year service life without maintenance, inspection, or repair access to the fiber-reinforced composite structure once it is deployed to the seabed. The present document, PART-082, provides a comprehensive technical specification of the basalt fiber production process as implemented at the Mojave facility's dedicated fiber production line, tracing the material from its geological origin in the volcanic quarries of the Cascade Range through the melting, fiberization, sizing, and winding stages to the finished tow spool that is delivered to the AFP cell's creel cabinet as documented in PART-081, and providing, at each stage, the quantitative process parameters, the quality control metrics, the failure modes and their mitigations, and the relationship between process control and the resulting fiber properties — tensile strength, elastic modulus, filament diameter uniformity, and surface chemistry — that determine the performance of the LBFRP-001 composite in the Atlantis application, a relationship that is not merely a matter of satisfying a material specification but that directly governs the factor of safety of a habitation module containing human beings, such that any deficiency in the fiber production process propagates through the composite fabrication and curing stages to become a latent defect in the deployed hull, a defect that cannot be repaired at the seabed and that could, in the worst case, manifest as a catastrophic pressure hull failure during a crustal displacement event when the hull is subjected to the maximum dynamic loads that it was designed to withstand, a failure mode that the Aegis C safety philosophy identifies as a "single-event, multiple-fatality" scenario and that the entire quality assurance architecture described in PART-088 is designed to prevent through the detection and correction of process deviations before they become embedded in the finished product.

The geological source material for the Atlantis basalt fiber is a specific basalt flow within the McKenzie Pass lava field of the central Oregon Cascade Range, a Quaternary-age volcanic complex that produced extensive olivine tholeiite basalt flows with a chemical composition that has been characterized through X-ray fluorescence (XRF) spectroscopy of samples collected from the Mojave facility's designated quarry site — a commercial aggregate quarry operated under a long-term supply agreement between Carrington Storm Motors and the quarry operator, Cascade Basalt Products LLC of Bend, Oregon — and that exhibits the following oxide composition, determined as the mean of twenty-seven independent XRF analyses of samples collected at quarterly intervals over a three-year period to characterize the compositional variability of the source material: SiO₂, 52.3 ± 1.2 weight percent; Al₂O₃, 17.1 ± 0.8 weight percent; Fe₂O₃ (total iron expressed as Fe₂O₃), 10.4 ± 0.6 weight percent; CaO, 8.9 ± 0.5 weight percent; MgO, 6.2 ± 0.4 weight percent; Na₂O, 3.1 ± 0.3 weight percent; K₂O, 1.2 ± 0.2 weight percent; TiO₂, 1.8 ± 0.2 weight percent; MnO, 0.18 ± 0.03 weight percent; P₂O₅, 0.22 ± 0.05 weight percent; loss on ignition (LOI, primarily H₂O and CO₂ from hydrous alteration minerals and carbonate veins within the basalt), 0.6 ± 0.2 weight percent. This composition falls within the narrow range that is essential for continuous basalt fiber production — specifically, the silica content must be between approximately 50 and 55 weight percent to ensure that the melt has a viscosity in the range of 100 to 300 poise at the fiberization temperature of 1,350 to 1,420°C, a viscosity window that allows the molten basalt to be drawn through the platinum-rhodium bushing orifices at a controlled rate without either the excessive fluidity that would cause the melt to drip from the bushing tips and form droplets rather than continuous filaments — a defect mode known in the basalt fiber industry as "dripping" and that is characterized by the periodic formation of beads along the filament, beads that act as stress concentrators in the finished composite and that reduce the effective fiber tensile strength by as much as 40 percent relative to a bead-free filament of identical composition and diameter — or the excessive viscosity that would require the melt temperature to be increased beyond the practical limit of the bushing's platinum-rhodium alloy, which begins to lose mechanical strength through grain growth and creep deformation at temperatures above approximately 1,450°C, leading to bushing distortion, orifice dimensional changes, and ultimately bushing failure that releases the molten basalt into the fiber production cell — a catastrophic failure mode that the Mojave facility has mitigated through the installation of a refractory-lined containment pit beneath each bushing position, capable of containing the full melt inventory of the furnace (approximately 2,500 kilograms of molten basalt at 1,400°C) without breaching the containment or endangering the operating personnel, a safety provision that the facility's hazard analysis identified as essential given the consequences of a molten basalt spill and that the facility's insurance underwriter required as a condition of the property damage coverage for the fiber production building, a building that represents a capital investment of approximately $18 million and that houses not only the basalt fiber production line but also the adjacent Elium resin synthesis and prepregging equipment described in PART-083 and PART-086, equipment that would be destroyed by contact with molten basalt at 1,400°C and whose replacement would delay the Atlantis program by an estimated eighteen to twenty-four months, a programmatic risk that the containment pit — a 4-meter-deep, 6-meter-diameter reinforced concrete vault lined with 300 millimeters of high-alumina refractory castable (Al₂O₃ content > 90 percent, service temperature limit 1,800°C) and equipped with a 1,200-millimeter-diameter emergency drain pipe leading to an outdoor secondary containment basin — is designed to eliminate.

The melting of the basalt feedstock is performed in a continuous electrically-heated furnace, a design that rejects the alternative of a gas-fired furnace — which would introduce combustion products (CO₂, H₂O, SO₂ from the sulfur content of the natural gas fuel) into the melt atmosphere, causing chemical reactions with the molten basalt that alter its redox state (specifically, the Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺ ratio, which affects the melt's viscosity and the crystallization behavior of the resulting fiber, with a higher Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺ ratio — i.e., more oxidized conditions — increasing the melt's tendency to crystallize iron-bearing phases such as magnetite and hematite during cooling, which would create crystalline inclusions in the amorphous basalt fiber that act as stress concentrators and reduce the fiber's tensile strength, a detrimental effect that the electrically-heated furnace avoids by operating with a neutral atmosphere above the melt, maintained by a continuous purge of dry nitrogen gas at a flow rate of 50 liters per minute that displaces atmospheric oxygen and maintains the oxygen partial pressure in the furnace headspace below 100 ppm, as measured by a zirconia oxygen sensor mounted in the furnace exhaust duct and interlocked to the furnace control system such that any increase in oxygen partial pressure above 500 ppm automatically increases the nitrogen purge flow rate and triggers an alarm in the control room, alerting the operator to a potential air leak into the furnace that could oxidize the melt and degrade the fiber quality — in favor of the electrically-heated design, which uses molybdenum disilicide (MoSi₂) resistance heating elements arranged in four independently-controlled zones (bottom, middle, top, and throat, where the throat zone heats the molten basalt immediately before it enters the platinum-rhodium bushing, ensuring that the melt temperature at the bushing entry is uniform to within ±5°C and that any thermal gradients introduced by the heat losses through the furnace walls are dissipated before the melt reaches the fiberization orifices), with each zone capable of delivering up to 150 kW of electrical power at 480 VAC, three-phase, for a total furnace power capacity of 600 kW, of which approximately 420 kW is required for continuous operation at the nominal production rate of 1,200 kilograms of basalt per twenty-four-hour operating day — a rate that corresponds to approximately 12 hours of AFP machine operation at the 100 kg/hour tow placement rate documented in PART-081, meaning that a single twenty-four-hour basalt melting campaign produces sufficient fiber for approximately 2 days of AFP operation, a production ratio that the Mojave production planning team uses to schedule the basalt fiber runs in batches of 72 hours (three furnace operating days, producing approximately 3,600 kilograms of fiber) followed by 120 hours of furnace cooldown, bushing inspection, and furnace refractory maintenance before the next production campaign, a duty cycle that ensures the furnace operates within its thermal fatigue life — the refractory lining, composed of high-alumina bricks (Al₂O₃ > 95 percent) with a service temperature limit of 1,750°C, experiences thermal expansion stresses during each heating and cooling cycle that, over hundreds of cycles, would cause cracking and spalling of the refractory surface, with the resulting refractory particles contaminating the melt and producing inclusions in the basalt fiber that would be detected by the fiber inspection system described below and would cause the rejection of the contaminated fiber production — and that the three-day / five-day duty cycle is designed to minimize by limiting the number of thermal cycles to approximately 120 per year, corresponding to a refractory replacement interval of approximately 3 years based on the refractory manufacturer's thermal cycling data for the specific high-alumina brick composition used in the Mojave furnace.

The fiberization bushing — the platinum-rhodium alloy plate containing the precisely-machined orifices through which the molten basalt is drawn by the winding machine to form continuous filaments — is the single most critical component of the fiber production line, because the dimensional accuracy of the orifices determines the filament diameter uniformity, which in turn determines the statistical distribution of fiber tensile strengths in the finished composite, a distribution whose lower tail — the weakest fibers in any given tow — governs the composite's failure initiation under load, because fiber-reinforced composites fail through a progressive fracture mechanism in which the weakest fibers break first, transferring their load to the neighboring fibers through shear stresses in the matrix, which in turn increases the stress on those neighboring fibers and causes them to break, a cascade of individual fiber fractures that culminates in macroscopic failure of the composite at a stress level that is determined not by the mean fiber strength but by the distribution of fiber strengths, specifically the Weibull modulus — the shape parameter of the Weibull probability distribution that describes the fiber strength variability, with a higher Weibull modulus indicating a narrower strength distribution and therefore a higher composite strength at any given probability of failure — which for basalt fibers is strongly influenced by the uniformity of the filament diameter, because both the measured tensile strength of an individual filament (which, for brittle materials such as basalt, is inversely proportional to the square root of the filament diameter according to the Griffith fracture criterion, such that a filament with a locally-thin section — a "neck" produced by a bushing orifice that is slightly larger than its neighbors, causing the melt to flow through that orifice at a higher rate and producing a larger-diameter filament that cools more slowly and is therefore more susceptible to devitrification — will fail at a lower applied stress than a filament of uniform diameter, not because the material properties are different but because the larger-diameter filament has a higher probability of containing a critical-sized flaw, a size effect that is inherent to brittle fracture statistics and that cannot be eliminated through material processing improvements, only through the reduction of filament diameter variability to the practical minimum) and the stress distribution among the individual filaments within a tow (which, for a tow of N filaments, is determined by the distribution of filament cross-sectional areas, with the larger-diameter filaments carrying a proportionally larger share of the applied load and therefore failing earlier in the progressive fracture sequence, a load sharing effect that accelerates the fracture cascade and reduces the composite's ultimate tensile strength below the value that would be predicted from the mean filament strength alone). The Mojave facility's bushing is fabricated from a platinum-rhodium alloy containing 10 percent rhodium by weight — the standard alloy for basalt fiber production bushings, selected for its combination of high melting point (approximately 1,850°C for the Pt-10Rh alloy, safely above the 1,400°C melt temperature), oxidation resistance (which is essential for the bushing tips, which are exposed to the ambient air in the fiberization chamber and which would rapidly oxidize and degrade if fabricated from a less noble metal, such as the nickel-based superalloys that are used for fiberglass bushings but that cannot withstand the higher temperatures and the corrosive nature of molten basalt, which contains dissolved iron oxide that attacks nickel-based alloys through a mechanism of grain boundary oxidation and sulfidation — the sulfur content of the basalt, originating from trace sulfide minerals in the parent rock, forming nickel sulfide eutectic phases at the grain boundaries that melt at temperatures as low as 645°C and cause catastrophic intergranular fracture of the bushing, a failure mode that was documented during the Soviet basalt fiber development program and that led to the universal adoption of platinum-rhodium alloys for basalt fiber production worldwide), and resistance to the corrosive attack of molten basalt at 1,400°C for extended periods — a critical property because the bushing is continuously immersed in the melt and must maintain its dimensional integrity for a service life of at least 12 months (approximately 3,600 operating hours) between rebuilds, during which time the orifices gradually enlarge due to the combined effects of high-temperature creep (the slow deformation of the platinum-rhodium alloy under the hydrostatic pressure of the molten basalt column above the bushing, which is approximately 0.02 MPa at the typical melt depth of 150 millimeters, a pressure that is small in absolute terms but that, when sustained for thousands of hours at 1,400°C, drives a measurable creep strain through the vacancy diffusion mechanism that is the dominant creep mode in platinum alloys at these temperatures, causing the orifice diameter to increase by approximately 0.015 millimeters per 1,000 operating hours, or approximately 0.05 millimeters over the bushing's 3,600-hour service life, an increase of approximately 2.5 percent relative to the nominal 2.0-millimeter orifice diameter, producing a corresponding increase in the filament diameter of approximately 2.5 percent if the winding speed is held constant, an increase that the process control system compensates for by gradually increasing the winding speed over the bushing's service life to maintain the target filament diameter of 9.0 ± 0.3 micrometers — the compensation algorithm being based on a linear model of bushing orifice growth derived from the bushing manufacturer's creep data and validated through periodic diameter measurements of fiber samples collected throughout each bushing's service life using a laser diffraction filament diameter gauge (Keyence LS-9000 series, 0.01-micrometer resolution, ±0.05-micrometer accuracy) that measures the diameter of each filament in the tow at a rate of 8,000 filaments per second as the tow passes through the gauge's measurement zone during the winding operation, the measured diameter distribution being compared to the specification limits and the winding speed being adjusted by a proportional-integral control algorithm executing on the winding machine's PLC to maintain the mean filament diameter at the 9.0-micrometer target) and the abrasive wear caused by the dissolution of the bushing alloy into the molten basalt at the orifice lip, a wear mechanism that removes approximately 0.5 milligrams of platinum-rhodium alloy per 1,000 operating hours, an amount that, while small relative to the bushing's total mass of approximately 8.5 kilograms, represents a material loss of approximately $60,000 per year at the 2026 platinum spot price of approximately $1,100 per troy ounce, a cost that the Atlantis program's material budget accounts for through a precious metal recovery agreement with the bushing manufacturer, Johnson Matthey precious metals division, which recycles the worn bushings and credits the recovered platinum and rhodium value — typically 92 to 95 percent of the original metal value, with the remaining 5 to 8 percent representing unrecoverable process losses — toward the purchase of replacement bushings, an arrangement that reduces the net annual bushing cost to approximately $45,000, a sum that is included in the pod fabrication cost breakdown documented in PART-094.

The bushing is equipped with 800 precisely-machined orifices arranged in a rectangular array of 20 rows by 40 columns on a 4.0-millimeter pitch, each orifice having a nominal diameter of 2.0 millimeters at the entry side (facing the melt) and a precisely-controlled exit tip geometry — a conical taper from the 2.0-millimeter entry diameter to a 0.8-millimeter tip diameter over a length of 3.0 millimeters, corresponding to a cone half-angle of approximately 11.3 degrees, an angle that has been optimized through computational fluid dynamics (CFD) modeling of the molten basalt flow through the orifice, using the ANSYS Fluent software package with a non-Newtonian viscosity model calibrated against the experimentally-measured viscosity-temperature relationship of the specific Cascade Basalt composition used in the Atlantis program — to produce a stable melt meniscus at the tip from which the filament is drawn, a stability that is essential for continuous fiber production because any oscillation of the meniscus, caused by variations in the melt flow rate through the orifice or by external perturbations such as air currents in the fiberization chamber, produces corresponding oscillations in the filament diameter — a phenomenon known as "draw resonance" in the fiber production literature, characterized by a periodic variation in filament diameter at a frequency determined by the interaction between the melt's viscoelastic properties and the fiber tension, with the oscillation amplitude increasing with draw ratio (the ratio of the winding speed to the melt extrusion speed at the orifice tip) until, at a critical draw ratio that depends on the melt's relaxation time — the characteristic time for the polymer-like melt structure to relax from an imposed deformation, which for molten basalt at 1,400°C is on the order of 0.01 seconds based on the fiber industry's empirical correlations — the oscillation becomes unstable and the filament breaks, a failure mode that limits the maximum practical draw ratio to approximately 10,000:1 for basalt fibers, corresponding to the 9.0-micrometer filament diameter produced from a 0.8-millimeter tip diameter at the winding speed of approximately 35 meters per second (2,100 meters per minute), a speed that represents the current state of the art for basalt fiber production and that is approximately twice the winding speed achieved by commercial basalt fiber manufacturers producing fibers for construction and automotive applications, where the specifications for filament diameter uniformity and tensile strength are less demanding than for the aerospace-grade fiber required by the Atlantis program, and where the lower winding speed reflects a deliberate economic trade-off between fiber quality (which decreases with increasing winding speed because the shorter residence time of the filament in the cooling zone before sizing application results in higher residual thermal stresses in the amorphous fiber structure, stresses that increase the fiber's susceptibility to stress corrosion cracking in the presence of moisture — a degradation mechanism that is particularly relevant for the Atlantis application, where the fibers will be immersed in seawater for fifty years, and that motivates the moisture-resistant resin and sizing chemistry described in PART-086 and the hydrophobic silane treatment that reduces moisture ingress to the fiber-matrix interface) and production cost (which decreases with increasing winding speed because the same bushing can produce more fiber per unit time, amortizing the bushing cost across a larger fiber output and reducing the fiber cost per kilogram). The Mojave facility's choice of 35 meters per second as the winding speed represents the optimal balance point determined through a designed experiment (DOE) varying winding speed from 20 to 50 meters per second in 5 m/s increments and measuring the resulting filament diameter uniformity, tensile strength (by single-filament tensile testing per ASTM C1557, with a 10-millimeter gauge length and a strain rate of 0.01 per second, testing 100 filaments per sample to characterize the Weibull strength distribution), and the interlaminar shear strength of LBFRP-001 composite laminates fabricated from fiber produced at each winding speed, with the results indicating that fiber tensile strength decreases approximately linearly from a maximum of 4,200 MPa at 20 m/s to 3,500 MPa at 50 m/s — a decrease of approximately 17 percent, attributed to the increasing residual thermal stress in the faster-cooled fibers — while the interlaminar shear strength of the composite decreases from 68 MPa at 20 m/s to 60 MPa at 50 m/s, a decrease of approximately 12 percent attributed to the reduced time available at the higher winding speeds for the silane sizing to react with the fiber surface and form the covalent bonds that are the basis of the fiber-matrix adhesion, with the 35 m/s operating point selected as the speed that achieves a fiber tensile strength of 4,050 ± 80 MPa and a composite interlaminar shear strength of 66 ± 2 MPa, values that exceed the LBFRP-001 material specification minima of 3,800 MPa tensile strength and 65 MPa interlaminar shear strength by comfortable margins (approximately 6.6 percent and 1.5 percent respectively, margins that account for the statistical variability of the fiber production process and the composite fabrication process as characterized by the process capability studies documented in PART-088) while maintaining a commercially viable fiber production rate of approximately 50 kilograms per hour per bushing (800 filaments × 9-micrometer diameter × 35 m/s draw speed × 2.7 g/cm³ basalt density × 3,600 seconds per hour / 1,000 grams per kilogram), which for the Mojave facility's two installed bushings (operating in parallel, each supplied from its own independent heating zone in the furnace throat to ensure uniform melt temperature at both bushing positions) yields a total fiber production rate of approximately 100 kilograms per hour, matching the AFP cell's tow placement rate and enabling a balanced production flow from fiber drawing through composite lamination without requiring an inventory buffer of intermediate fiber spools, a just-in-time production philosophy that minimizes the capital tied up in work-in-progress inventory and that reduces the risk of fiber property degradation during extended storage — basalt fibers, unlike carbon fibers, are hygroscopic to a limited degree (absorbing approximately 0.1 to 0.3 percent moisture by weight when exposed to ambient humidity), and prolonged storage in uncontrolled humidity conditions can degrade the fiber surface chemistry, reducing the effectiveness of the silane sizing applied during fiber drawing and compromising the fiber-matrix adhesion in the finished composite, a degradation that the just-in-time production approach avoids by ensuring that fiber is typically converted into composite within 72 hours of drawing, well within the sizing manufacturer's recommended maximum storage period of 30 days at 23°C and 50 percent relative humidity, beyond which the sizing's reactivity decreases measurably due to the gradual hydrolysis of the silane functional groups by ambient moisture — a hydrolysis reaction that proceeds slowly at room temperature but that, over weeks to months, consumes the silane molecules that are essential for the chemical bonding between the basalt fiber surface and the Elium resin matrix, a bonding that the fluorinated silane coupling agent described in PART-086 is specifically designed to protect by forming a hydrophobic barrier at the fiber-matrix interface that resists the ingress of water molecules and thereby preserves the hydrolytic stability of the silane bonds over the fifty-year immersion service life, a barrier whose effectiveness depends on the silane being applied to a freshly-drawn fiber surface that has not been pre-hydrolyzed by moisture exposure during storage, a requirement that is satisfied by the just-in-time production philosophy but that would be violated if fiber spools were stockpiled for weeks before use.

The sizing application system applies the silane coupling agent — an aqueous solution of 3-aminopropyltriethoxysilane (APTES) at a concentration of 1.0 percent by weight in deionized water, adjusted to pH 4.0 with acetic acid to promote the hydrolysis of the ethoxy groups to silanol groups, which then condense with the hydroxyl groups on the basalt fiber surface to form covalent siloxane (Si-O-Si) bonds, the chemical mechanism that is the basis of the adhesion between the basalt fiber and the Elium resin matrix and that has been studied extensively through X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis of the fiber surface before and after sizing application, confirming the presence of the characteristic Si-O-Si absorption bands at 1,000-1,100 cm⁻¹ in the FTIR spectrum and the nitrogen 1s XPS peak at a binding energy of 399.5 eV corresponding to the amine functional group of the APTES molecule, with the intensity of these spectroscopic signatures correlating with the measured interlaminar shear strength of the resulting composite, providing a quantitative spectroscopic quality control metric that the Mojave fiber production line monitors by periodically sampling sized fiber from the winding station and analyzing it via a portable FTIR spectrometer (Agilent 4300 Handheld FTIR with a diamond attenuated total reflectance (ATR) accessory) configured to acquire spectra in the range of 650 to 4,000 cm⁻¹ at a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹ with 32 co-added scans per spectrum, comparing the measured Si-O-Si peak area to a reference standard established from fibers that were used to produce composite laminates with validated interlaminar shear strength exceeding the 65 MPa specification minimum — a spectroscopic quality control approach that provides a rapid, non-destructive, in-line verification of sizing quality that complements the destructive interlaminar shear strength testing that is performed on witness coupons fabricated alongside each pod hull, as described in PART-087, and that enables the fiber production operator to detect and correct sizing application problems — such as a depleted sizing solution concentration, a plugged sizing applicator nozzle, or a degraded sizing solution due to bacterial growth in the aqueous sizing bath — within minutes of the problem's onset rather than waiting for the destructive test results, which require the composite laminate to be cured and machined into test specimens, a process that takes approximately 24 hours and that, if a sizing problem is discovered at that point, would require the rejection of an entire day's fiber production, representing a material loss of approximately 2,400 kilograms of fiber worth approximately $36,000 at the Atlantis program's basalt fiber cost of $15 per kilogram (approximately three times the cost of commercial-grade basalt fiber, reflecting the tighter quality specifications, the lower production rate, and the more expensive sizing chemistry of the aerospace-grade Atlantis fiber), a loss that the in-line spectroscopic monitoring is designed to prevent by providing early warning of sizing quality degradation, enabling the operator to correct the problem after the loss of at most a few minutes of production rather than an entire day.

The fiber winding system collects the sized filaments into a tow — a bundle of 800 filaments, corresponding to one filament from each bushing orifice, combined into a single continuous strand with a linear density of approximately 140 tex (grams per 1,000 meters) — and winds the tow onto a 3-inch (76-millimeter) internal diameter cardboard core at a winding tension of 2.0 ± 0.2 newtons, using a precision traverse mechanism that guides the tow back and forth across the spool width of 254 millimeters (10 inches) at a traverse rate synchronized with the spool rotational speed to produce a cross-wound package with a constant winding angle of approximately 15 degrees, a winding pattern that prevents the tow from embedding itself into the underlying layers — a defect known as "cinching" that occurs when the winding tension causes the tow to cut into the previously wound layers, pinching and damaging the filaments at the crossing points and creating weak spots in the tow that would cause breaks during the high-speed AFP layup process described in PART-081, where the tow is pulled from the spool at speeds up to 1.2 meters per second and any filament breakage at a cinch point would propagate along the tow, causing a complete tow break that halts the AFP machine and requires operator intervention to rethread the tow, a thirty-minute interruption that, if it occurred once per hull, would extend the production schedule by only half an hour — an acceptable impact — but that, if the cinching was systematic because of incorrect winding parameters, could occur multiple times per hull, potentially adding days to the production schedule and jeopardizing the eighteen-month demonstration program timeline, a risk that the precise winding tension control — achieved through a closed-loop tension control system using a load cell (Interface Model 1210, 10 N capacity, identical to the load cells used in the AFP tow tension control described in PART-081, providing commonality of spare parts and maintenance procedures across the fiber production and AFP cells) measuring the tow tension at the winding point and a PID controller adjusting the speed of the winding spindle drive motor to maintain the setpoint — is designed to eliminate, and that the cross-wound package geometry further mitigates by ensuring that successive layers of tow are laid down at an angle to each other, distributing the contact pressure between layers across a larger area and reducing the tendency of the tow to embed into the underlying layers compared to a level-wound package where successive layers are parallel and the full winding tension is concentrated along a line contact between each tow and the layer beneath it. Each completed spool contains approximately 8,200 meters of continuous 800-filament basalt tow, corresponding to a spool mass of approximately 1.15 kilograms (8,200 m × 140 tex / 1,000 g/kg) excluding the cardboard core, a quantity that is matched to the AFP creel cabinet's spool capacity described in PART-081 and that provides approximately 10 minutes of AFP operation at the maximum layup rate before spool changeover is required — a changeover interval that the production planning team has determined is the optimal balance between the productivity loss from frequent spool changes (each changeover takes approximately 5 minutes, during which the AFP machine is idle, representing a 5/10 = 50 percent downtime fraction that would be unacceptably high) and the quality risk from extended spool run time (which increases the probability of a tow break or a tension control excursion during the spool's operating life, as the probability of encountering a filament weak point increases linearly with the length of tow on the spool), resulting in the current spool specification of 8,200 meters and the changeover downtime fraction of approximately 5/(5 + 10 × (100/7.6)) ≈ 3.6 percent — the denominator representing the average time between spool changes adjusted for the fact that the AFP machine lays up 8 tows simultaneously from 8 spools, and only one spool at a time reaches its end, such that the average interval between ANY spool reaching its end is the spool run time divided by 8, or approximately 1.25 minutes, meaning that the operator is changing a spool somewhere in the creel cabinet every 1.25 minutes during continuous operation, which is feasible only because the creel cabinet is designed with a "flying splice" capability — a mechanism that automatically splices the trailing end of the expiring spool to the leading end of the replacement spool without requiring the AFP machine to pause, using an ultrasonic splice technique that momentarily compresses the two tow ends between a titanium sonotrode vibrating at 20 kHz, generating frictional heat that melts the Elium thermoplastic resin on both tow ends and fuses them into a continuous joint with a tensile strength of approximately 85 percent of the unspliced tow's strength, sufficient to withstand the 15-newton layup tension with a factor of safety of approximately 5, a splice that the in-process inspection system described in PART-088 monitors by detecting the slight increase in tow thickness at the splice location — approximately 15 percent thicker than the unspliced tow — and flagging the splice for the operator to verify that the splice has been properly consolidated by the compaction roller, a verification that the operator performs by visually inspecting the laminate surface at the splice location at the next scheduled inspection pause, approximately every 2 hours of AFP operation, and confirming that the splice is flush with the surrounding laminate and that no dry fiber or resin-rich pocket is visible at the splice point, a quality check that adds approximately 30 seconds per splice to the operator's workload and that is essential for ensuring that the splices do not become defect initiation sites in the finished laminate, where a poorly-consolidated splice could create a planar void between adjacent plies that would act as a delamination initiation site under the hydrostatic compressive loading that the hull experiences at the 45-meter deployment depth, a defect that the ultrasonic inspection system described in PART-087 would detect as a localized region of high ultrasonic attenuation corresponding to the splice location, flagging it for rework if the attenuation exceeded the acceptance threshold specified in the LBFRP-001 defect acceptance criteria documented in PART-088.

The Soviet military heritage of basalt fiber production — a heritage that the Atlantis program acknowledges not merely as a historical curiosity but as a direct technical lineage that informs the Mojave facility's process parameters, quality control methodologies, and equipment design choices — originated with the Soviet Union's strategic materials program of the 1960s, which identified basalt fiber as a domestically-available, low-cost alternative to Western carbon and aramid fibers for military applications including missile nose cones, rocket motor casings, submarine hulls, and ballistic armor for armored vehicles and personnel. The Soviet basalt fiber development program, centered at the Kiev Institute under the direction of Dr. Dzhigiris and his research group, made several fundamental contributions to basalt fiber science and technology that the Atlantis program has inherited and advanced: the development of the platinum-rhodium bushing technology described above, which the Kiev Institute developed through an empirical research program that systematically varied bushing alloy composition, orifice geometry, and melt temperature to determine the optimal parameters for continuous filament drawing from a wide range of Soviet basalt compositions sourced from quarries in Ukraine, the Russian Far East, and the Caucasus Mountains, a body of empirical data that the Atlantis program has augmented with modern CFD and finite element analysis (FEA) modeling of the melt flow and heat transfer within the bushing, producing a level of understanding of the fiberization process that was unattainable with the experimental and analytical tools available to the Soviet researchers but that validates and refines the empirical relationships they established, confirming for example that the optimal bushing orifice geometry for basalt fiber production is a conical entry with a length-to-diameter ratio of approximately 1.5 to 2.0 (the Mojave bushing's 3.0 mm length / 2.0 mm entry diameter = 1.5 ratio falling exactly within this range), a geometry that minimizes the pressure drop across the bushing — which would reduce the melt flow rate and therefore the fiber production rate if the pressure drop were excessive — while providing sufficient residence time for the melt temperature to equilibrate within the orifice before fiberization, ensuring uniform filament temperature and therefore uniform viscosity at the point of drawing, which is essential for filament diameter uniformity; the development of the sizing chemistry that bonds the basalt fiber to organic resin matrices, a challenge that the Soviet researchers addressed through the application of organosilane coupling agents — specifically, gamma-aminopropyltriethoxysilane, the same APTES chemistry that the Atlantis program uses, a testament to the fundamental correctness of the Soviet research — which they applied to the fiber surface immediately after drawing, while the fiber was still at an elevated temperature of approximately 200-300°C, the residual heat promoting the condensation reaction between the silanol groups and the fiber surface hydroxyl groups, a process that the Mojave facility replicates through the placement of the sizing applicator nozzle within 300 millimeters of the bushing tip, where the fiber temperature is approximately 250°C as measured by an infrared pyrometer aimed at the filament bundle immediately above the sizing applicator, ensuring that the sizing chemistry benefits from the same thermally-accelerated reaction kinetics that the Soviet researchers exploited, a reaction that proceeds approximately 100 times faster at 250°C than at room temperature according to the Arrhenius kinetics of the silanol condensation reaction (activation energy approximately 60 kJ/mol as measured by the Kiev Institute through a series of experiments in which sized fibers were cured at temperatures ranging from 25°C to 300°C and the degree of silane bonding was quantified through the weight loss upon solvent extraction of the unreacted silane, a methodology that the Mojave quality control laboratory has replicated using Soxhlet extraction with toluene as the solvent, confirming that the degree of silane bonding achieved at the Mojave facility matches the Soviet reference values within the measurement uncertainty of the extraction method); and the characterization of the relationship between basalt chemical composition and fiber properties, a monumental empirical effort in which the Kiev Institute collected basalt samples from dozens of Soviet quarries, characterized their chemical composition through wet chemical analysis (the Soviet analog of modern XRF spectroscopy, and the source of the composition-fiber property correlations that the Atlantis program has validated through its own XRF and fiber property testing program on the Cascade Basalt composition), produced experimental fiber batches from each composition using a laboratory-scale single-orifice bushing — the precursor of the 800-orifice production bushing used at Mojave — and measured the resulting fiber properties (tensile strength, elastic modulus, chemical durability as measured by weight loss after immersion in boiling water for 3 hours — a test that the Atlantis program has modified to a 5,000-hour immersion test in synthetic seawater at 60°C, as described in PART-086, to provide a more relevant assessment of long-term fiber durability in the Atlantis application), establishing the correlations that guide the selection of basalt compositions for specific applications: higher SiO₂ content increases the fiber's chemical durability but reduces its tensile strength because of the lower concentration of network-modifying cations (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Fe²⁺) that disrupt the aluminosilicate glass network and reduce the melt's viscosity at a given temperature, requiring higher fiberization temperatures that increase the rate of bushing degradation; higher Al₂O₃ content increases the fiber's elastic modulus (from approximately 80 GPa at 15 percent Al₂O₃ to approximately 95 GPa at 20 percent Al₂O₃) but makes the melt more prone to crystallization — specifically, the precipitation of plagioclase feldspar crystals (albite-anorthite solid solution series) on cooling, which degrade the fiber's tensile strength because the crystal-matrix interface is a source of critical-sized flaws — requiring a narrower composition window for reliable fiber production; higher Fe₂O₃ content increases the fiber density and darkens its color (the characteristic brown-green color of basalt fiber, which the Soviet researchers found to be aesthetically unappealing but which the Atlantis program accepts as functionally irrelevant because the fiber is embedded within an opaque composite laminate and is never seen by the pod occupants) but also increases the fiber's absorption of infrared radiation, which is relevant for the AFP laser heating system described in PART-081 because the basalt fiber must absorb the 980 nm laser radiation to heat the surrounding Elium resin — a coupling between the fiber's chemical composition and the AFP process that the Soviet researchers could not have anticipated, because the laser-heated AFP technology postdates their work by three decades, but that the Atlantis program's process engineering team discovered during the initial AFP trials on basalt-Elium prepreg, when the laser heating parameters that had been optimized for carbon fiber prepreg (which absorbs 980 nm radiation very efficiently because of its black color) proved inadequate for basalt fiber prepreg (which absorbs 980 nm radiation less efficiently because of its lighter color and different electronic structure), requiring an increase in laser power from the 2.5 kW used for carbon fiber to the 4.0 kW specified for the Atlantis AFP machines, an adjustment that the Soviet-era composition-property correlations — which included spectral reflectance measurements of basalt fibers in the visible and near-infrared range as part of the Soviet program's comprehensive fiber characterization effort — enabled the Atlantis process engineers to predict and to compensate for during the AFP machine commissioning phase, eliminating what would otherwise have been a time-consuming empirical optimization process and saving an estimated three months of production schedule, a tangible benefit derived from the Soviet material science legacy that underlies the Atlantis program's basalt fiber technology.

The CSM advancement of the Soviet basalt fiber technology — the specific innovations that the Carrington Storm Motors engineering team has contributed to the state of the art, building upon the Soviet foundation to produce a fiber that meets the aerospace-grade quality standards required for a subsea habitation module — comprises three principal developments. First, the implementation of a fully-automated, PLC-controlled fiber production line that replaces the largely manual operation of the Soviet-era pilot plants with closed-loop control of every process parameter that affects fiber quality: melt temperature is controlled to ±2°C at each of the four furnace heating zones through cascade PID loops that adjust the MoSi₂ heating element power based on the feedback from type-B thermocouples (Pt-30Rh/Pt-6Rh) immersed directly in the melt at each zone, with the setpoint for each zone determined by a supervisory model-predictive control (MPC) algorithm that solves the inverse heat transfer problem — given the desired temperature profile in the melt, calculate the required heating element power for each zone, accounting for the thermal interactions between zones through a dynamic model of the furnace's thermal behavior identified from step-response experiments — to maintain the optimal temperature profile that maximizes fiber quality while minimizing energy consumption, a dual-objective optimization that the Soviet-era manual control could not approach and that reduces the furnace's electrical energy consumption by approximately 18 percent compared to the constant-power heating profile that the Mojave facility used during its initial commissioning phase before the MPC implementation, a saving of approximately $85,000 per year at the Mojave facility's industrial electricity rate of $0.08 per kilowatt-hour, an operational cost reduction that partially offsets the higher cost of the aerospace-grade basalt fiber relative to commercial grades. Second, the development of an in-line filament diameter measurement and feedback control system that continuously adjusts the winding speed to maintain the target filament diameter, as described above, a capability that did not exist in the Soviet plants (which relied on periodic manual sampling and off-line microscopic measurement of filament diameter, with the winding speed set at the beginning of each production run and not adjusted thereafter, resulting in a gradual increase in filament diameter over the course of a production run as the bushing orifices enlarged through creep and wear, an increase that produced fiber with a systematically declining tensile strength over the run, a trend that the Soviet researchers documented in their publications but that they lacked the measurement and control technology to correct in real time, and that the Atlantis program's in-line diameter control eliminates by continuously measuring and adjusting, maintaining the mean filament diameter within the ±0.3-micrometer tolerance band throughout the bushing's service life, a uniformity that the Weibull analysis of single-filament tensile test data confirms produces a narrower strength distribution (Weibull modulus of 8.5 ± 0.8 for the Atlantis fiber versus approximately 5.0 for the best Soviet-era fiber produced with periodic diameter control, a difference that translates to a composite strength increase of approximately 12 percent at the 0.1 percent probability of failure level that is the design basis for the Atlantis hull per the probabilistic design methodology described in the parent document's Section 5, a strength increase that directly improves the hull's factor of safety and that justifies the additional capital cost of the in-line diameter measurement and control system — approximately $180,000 for the laser diffraction gauge, the winding machine PLC upgrade, and the software integration — through the increased reliability of the deployed pods). Third, the qualification of the basalt fiber for a fifty-year service life in continuous seawater immersion at elevated hydrostatic pressure — a qualification that the Soviet program never performed because its military applications (missile components, rocket motor casings, vehicle armor) did not require extended immersion durability, and that the Atlantis program has accomplished through a comprehensive accelerated aging program in which basalt fiber tows, sized with the APTES silane and embedded in Elium resin coupons (prepared by the same AFP process used for production hulls, to ensure that the aging data is representative of the actual fabrication process rather than of idealized laboratory-scale specimens that might exhibit different aging behavior because of differences in void content, fiber volume fraction, or fiber-matrix interface quality), have been immersed in synthetic seawater (prepared per ASTM D1141, a standardized formulation that mimics the major ion composition of natural seawater — Na⁺, K⁺, Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺, Cl⁻, SO₄²⁻, HCO₃⁻ — at a salinity of 35 parts per thousand, the average salinity of the world ocean and representative of both the Monterey and Osa deployment sites, as confirmed by conductivity-temperature-depth (CTD) profiles collected during the site surveys described in Sections 3 and 4 of the parent document) at a temperature of 60°C and a hydrostatic pressure of 4.5 MPa (the design external pressure at the Monterey deployment depth, applied to the aging specimens through a custom-built pressure vessel equipped with a high-pressure seawater circulation loop that maintains the temperature and pressure at the specified setpoints and that periodically samples the circulating seawater for chemical analysis — pH, dissolved oxygen, chloride concentration, and the concentration of any elements leached from the basalt fiber, indicating fiber degradation — to monitor the progression of the aging process) for a duration of 10,000 hours, representing approximately fifty years of equivalent service life at the 10-15°C ambient temperature of the deployment sites, per the Arrhenius acceleration model with an activation energy of 55 kJ/mol derived from the water absorption kinetics of the Elium matrix and the hydrolytic degradation kinetics of the silane bond at the fiber-matrix interface, which have been separately characterized through experiments at four temperatures (25, 40, 60, and 80°C) to establish the validity of the Arrhenius extrapolation across the temperature range of interest, an experimental design that is consistent with the ISO 10952 standard for accelerated aging of fiber-reinforced polymer composites in marine environments and that has been reviewed and accepted by the ABS/DNV/Lloyds certification authority as part of the certification pathway described in PART-120, providing the regulatory confidence that the fifty-year design life is technically justified rather than aspirational. The results of the 10,000-hour accelerated aging program — which is approximately 80 percent complete at the time of this document's publication, with the remaining 2,000 hours scheduled to conclude concurrently with the fabrication of the first demonstration pod hull, a timing that ensures that any unexpected aging phenomena discovered in the final phase of the test program can be incorporated into the hull design before the pod is committed to deployment — indicate that the basalt fiber retains more than 95 percent of its initial tensile strength after the equivalent of fifty years of seawater immersion, that the interlaminar shear strength of the composite retains more than 92 percent of its initial value, and that no systematic change in the fiber's chemical composition or surface morphology is detectable through XPS or scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis of fiber surfaces exposed by fracturing the aged composite coupons under liquid nitrogen to expose fresh fiber surfaces without disturbing the fiber-matrix interface, results that the Aegis C safety analysis interprets as confirming the adequacy of the basalt fiber's seawater durability for the Atlantis application, subject to the final confirmation that will be provided by the completion of the 10,000-hour aging program and the subsequent analysis of the fully-aged specimens.

The Soviet military heritage of basalt fiber is acknowledged here not as a historical curiosity but as a technical debt — the Atlantis program stands on the shoulders of the Soviet material scientists who, working under conditions of resource scarcity and political constraint that the Mojave facility's engineers cannot fully appreciate, established the fundamental principles that make basalt fiber viable as a structural material for subsea habitation. The Kiev Institute's researchers documented their findings in a series of publications in the Soviet journal "Steklo i Keramika" (Glass and Ceramics) and in classified technical reports that were declassified after the dissolution of the Soviet Union and that have been translated into English by the CSM technical library staff, providing the Atlantis program with access to six decades of empirical data that would have cost hundreds of millions of dollars and decades of effort to replicate independently, and that the Atlantis program has validated, refined, and extended through the application of modern computational modeling, automated process control, and accelerated aging test methods that were not available to the Soviet researchers but that are essential for qualifying a material for the extreme demands of the Atlantis application. The basalt fiber that emerges from the Mojave furnace — 9 micrometers in diameter, 4,050 MPa in tensile strength, 89 GPa in elastic modulus, chemically bonded to the Elium resin through covalent siloxane linkages, and verified through 10,000 hours of accelerated aging to retain its properties for fifty years in the deep ocean — is the product of a technological lineage that extends from the Soviet missile silos to the Atlantis seabed, a lineage that the program honors by acknowledging its origins while pursuing its own advancements, and that will culminate in a habitation module whose structural integrity depends on the quality of every one of the trillions of continuous basalt filaments whose production is described in the preceding paragraphs, each filament contributing its micro-scale share of the macro-scale burden of resisting the crushing pressure of the deep sea, the battering impact of turbidite-borne boulders, and the slow degradation of saltwater immersion across a half-century of service on the floor of the ocean, beneath a world that may, at any moment, be torn apart by the forces of crustal displacement that the Atlantis pods are built to survive.
