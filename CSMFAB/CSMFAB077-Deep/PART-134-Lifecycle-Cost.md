# CSMFAB077 — PART 134: LIFECYCLE COST — 50-YEAR OPERATIONAL SPAN

**Parent:** CSMFAB077 | **Part:** 134 of 160 | **Block:** 12 | **Aegis C** | **July 2026**

The 50-year lifecycle cost of $112 million for the combined Monterey and Osa demonstration communities — $62 million for Monterey (6 pods), $50 million for Osa (4 pods) — reflects the cumulative net present value of all costs incurred from the initial design phase through the final decommissioning of the pods, calculated using a real discount rate of 3 percent (the US Office of Management and Budget's Circular A-94 recommended rate for public investment analysis, applied here because the Atlantis program's government partners at FEMA and DHS require the lifecycle cost to be evaluated using the federal government's standard discounting methodology for the purposes of the public-private partnership funding model, and because the program's own financial analysis has demonstrated that the choice of discount rate — within the range of 2 to 5 percent that is conventionally used for long-horizon infrastructure investments — has a relatively modest effect on the lifecycle cost estimate, varying from $98 million at 5 percent to $128 million at 2 percent, a 31 percent sensitivity that is typical for projects with large upfront capital costs and a long stream of relatively constant annual operating costs, precisely the cost profile that characterizes the Atlantis program) and incorporating the replacement cycles for the major subsystems: the LiOH canisters (replaced every 30 days during occupancy, 10 replacement cycles per deployment year, at $380 per canister for 3 canisters per day for 90 days = 270 canisters per deployment, 2,700 canisters per year at $1.03 million per year, totaling $51.5 million over 50 years — the single largest operational cost category, exceeding even the crew salaries, and the primary driver of the program's research into regenerative CO₂ scrubbing technologies (the MEA liquid scrubber described in PART-121, which uses steam stripping to regenerate the scrubbing solution, reducing the LiOH consumption by approximately 80 percent when the MEA system is operational, an improvement that would save approximately $41 million over the 50-year lifecycle but that requires the continuous availability of the steam stripping system's electrical power, which the Grid-Seed ocean-current turbine supplies at a cost of approximately $0.15 per kilowatt-hour — accounting for the turbine's capital cost amortization, the battery pack replacement every 8 years, and the maintenance labor — or approximately $13,000 per year for the steam stripping system's 10 kW continuous power draw, compared to the $1.03 million per year LiOH cost, a ratio of approximately 79:1 in favor of the regenerative scrubbing, a savings that the Zirconia analysis describes as "the most economically compelling argument for the regenerative scrubbing technology investment" and that the program's research partners at the University of California, San Diego's Scripps Institution of Oceanography — who are developing a next-generation MEA scrubbing system with a 50 percent higher CO₂ absorption capacity per unit volume of scrubbing solution, utilizing a proprietary amine blend (a mixture of monoethanolamine, piperazine, and 2-amino-2-methyl-1-propanol) that was identified through a computational screening of more than 10,000 candidate amine compounds using density functional theory calculations of the amine-CO₂ reaction energetics, a screening that was performed on the Carr-Library high-performance computing cluster and that required approximately 2 million core-hours of computation, an investment of approximately $60,000 in computing cost that identified the optimal amine blend, which the Scripps team has subsequently validated through bench-scale testing in a 5-liter scrubbing column, demonstrating a 50 percent improvement in the CO₂ mass transfer coefficient compared to the conventional 30-weight-percent MEA solution, a breakthrough that the program's technology roadmap (PART-138) projects will reduce the Atlantis lifecycle cost by approximately $28 million when the new scrubbing system is retrofitted to the existing pods during the first major overhaul cycle in Year 10); the fuel cell membrane-electrode assemblies (replaced every 5 years at $12,000 per pod, totaling $1.2 million over 50 years); the battery pack cells (replaced every 8 years at $45,000 per pod, totaling $3.4 million); the viewport acrylic panels (replaced every 10 years at $95,000 per pod, the cost including the PMMA procurement, the CNC machining of the spherical sector geometry to the optical finish specification of λ/4 at 589 nanometers — the sodium D-line, the standard wavelength for optical surface quality measurement — and the pressure testing to 6.0 ATA, with the replaced panels being returned to the surface for recycling — the PMMA is ground into pellets and reprocessed into new acrylic products, a sustainability measure that the Atlantis environmental compliance program (PART-142) requires for all major consumable components, and that the program's materials procurement contract with the acrylic supplier (Röhm GmbH, Darmstadt, Germany, the manufacturer of the PLEXIGLAS GS 249 material specified for the viewports) includes as a contractual obligation: the supplier accepts the returned panels at no cost to the Atlantis program and credits the material recovery value — approximately $2,500 per panel — against the cost of the replacement panels, a closed-loop material management practice that reduces the viewport replacement cost by approximately 3 percent and, more importantly, eliminates the environmental impact of disposing of 4 large PMMA panels (each weighing approximately 80 kilograms) every 10 years, a practice that the program's sustainability officer describes as "a small but symbolically important demonstration of the circular economy principles that we believe should govern all Aegis C operations — nothing from the pod should end up in a landfill, because the pod's purpose is to preserve life, and that purpose is contradicted by disposing of its components in a way that harms the environment that the pod is designed to survive within"); and the decommissioning cost of $8.5 million at Year 50 (the cost of the ROV-assisted disconnection of the pods from their anchoring systems, the controlled buoyant ascent of each pod to the surface using the ballast tank blow system — the ballast tanks are blown with compressed air from the surface support vessel, and the pods ascend at a controlled rate of 0.3 meters per second, monitored by the acoustic positioning system to ensure that the pods do not tumble or collide during the ascent, with the ascent from 45 meters taking approximately 2.5 minutes per pod, after which the pods are lifted aboard the Charlemagne heavy-lift vessel and transported to the Mojave facility, where they are disassembled, their materials recycled (the LBFRP-001 composite is ground into aggregate for road construction — a somewhat ignominious end for a material that survived the ultimate test of the Episode Twenty-One extreme environments, but the program's materials engineers have determined that the composite cannot be economically recycled into new structural components because the Elium thermoplastic matrix cross-links irreversibly after 50 years of exposure to seawater, pressure, and radiation, and the composite's residual mechanical properties, while still impressive (tensile strength approximately 60 percent of the original 340 MPa), are insufficient for re-use in a pressure-rated application, a degradation that was anticipated in the original materials selection — the 50-year design life was chosen specifically because the composite's properties degrade to approximately 60 percent of their original values at 50 years, as predicted by the Arrhenius aging model described in PART-121, and the pod's structural design was correspondingly conservative, with a factor of safety of 1.67 on the ultimate strength at 50 years, ensuring that the pod remains safe throughout its design life even as the composite slowly degrades — and the MXene coating, which has protected the hull from chloride ion penetration for 50 years, will have been largely consumed by the sacrificial anodic reaction, its once-uniform 2-millimeter thickness reduced to a mottled, discontinuous layer averaging 0.3 millimeters, still providing some corrosion protection but approaching the end of its effective life, a testament to the MXene coating's durability that the program's materials scientists intend to document through detailed post-decommissioning analysis, contributing to the scientific literature on long-term MXene performance in marine environments, a contribution that will benefit future underwater structures, from offshore wind turbine foundations to deep-sea mining equipment, long after the Atlantis pods themselves have been dismantled and recycled into roads and park benches and the memories of the people who lived in them.
