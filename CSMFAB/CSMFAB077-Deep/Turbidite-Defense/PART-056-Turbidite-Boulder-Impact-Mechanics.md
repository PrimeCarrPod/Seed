The penetration depth — the distance that the boulder penetrates into the auxetic cladding before the cladding arrests the boulder's motion — is estimated from the energy balance: the boulder's kinetic energy (7,640 J) is absorbed by the plastic work of the cladding's cell-wall buckling and the viscoelastic dissipation in the TPU matrix, with the specific energy absorption (SEA) of the auxetic TPU — the energy absorbed per unit volume of the cladding material — being approximately 12 to 18 MJ/m³ for the 50 kg/m³ density, re-entrant honeycomb TPU at the 50 percent compressive strain (the strain at which the cell walls have fully collapsed and the material densifies, a process that absorbs the energy through the sequential elastic buckling, the plastic hinging, and the final densification of the cell walls, the three stages of the compressive stress-strain curve of the auxetic cellular solid — the linear-elastic region (up to approximately 5 percent strain, where the cell walls bend elastically), the plateau region (from 5 to 60 percent strain, where the cell walls buckle plastically and the stress remains approximately constant at the plateau stress of approximately 2 to 5 MPa), and the densification region (above 60 percent strain, where the collapsed cell walls press against each other and the stress rises steeply), the plateau region absorbing the majority of the impact energy at the approximately constant stress, and the total energy absorbed being the integral of the stress-strain curve over the impacted volume: E_absorbed = V_impacted × SEA, where V_impacted is the volume of the cladding that is compressed to the densification strain, approximated as the conical frustum of the impact zone with the top radius equal to the boulder's radius (0.15 m), the bottom radius equal to the boulder's radius plus the penetration depth times the tangent of the load-spreading angle — and since the auxetic cladding does NOT spread the load laterally (the negative Poisson's ratio pulls the material inward), the load-spreading angle is approximately zero, and the impacted volume is approximately the cylindrical column of the cladding directly beneath the boulder: V_impacted ≈ π·(0.15)²·p, where p is the penetration depth, and equating the absorbed energy to the impact kinetic energy gives p = KE/(π·(0.15)²·SEA) = 7,640/(π × 0.0225 × 15 × 10⁶) ≈ 7,640/(1.06 × 10⁶) ≈ 0.0072 m = 7.2 mm — a penetration depth of only 7.2 millimeters, which is far less than the 150 mm cladding thickness, indicating that the boulder penetrates only approximately 5 percent of the cladding thickness before being arrested, the remaining 95 percent of the cladding being largely undeformed and available to absorb the subsequent impacts, a remarkable protective efficiency that the auxetic's high SEA provides and that is the key advantage of the auxetic over the conventional, the non-auxetic cellular solids (such as the aluminum honeycomb or the polymer foams, which have the SEA values of approximately 5 to 10 MJ/m³ — roughly half the auxetic's SEA — and which would require approximately twice the thickness for the same energy absorption, a weight and the volume penalty that the Atlantis cladding's auxetic design avoids, contributing to the pod's overall mass budget compliance).

The spallation — the ejection of the material fragments from the rear surface of the cladding, opposite the impact point, caused by the reflection of the compressive stress wave as the tensile wave at the cladding-laminate interface, the tensile wave having the amplitude sufficient to fracture the TPU and to propel the fragments into the laminate, potentially damaging the laminate even if the boulder itself does not penetrate the full cladding thickness — is suppressed by the cladding's viscoelastic damping: the TPU's high loss tangent (tan δ ≈ 0.3 to 0.5 at the 10⁴ Hz frequency characteristic of the impact stress wave, the frequency being f ≈ c/(2t_clad) ≈ 1,500/(2 × 0.15) ≈ 5,000 Hz, where c ≈ 1,500 m/s is the longitudinal wave speed in the TPU (the wave speed in the cellular solid is lower than in the bulk TPU because of the porosity — the effective wave speed scales as √(E_eff/ρ_eff), and with E_eff ≈ 120 MPa and ρ_eff ≈ (1 - φ)·ρ_TPU ≈ 0.52 × 1,200 ≈ 624 kg/m³ for the 48 percent porosity, the wave speed is approximately √(120 × 10⁶/624) ≈ √(1.92 × 10⁵) ≈ 438 m/s, giving the frequency of approximately f = 438/(2 × 0.15) ≈ 1,460 Hz, which is within the TPU's high-loss-frequency range, where the tan δ is approximately 0.4, meaning that the stress wave amplitude is attenuated by a factor of e^(-π·tan δ·N) ≈ e^(-π × 0.4 × (0.15/λ)) per round-trip through the cladding thickness, and with the wavelength λ = c/f ≈ 438/1,460 ≈ 0.30 m, the round-trip distance of 0.30 m (twice the thickness) is exactly one wavelength, so N = 1, and the attenuation factor is e^(-π × 0.4) ≈ e^(-1.257) ≈ 0.285 — i.e., the reflected tensile wave has only 28.5 percent of the incident compressive wave's amplitude, and the spallation threshold — the tensile stress required to fracture the TPU, approximately 8 to 12 MPa — is not reached for the incident compression of approximately 20 to 30 MPa (the plateau stress), the reflected tension being approximately 30 × 0.285 ≈ 8.6 MPa, which is near the fracture threshold, and the spallation is marginal: the cladding may spall slightly at the rear surface (the small, shallow fragments at the cladding-laminate interface), but the spallation will not penetrate the laminate because the spall fragments have low mass (a few grams) and low velocity (the residual stress-wave energy that drives the spallation is only approximately 8 percent of the incident energy, i.e., the reflected wave's energy is approximately (0.285)² ≈ 0.081, so the spall-fragment kinetic energy is less than approximately 0.6 kJ — an order of magnitude below the energy that would damage the laminate), a fragment-energy analysis that confirms the cladding's spallation resistance is adequate to protect the laminate, and the spallation that does occur is a cosmetic, non-structural effect that the cladding's sacrificial-panel design (PART-064) accommodates by the periodic replacement of the panels that have experienced the multiple impacts, the replacement being a routine, ROV-executed maintenance operation that does not require the pod to surface.

The Hertzian fracture model — the classical analysis of the crack initiation in the brittle materials under the elastic contact loading, which applies to the ALON viewport (PART-046) and, with the modifications for the anisotropic, the laminated composite, to the LBFRP laminate — predicts that the ring crack, the characteristic surface crack that forms at the perimeter of the contact circle in the brittle materials, initiates when the maximum tensile stress at the contact perimeter, the radial tensile stress σ_r = (1 - 2ν)·p_0/2 (for the frictionless contact, the Poisson's-ratio-dependent expression that reflects the tension generated in the material just outside the contact circle by the Poisson's expansion of the compressed material inside the contact circle), exceeds the material's tensile strength, and for the ALON viewport with ν = 0.24 and p_0 ≈ 5.07 GPa (the granite-boulder impact on the bare ALON, from PART-046), the radial tensile stress is σ_r = (1 - 0.48) × 5.07 × 10⁹/2 ≈ 0.52 × 2.535 × 10⁹ ≈ 1.32 GPa — far exceeding the ALON's tensile strength of approximately 300 to 400 MPa (the polycrystalline ALON's tensile strength is limited by the grain-boundary flaws, the intrinsic processing defects that are the fracture-initiating sites, and the 1.32 GPa tensile stress would certainly initiate the ring crack at the contact perimeter, the initial stage of the Hertzian fracture that the ALON viewport's sacrificial outer pane (PART-046) is designed to absorb, the outer pane fracturing by the ring-crack initiation and the subsequent cone-crack propagation (the Hertzian cone crack, which grows from the ring crack downward and outward into the material at an angle of approximately 22° to 25° from the surface, forming the conical fracture surface that is the classic signature of the blunt-indenter impact on the brittle materials), and the outer pane's 25 mm thickness is sufficient to arrest the cone crack before it reaches the inner pane's 55 mm thickness, the crack-arrest mechanism that the ALON's fracture toughness of K_Ic ≈ 2.0 MPa·√m provides — the cone crack propagates as long as the stress intensity factor at the crack tip exceeds K_Ic, and the crack-arrest depth is approximately z_arrest = (K_Ic/(σ_hydrostatic × √(π)))², where σ_hydrostatic is the hydrostatic component of the contact stress field at the crack-tip depth, which decays approximately as p_0·(1 + (z/a)²)^(-1), and for z = 25 mm, a = 19.4 mm (the contact radius from PART-046), the decay factor is approximately 1/(1 + (25/19.4)²) ≈ 1/(1 + 1.66) ≈ 0.376, giving the local stress of approximately 5.07 × 0.376 ≈ 1.91 GPa, and z_arrest ≈ (2.0 × 10⁶/(1.91 × 10⁹ × 1.772))² ≈ (2.0 × 10⁶/(3.39 × 10⁹))² ≈ (5.9 × 10⁻⁴)² ≈ 3.48 × 10⁻⁷ m = 0.35 μm — a vanishingly small crack-arrest depth that indicates the cone crack, once initiated, will propagate through the entire 25 mm outer-pane thickness without arresting, a result that confirms the outer pane is a single-use, sacrificial element that absorbs the impact by fracturing completely, leaving the inner pane to sustain the pressure boundary after the impact, exactly as the viewport's fail-safe design philosophy intends. The inner pane, protected by the outer pane's fracture and the impedance-mismatch attenuation of the stress wave through the gel gap (PART-046), experiences a peak tensile stress of only approximately 40 MPa — well below the ALON's tensile strength — and does not fracture, preserving the pressure boundary, a two-pane, sacrificial+pressure-bearing architecture that the Hertzian fracture analysis validates and that the viewport certification (PART-055) confirms through the full-scale impact test on the qualification viewport assembly, which successfully survived the 0.3-meter granite-boulder impact at 20 m/s without inner-pane fracture, the test article's post-impact ultrasonic inspection showing no detectable cracks or the delaminations in the inner pane, the definitive validation that the Hertzian fracture model's predictions are conservative and that the ALON viewport's impact survivability meets the Atlantis turbidite-defense requirements.
