# CSMFAB077-DEEP-028 — CONSTRUCTION CONSTRAINTS: CORCOVADO NATIONAL PARK REGULATIONS, ZERO-DREDGE DEPLOYMENT, ACOUSTIC IMPACT ON MARINE MAMMALS

**Document ID:** CSMFAB077-DEEP-028 | **Parent:** CSMFAB000000000077 | **Version:** 2.0 | **Part:** 028 of 160 — Block 3

The deployment of the Osa demonstration community at the boundary of the Corcovado National Park — a 42,000-hectare protected area that encompasses the entire southwestern portion of the Osa Peninsula and that is widely considered the crown jewel of Costa Rica's national park system, harboring an estimated 2.5 percent of the world's biodiversity within its borders, including the last viable populations of several endangered species (the Baird's tapir, the jaguar, the harpy eagle, the scarlet macaw) — imposes construction constraints that are among the most stringent of any engineering project in the world, constraints that the Atlantis program has addressed through the adoption of a "zero-dredge" deployment philosophy, meaning that the seabed at the deployment site is not altered — not excavated, not leveled, not cleared — before the pod is placed, a philosophy that is made possible by the careful site selection (the Osa site's bathymetry, as described in PART-024, is naturally flat and stable, requiring no preparation), by the use of the helical pile anchoring system (which screws into the seabed without excavation, as described in PART-015), and by the use of the dynamic positioning system of the Charlemagne deployment vessel (which holds the vessel precisely on station, using GPS and the acoustic positioning transponders on the seabed, without the need for the conventional mooring anchors that would disturb the seabed — a disturbance that, in the sensitive environment of the Corcovado marine protected area, would be unacceptable, and that the Atlantis deployment plan avoids, by specifying that the deployment vessel shall use only the dynamic positioning system, and that the ROV operations — the installation of the piles, the connection of the tunnels, the deployment of the surface relay buoy — shall be conducted with the minimum possible seabed contact, a contact that the environmental monitoring program assesses, through the pre- and post-deployment ROV surveys that measure the area of the seabed that has been disturbed, a disturbance that the SETENA permit limits to 500 square meters (the pod's footprint, plus a 10-meter buffer around the pod for the ROV operations), a limit that the Atlantis deployment team, through careful planning and the extensive use of the ROV simulators (which allow the ROV pilots to practice the deployment procedures in a virtual replica of the Osa site, developing the muscle memory and the spatial awareness that minimize the inadvertent seabed contact), has demonstrated, during the mock deployment that was conducted at the Mojave facility's test tank in April 2026, can be met — the actual disturbed area, as measured by the post-mock-deployment survey, was 420 square meters, 16 percent below the permitted limit, a margin that the Atlantis environmental team considers sufficient to accommodate the variability of the real deployment conditions, a variability that the deployment team is prepared for, through the contingency plans that address the most likely deviations — the stronger-than-expected currents (the Golfo Dulce is subject to the tidal currents that can reach 1 to 2 meters per second at the gulf's entrance, a velocity that, while modest, can complicate the ROV's station-keeping, a complication that the deployment plan addresses by scheduling the most critical ROV operations — the pile installation, the tunnel connection — during the slack-water periods, the brief intervals of still water that occur between the flood and ebb tides, and that the TideModel oceanographic forecasting system, which the Charlemagne deployment vessel carries, predicts to within ±15 minutes, an accuracy that the deployment team has validated, through the comparison of the TideModel predictions with the observations from the Osa tide gauge — a bottom-pressure recorder that the Charlemagne survey vessel installed in October 2025 and that has been transmitting the tide data, via the Iridium satellite link, to the Atlantis operations center in Mojave, providing a 12-month record of the tidal currents at the Osa site, a record that the deployment team has used to calibrate the TideModel and to develop the slack-water deployment schedule, a schedule that the SETENA permit requires and that the Atlantis program has committed to, as part of the comprehensive environmental management plan that the permit embodies.
