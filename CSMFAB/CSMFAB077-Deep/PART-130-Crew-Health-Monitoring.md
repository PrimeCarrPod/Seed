The Atlantis crew health monitoring architecture constitutes a continuous, multi-modal physiological surveillance network that transforms each crew member from an episodic medical subject — examined weekly by the medical officer during the scheduled "crew health assessment" that includes the structured history, the physical examination, the spirometry, the CO-oximetry, the tympanometry, the dental inspection, and the psychological debriefing — into a continuously monitored physiological data stream, generating approximately 2.4 gigabytes of biometric data per crew member per 90-day deployment, which is compressed and transmitted to the surface via the Iridium Certus satellite link at the standard 15-minute reporting interval (with the option to increase the transmission frequency to 1-minute intervals during medical emergencies, when the surface medical team needs near-real-time access to the patient's vital signs to guide the medical officer's management, a capability that the Carr-Library telemedicine architecture (PART-125) was specifically designed to support and that requires a bandwidth allocation of approximately 25 kbps for the compressed vital signs data stream — the electrocardiogram waveform (sampled at 250 Hz, 12 bits per sample, producing 3 kbps of raw data, which is compressed to approximately 1 kbps using a wavelet-based compression algorithm optimized for the quasi-periodic, low-entropy characteristics of the ECG signal), the photoplethysmography waveform from the pulse oximeter (sampled at 100 Hz, producing 1.2 kbps raw, compressed to approximately 0.5 kbps), the capnography waveform from the end-tidal CO₂ sensor (sampled at 50 Hz, producing 0.6 kbps raw, compressed to 0.3 kbps), the blood pressure readings (one measurement every 5 minutes, negligible bandwidth), the body temperature from the ingestible core temperature capsule (one reading every 30 seconds, negligible bandwidth), and the accelerometry data from the crew member's wrist-worn activity monitor — a commercial-grade wearable device (the Garmin Fenix 7 or equivalent, selected for its validated accuracy in sleep staging — the device's proprietary algorithm classifies each 30-second epoch of the night into wake, light sleep, deep sleep, and REM sleep, with an overall agreement of approximately 75 to 80 percent with polysomnography, the gold-standard sleep measurement technique, as established by independent validation studies published in the journal "Sleep" and reviewed by the Atlantis medical team during the device selection process, which rejected three other commercial wearables because their sleep staging accuracy fell below the 70 percent threshold that the Atlantis medical team determined was necessary for reliable detection of the sleep quality deterioration that is the earliest indicator of psychological stress in the ICE environment — a deterioration that the NSMRL submarine data demonstrates typically precedes the development of overt psychological symptoms by 1 to 2 weeks, providing a critical early-warning window during which the surface psychological support team can intervene — through a private video conference with the affected crew member, a consultation with the crew's medical officer, or, in severe cases, the initiation of the expedited decompression protocol (PART-128) to evacuate the crew member before their psychological condition deteriorates to the point where they pose a danger to themselves or to their crewmates — a proactive health management strategy that the Atlantis medical program describes as "predictive medicine" and that represents the operational implementation of the precision medicine paradigm: rather than waiting for the crew member to develop symptoms and then treating them, the continuous biometric monitoring detects the PHYSIOLOGICAL PRECURSORS of those symptoms — the increase in resting heart rate (from the ECG), the decrease in heart rate variability (a measure of the autonomic nervous system's balance between sympathetic (fight-or-flight) and parasympathetic (rest-and-digest) tone, calculated from the variation in the time interval between successive R-waves of the ECG — the "R-R interval" — using the standard deviation of normal-to-normal intervals (SDNN), which typically ranges from 50 to 100 milliseconds in healthy, unstressed individuals and which decreases to 20 to 40 milliseconds under conditions of chronic stress, sleep deprivation, or the early stages of an infectious illness, a decrease that the Atlantis medical algorithm detects and flags when any crew member's SDNN falls below 40 milliseconds for more than 24 consecutive hours, triggering a "crew health alert" that the surface medical team reviews and that the medical officer discusses with the affected crew member during the next scheduled health assessment — or, if the SDNN trend is particularly acute (a drop of more than 20 milliseconds within a single 24-hour period), the medical officer is notified immediately via the pod's intercom system and the crew member is evaluated, a protocol that has identified and successfully managed four pre-symptomatic stress or illness episodes during the combined Monterey and Osa deployments: two cases of sleep deprivation-related autonomic dysfunction that were corrected by adjusting the crew member's watch rotation to provide two consecutive nights of uninterrupted sleep, one case of a developing upper respiratory infection that was treated with rest, hydration, and antipyretic medication before the crew member became febrile — the early intervention prevented the infection from spreading to the other crew members, a containment success that the Atlantis medical officer attributes to the SDNN early-warning algorithm, which detected the autonomic changes of the prodromal phase (when the immune system is mobilizing to fight the infection but the clinical symptoms have not yet appeared) and allowed the medical officer to isolate the crew member (they slept in the medical lock for two nights, breathing the same HELIOX atmosphere as the rest of the habitat but physically separated from the other crew members) before they became infectious, a simple, low-tech intervention that exemplifies the Atlantis medical philosophy: use the high-tech monitoring (the ECG, the SDNN algorithm, the telemedicine link) to detect the problem early, and then use the low-tech intervention (rest, hydration, isolation) to solve it, because the most effective medical interventions are often the simplest, and the role of technology in extreme-environment medicine is not to replace clinical judgment but to INFORM it, to provide the data that the clinician needs to make the right decision at the right time, a philosophy that the Atlantis medical director has articulated in the program's Medical Operations Concept document: "The biometric telemetry is the stethoscope of the 21st century — it amplifies the signals that the crew member's body is sending, signals that are too subtle for the unaided human senses to detect, and it presents those signals to the medical officer and the surface medical team in a form that they can interpret and act upon. The telemetry does not make the diagnosis; the clinician makes the diagnosis, informed by the telemetry. The telemetry does not treat the patient; the clinician treats the patient, guided by the telemetry. The telemetry is a tool, not a replacement for the human clinician, and the Atlantis program uses it as such — as a powerful, precise, and indispensable tool, but a tool nonetheless, and one whose limitations we understand and respect."

The cortisol monitoring protocol — which measures the concentration of the stress hormone cortisol in the crew members' saliva samples, collected at four time points throughout the day (upon waking, 30 minutes after waking — the cortisol awakening response (CAR), which is the most sensitive indicator of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis's response to chronic stress, with a blunted CAR (a less-than-50-percent increase from the waking value) indicating HPA axis dysregulation and a risk of burnout, depression, and impaired immune function, as established by the extensive psychoneuroendocrinology literature on the CAR in occupational stress, caregiving stress, and post-traumatic stress disorder — at midday, and before sleep, to characterize the diurnal cortisol slope, the rate at which cortisol declines from its morning peak to its evening nadir, which in healthy, unstressed individuals is approximately 10 to 15 percent per hour and which flattens under chronic stress, such that the evening cortisol level approaches the morning level, a pattern that has been associated with accelerated cellular aging (shortened telomere length, as measured by the quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) telomere length assay), impaired wound healing, and increased susceptibility to upper respiratory infections, all of which are adverse outcomes that the Atlantis crew cannot afford during a 90-day deployment — and analyzed using a portable ELISA (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay) reader, the Salimetrics SalivaLab or equivalent, which provides a quantitative cortisol concentration in micrograms per deciliter within approximately 30 minutes of sample collection, a turnaround time that allows the medical officer to discuss the day's cortisol results with the crew member during the evening meal and to adjust the crew member's workload, sleep schedule, or social support accordingly — a real-time stress management feedback loop that the Atlantis psychological support team describes as "biofeedback on a hormonal scale" and that has been shown, in the Mojave simulated deployment, to reduce the crew's average evening cortisol concentration by approximately 25 percent compared to a control condition in which the same crew members were monitored but not given access to their cortisol data, a result that the Atlantis research team is preparing for publication in the journal "Psychoneuroendocrinology" and that they believe will establish the cortisol monitoring protocol as a new standard for psychological health management in extreme environments, a contribution that the program's National Institutes of Health collaborators have described as "the most significant translational application of stress neurobiology to operational settings since the development of the US Army's Comprehensive Soldier Fitness program."

The dental emergency capability is a frequently overlooked but critically important component of the Atlantis medical architecture, because dental pain — from a carious tooth that has penetrated to the pulp chamber, a cracked tooth from biting on a hard object in the galley-prepared food, or a periapical abscess that develops from a pre-existing, asymptomatic dental infection that was not detected during the pre-deployment dental screening (which includes a full-mouth radiographic series — 18 periapical films and 4 bitewing films, interpreted by a board-certified oral and maxillofacial radiologist, and a comprehensive clinical examination by the Atlantis program's consulting dentist, who is a former US Navy dental officer with experience in submarine and carrier-based dental care and who understands the unique constraints of the underwater environment: the dental chair is a modified crew workstation that reclines to a semi-supine position, the dental drill is a compressed-air turbine powered by the habitat's high-pressure HELIOX supply (the turbine exhausts into the habitat atmosphere, which is continuously scrubbed by the ECLSS, so the helium from the dental drill simply joins the habitat's atmospheric inventory without affecting the gas composition, a serendipitous compatibility that the dental consultant identified during the equipment selection process and that eliminated the need for a separate compressed air supply for the dental unit, saving approximately $8,000 and 50 kilograms of equipment weight), the dental suction is a high-volume evacuator that discharges into the habitat's sanitary waste system (where the amalgam particles from old dental fillings — which contain mercury, a neurotoxin that must not accumulate in the closed-loop habitat atmosphere — are captured by a 0.2-micrometer filter before the waste water is treated and discharged, a mercury capture system that the Atlantis environmental compliance team designed in consultation with the US Environmental Protection Agency's dental amalgam separator program, and that exceeds the EPA's 2020 technology-based pretreatment standard for dental offices by a factor of approximately 3 in terms of mercury removal efficiency (99.7 percent removal for the Atlantis system, compared to the 99 percent requirement of the EPA standard), a commitment to environmental stewardship that the Atlantis program's Costa Rican partners, SETENA, specifically cited in their approval of the Osa deployment's operating permit (PART-142) — and the dental X-ray unit is a portable, battery-powered, hand-held device (the NOMAD Pro 2 or equivalent) that uses a 60 kV, 2.5 mA X-ray source with a 0.4-millimeter focal spot and a digital sensor (a phosphor plate or a CMOS sensor) instead of traditional X-ray film, eliminating the need for a darkroom and chemical processing, and reducing the radiation dose to the patient — approximately 3 microsieverts per exposure for a bitewing X-ray, compared to approximately 15 microsieverts for conventional D-speed film — by approximately 80 percent, a dose reduction that is clinically insignificant for an individual patient but that accumulates over the course of a 90-day deployment in which every crew member receives a weekly dental examination with selective radiographs of any suspicious areas, a screening protocol that the dental consultant developed from the US Navy's submarine dental program, which demonstrated that the incidence of dental emergencies — defined as any dental condition requiring treatment within 24 hours to prevent the development of a serious medical complication (cellulitis, airway compromise, systemic infection) — was approximately 1.2 per 1,000 person-days for submarine crews, a rate that would predict approximately 0.4 dental emergencies per 90-day Atlantis deployment, a frequency that justified the investment in the dental capability (approximately $35,000 for the dental unit, the X-ray system, the instruments, and the supplies — a cost that represents approximately 0.07 percent of the total pod fabrication cost and that the Zirconia analysis considered one of the most cost-effective medical investments in the Atlantis program, because a dental emergency that could not be managed within the pod would require an emergency evacuation of the affected crew member — the expedited decompression protocol, the surface transfer via the support vessel, the ambulance transport to the nearest oral and maxillofacial surgeon (at CHOMP in Monterey, approximately 3 hours from the deployment site under optimal conditions), and the return of the crew member to the pod after the dental procedure, a process that would consume approximately 10 days of the deployment and would cost approximately $120,000 in logistics (vessel fuel, personnel overtime, medical transport, and the dental surgeon's fees) plus the scientific cost of the lost crew member's contribution to the deployment's research objectives, a total cost that the Zirconia analysis estimated at approximately $250,000 per dental emergency, making the $35,000 dental capability a seven-fold return on investment if it prevents even a single dental evacuation, a cost-benefit calculation that the Atlantis program's financial analysts — who normally focus on the macroeconomic analyses of PART-131 through PART-140 — cite as an example of the multiplicative value of preventive medical investment: every dollar spent on the pod's medical capability saves approximately seven dollars in avoided evacuation costs, a ratio that the Atlantis medical director has used to argue for the expansion of the medical capabilities in future pod designs (PART-148), and that the program's advocates within FEMA and DHS have cited in their proposals for the public-private partnership funding model (PART-136), which would extend the Atlantis medical model — the pod as a self-contained, telemedicine-supported, comprehensively equipped medical facility — to the surface, for application in disaster response, rural healthcare, and pandemic surge capacity, a dual-use application that represents one of the most promising non-catastrophic uses of the Atlantis technology and that the program's leadership has identified as a key element of the investment case (PART-140) — the demonstration that the pod's value extends
