## 2. SOLID-STATE CARBON MONOXIDE DETECTION: SENSOR TECHNOLOGY, CALIBRATION, AND THE ATLANTIS CO MONITORING NETWORK ARCHITECTURE

The Atlantis CO monitoring system employs metal-oxide semiconductor (MOS) solid-state sensors — specifically, tin dioxide (SnO₂) sensors operating at an elevated temperature of approximately 350°C, achieved by an integrated platinum thin-film heater that consumes approximately 30 milliwatts of power and that is regulated to ±2°C by the sensor's onboard control circuitry, which measures the heater's resistance (a known function of temperature for platinum, with a temperature coefficient of resistance of approximately 0.00385 per degree Celsius, per the IEC 60751 standard for platinum resistance thermometers) and adjusts the heater current to maintain the target temperature, compensating for the cooling effect of the gas flow across the sensor surface and for the gradual drift in the heater's resistance due to electromigration of the platinum atoms over years of continuous operation — a failure mode that the sensor's self-diagnostic algorithm detects by comparing the heater's voltage-current characteristic to the expected characteristic for a properly functioning sensor, flagging any sensor whose heater resistance has drifted by more than 5 percent from its calibration value for replacement by the crew during the next scheduled maintenance period. The SnO₂ sensor's detection principle exploits the fact that the electrical conductivity of tin dioxide — an n-type semiconductor with a bandgap of approximately 3.6 electronvolts — increases when the sensor surface is exposed to reducing gases such as CO, hydrogen, methane, ethanol, and other volatile organic compounds, because these gases react with the chemisorbed oxygen species (O⁻, O₂⁻, O²⁻) on the SnO₂ surface, releasing electrons back into the conduction band of the semiconductor and thereby reducing the surface's electrical resistance, which is measured by the sensor's interdigitated electrodes (gold thin-film electrodes deposited on the SnO₂ substrate in a comb-like pattern that maximizes the electrode perimeter for a given sensor area, improving the sensitivity — the sensor's resistance change per unit concentration of the target gas — at the cost of a slightly longer response time because the gas must diffuse into the narrow gaps between the electrode fingers, a tradeoff that the sensor manufacturer (Figaro Engineering Inc., Osaka, Japan, model TGS 3870, selected for the Atlantis application after a comparative evaluation of nine CO sensor technologies including electrochemical cells, non-dispersive infrared (NDIR) absorption sensors, and other MOS sensor models from manufacturers in Japan, Germany, and the United States) has optimized for a balance of sensitivity (a detection limit of 1 ppm CO, with a resolution of 0.5 ppm in the 0 to 50 ppm range that is most relevant to the Atlantis cabin atmosphere monitoring), response time (T90 — the time to reach 90 percent of the final sensor reading after a step change in CO concentration — of less than 30 seconds, which is adequate for the Atlantis monitoring application because CO concentrations in the cabin atmosphere change gradually over hours rather than abruptly over seconds, the rate of change being determined by the balance between the CO source rate (endogenous production, approximately 1.6 milliliters per hour for a four-person crew, plus the occasional contribution from the TCCS catalyst degradation — a source that can increase the cabin CO concentration by approximately 10 ppm per hour if the catalyst is completely bypassed, a rate that is well within the sensor's response capability), the CO removal rate (the TCCS catalyst in its normal operating condition removes CO at a rate proportional to the CO concentration, with a first-order rate constant of approximately 0.5 per hour — equivalent to a CO half-life in the cabin of approximately 1.4 hours — meaning that the CO concentration approaches a steady state, determined by the source rate divided by the removal rate constant, within approximately 6 hours (four half-lives) after a change in the source rate, a timescale that allows the crew to respond to a rising CO concentration (due to a catalyst degradation, for example) with ample time to don the emergency breathing masks, bypass the TCCS, and activate the backup LiOH/KMnO₄ canister, all of which can be accomplished within approximately 15 minutes of the CO alarm activation (set at 25 ppm, one-half of the 50-ppm OSHA permissible exposure limit for an 8-hour workday, providing a generous margin for the crew to respond before the CO concentration reaches a level that would produce a measurable increase in the crew's COHb — a CO concentration of 50 ppm for 8 hours produces a COHb level of approximately 3 to 5 percent in a resting individual, a level that is associated with a slight decrement in the performance of vigilance tasks (sustained attention to a monotonous monitoring display, a task that is representative of the ECLSS monitoring duties that the crew performs during their watch rotations) but that is well below the 10 percent COHb level at which symptoms (headache, mild dyspnea on exertion) become noticeable, and far below the 20 to 30 percent COHb level at which nausea, vomiting, confusion, and loss of consciousness occur — a progression that the Atlantis medical protocol (PART-121, Section 7, "Medical Gas Reserves and Pharmaceutical Storage Stability") tracks by monitoring the crew's COHb levels using a pulse CO-oximeter (Masimo Radical-7 or equivalent), a non-invasive spectrophotometric device that measures the absorption of eight wavelengths of light transmitted through the finger to determine the fractional concentrations of oxyhemoglobin, deoxyhemoglobin, carboxyhemoglobin, and methemoglobin, providing a COHb measurement with an accuracy of ±3 percent (absolute) in the range of 0 to 40 percent COHb, and that the crew uses during the weekly medical check (see PART-130) to establish their individual COHb baseline and to detect any upward trend that might indicate a CO exposure source — a malfunctioning galley appliance, a smoldering electrical component, or a TCCS catalyst that is producing more CO than it is destroying — before it reaches a level that would impair their performance or endanger their health, a proactive monitoring strategy that the Atlantis medical director describes as "the canary in the coal mine, updated for the 21st century — the CO sensor detects Together, they provide a complete picture of the crew's CO exposure, and together they ensure that no crew member ever experiences a COHb level above 5 percent, the threshold at which any measurable physiological effect begins, for any duration during the 90-day deployment."
