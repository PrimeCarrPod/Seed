## 1.0 THE OXISOL EVIDENCE: STRATIGRAPHIC PROOF OF ATTENUATED INUNDATION AT THE OSA PENINSULA

The geological evidence that Costa Rica experienced inundation capped at approximately 170 meters — rather than the multi-kilometer scouring that devastated California, Chile, and other coastlines oriented perpendicular to the crustal displacement wave propagation — is preserved in the Oxisol clay beds of the Osa Peninsula, tropical soils that are characterized by their extreme weathering (the product of millions of years of intense chemical alteration in the warm, humid, tectonically stable environment of the southern Central American isthmus, where annual rainfall exceeds 4,000 millimeters and where the mean annual temperature of approximately 27 degrees Celsius drives rapid mineral hydrolysis — the breakdown of primary silicate minerals into secondary clay minerals, primarily kaolinite, gibbsite, and iron oxides such as hematite and goethite, through reactions that consume hydrogen ions, release silica and base cations into solution, and leave behind a residual accumulation of aluminum and iron that gives the Oxisol its characteristic red or yellow color and its low fertility for agriculture but its remarkable stability as a geological archive — because the iron-oxide cementation that binds the Oxisol particles together makes the soil resistant to erosion, resistant to compaction, and resistant to the bioturbation — the mixing of soil layers by roots, worms, and burrowing animals — that, in less weathered soils, would obliterate the stratigraphic signatures of past depositional events, including the signature of a megatsunami inundation, a signature that consists of a distinctive layer of marine sediment — fine sand, silt, and microfossils (foraminifera, coccoliths, and dinoflagellate cysts) that are characteristic of the open-ocean or the continental shelf environment, not of the terrestrial environment — intercalated within the Oxisol profile, a layer that can be dated through radiocarbon analysis of the organic matter that it contains (plant fragments, charcoal, and soil organic carbon that was entrained by the tsunami and deposited with the marine sediment) and through optically stimulated luminescence (OSL) dating of the quartz grains within the sediment, a technique that measures the time since the grains were last exposed to sunlight — essentially, the time since they were buried by the tsunami deposit — with a precision of approximately 5 to 10 percent of the age, sufficient to correlate the tsunami deposits across multiple sites and to establish their synchroneity — their deposition during the same event — a correlation that, for the Costa Rica sites, consistently yields ages of approximately 11,600 years before present, the same age that is associated with the Younger Dryas boundary and with the crustal displacement event that terminated the Pleistocene, a correlation that the Crustal Displacement Geological Analysis (DeepResearch/CrustalDisplacementAnalysis/PART-06) documents in exhaustive detail, describing the stratigraphy of 47 sites distributed across the Osa Peninsula, the Nicoya Peninsula, and the intervening coastal plain, at elevations ranging from 20 to 220 meters above present sea level, and finding that the tsunami deposit — a distinctive layer of well-sorted, fine- to medium-grained sand with marine microfossils, overlying the truncated surface of the Oxisol (the truncation indicating that the pre-event soil surface was eroded by the incoming wave, stripping away the uppermost — and youngest — part of the soil profile before depositing the marine sediment on
