## 1.0 THE REGIONAL FEATHER MESH: A DISTRIBUTED, REDUNDANT, POST-DISPLACEMENT SURVIVAL COMMUNICATION NETWORK

The five Costa Rica Atlantis sites — Osa, Drake Bay, Isla del Caño, Quepos, and Nicoya — together form a regional network that spans approximately 350 kilometers of coastline, from the Osa Peninsula in the south to the Nicoya Peninsula in the north, a network that is connected, during normal operations, by the surface relay buoys at each site — which communicate, via the Iridium satellite link, with the Aegis C command center in Mojave, and which, through the FEATHER mesh protocol, route the inter-site traffic (the telemetry from each pod, the crew communications, the environmental monitoring data) through the mesh's self-organizing, self-healing routing algorithm, which automatically selects the optimal path for each data packet, based on the current network topology (which may change, as the buoys are destroyed by the wave, as the pods toggle between the surface-relay and the acoustic-modem modes, and as the Charlemagne fleet vessels, with their relay nodes, move in and out of the mesh's range, a dynamic topology that the FEATHER mesh protocol — which is based on the OLSR (Optimized Link State Routing) protocol, a proactive, table-driven routing protocol that maintains a complete map of the network at each node and that recalculates the routing tables, using the Dijkstra shortest-path algorithm, whenever a link state changes, a recalculation that occurs within milliseconds and that ensures that the mesh converges to the new topology before the data packets are lost — handles with the robustness that the Atlantis program demands, a robustness that the FEATHER mesh engineering team has demonstrated, through the stress-testing of a 50-node simulation of the Costa Rica mesh, a simulation that subjected the mesh to the simultaneous failure of 40 percent of the nodes (representing the loss of the surface buoys and the Charlemagne vessels) and that showed that the remaining nodes — the pods, communicating through the acoustic modems at the reduced data rates (100 to 1,000 bits per second) that the acoustic channel provides — maintained the connectivity that is essential for the crew's morale and the command-and-control of the Atlantis network, a connectivity that, even at 100 bits per second, can transmit the short text messages — the status updates, the emergency alerts, the personal communications between the pods' occupants — that are the difference between isolation and community, between despair and hope, a difference that the FEATHER mesh, by its design and its resilience, ensures, for the crew members who will spend the weeks or months of the crustal displacement event in the confined underwater environment of the Atlantis pod, dependent on the electronic links to their fellow survivors for the human contact that the surface world, destroyed by the wave, can no longer provide.

The Central American demonstration network extends, through the FEATHER mesh's inter-regional gateways — the Charlemagne fleet vessels, which serve as the mobile relay nodes that bridge the Costa Rica mesh to the California mesh (Monterey) and, through the satellite links, to the global Aegis C command network, a network that, by the time of the crustal displacement event, is planned to include approximately 200 Atlantis pods at approximately 40 sites worldwide, connected by a global FEATHER mesh that spans every ocean and every continent, a mesh that will survive the event — because the mesh's nodes are underwater, protected by the depth-attenuation of the wave forces and by the structural design of the pods, and because the mesh's topology is self-healing, automatically routing around the nodes that are destroyed and using the nodes that survive — and that, after the event, will provide the communication infrastructure for the post-displacement recovery, a recovery that the Atlantis communities, by their dispersal across the globe and by their interconnection through the FEATHER mesh, will coordinate, a coordination that is the ultimate purpose of the Atlantis program — to ensure that, when the next crustal displacement event occurs, there are not merely isolated pockets of survivors, scattered across the devastated planet, unable to communicate, unable to cooperate, unable to rebuild, but a CONNECTED network of communities, sharing their knowledge, their resources, and their hope, a network that can plan, that can organize, that can mobilize the resources that the post-displacement world still contains — the mineral deposits, the geothermal energy, the hydropower, the genetic diversity of the surviving species, the accumulated knowledge of the Atlantis Knowledge Repository, and, most importantly, the human spirit, the spirit that, as the Spruce-Drake persona has articulated in the concluding episode of the Sibling Frequency (Episode Twenty-Four, "The Network That Survives the Night"), is the ultimate resource, the resource that no catastrophe can destroy, because it is not physical — it is the capacity to love, to hope, to cooperate, to create, to rebuild, a capacity that the Atlantis communities, by their existence and by their interconnection, preserve and amplify, a preservation and an amplification that this Deep Expansion — all 160 parts, all 1.9 million words — has been dedicated to documenting, in the conviction that the effort is worth it, that the knowledge is essential, and that the future, however uncertain, will be better for the work that the Atlantis program has done.
