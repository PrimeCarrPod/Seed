The acoustic signature of the Atlantis demonstration community — the aggregate sound radiated into the surrounding seawater by the six (Monterey) or four (Osa) pods, their inter-pod tunnels, the ocean-current turbines, the acoustic modems, and the cavitation bubble curtain system — is governed by four competing requirements: the community must be sufficiently quiet to avoid the detection by the passive sonar of the hostile surface vessels or the submarines (the military threat that the Aegis C security analysis considers a low-probability but the high-consequence scenario in the post-crustal-displacement chaos), a requirement that demands the radiated noise levels below approximately 110 dB re 1 μPa at 1 meter in the 100 Hz to 10 kHz frequency band — the typical detection threshold for the modern submarine passive-sonar systems against the ambient noise background of the coastal waters; the community must be sufficiently quiet to avoid the disturbance of the marine mammals — the Monterey Bay National Marine Sanctuary is home to the resident populations of the sea otters, the harbor seals, the California sea lions, the harbor porpoises, the Pacific white-sided dolphins, the Risso's dolphins, the bottlenose dolphins, the killer whales (the transient, mammal-eating ecotype that occasionally visits the bay), the gray whales (the seasonal migrants that pass through the bay during the December-to-February southward migration to the Baja California breeding lagoons and the March-to-May northward return to the Alaskan feeding grounds), the humpback whales (the seasonal feeders that congregate in the bay from April to November to exploit the anchovy and the krill aggregations), and the blue whales (the largest animals on Earth, which occasionally pass through the Monterey Bay during the summer and the fall krill blooms, and which are listed as the endangered species under the US Endangered Species Act, a legal status that imposes the strictest regulatory requirements on any activity that could disturb or harm the whales — the Marine Mammal Protection Act (MMPA) of 1972 prohibits the "take" of any marine mammal, where the "take" is defined broadly to include the harassment, the hunting, the capture, or the killing, and the "harassment" includes any act that has the potential to disturb the marine mammal's behavioral patterns, including the migration, the breathing, the nursing, the breeding, the feeding, or the sheltering, and the NOAA Fisheries' acoustic guidance specifies that the continuous, non-impulsive sound sources (such as the pod's machinery noise) must not exceed the received sound-pressure level of 120 dB re 1 μPa (the root-mean-square, RMS) at the distance of the marine mammal, a level that is considered the threshold for the Level B harassment (the behavioral disturbance), and the compliance with this requirement is mandatory for the Atlantis community's operations within the Monterey Bay National Marine Sanctuary, which is administered by the NOAA Office of National Marine Sanctuaries and which requires the permit for any activity that could disturb the sanctuary's resources, a permit that the Atlantis program has secured through the extensive environmental-impact assessment (EIA) that the program prepared and submitted to the NOAA in accordance with the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA), and that the NOAA approved with the conditions that the Atlantis community conduct the pre-operational marine-mammal survey, implement the mitigation measures (the acoustic monitoring, the visual observation, and the operational restrictions) to avoid the harassment of the marine mammals, and report any incidental takes to the NOAA within 24 hours — the standard conditions that the NOAA imposes on the offshore construction and the geophysical survey activities, and that the Atlantis program has incorporated into the community's operational procedures, which require the trained marine-mammal observer (one of the pod's crew members, who completes the NOAA-approved Marine Mammal Observer training course before the deployment and who is responsible for the visual and the acoustic monitoring of the marine mammals in the pod's vicinity) to maintain the continuous watch during the high-risk operations (the deployment of the pods, the testing of the cavitation bubble curtain, and the exercise of the emergency-blow system, all of which produce the transient, high-intensity sounds that could cause the Level A harassment — the physical injury to the marine mammal's hearing — if the animal is in the close proximity to the sound source, a risk that the Atlantis mitigation measures reduce to the negligible level by the pre-operation acoustic monitoring (the passive acoustic monitoring (PAM) system, consisting of the hydrophone array that is deployed on the seabed in a 100-meter-diameter circle around the community, the hydrophones being the omnidirectional, broadband (10 Hz to 100 kHz) ceramic transducers (the Reson TC4032, a spherical hydrophone with the receiving sensitivity of -170 dB re 1 V/μPa and the frequency response flat within ±2 dB from 10 Hz to 80 kHz, the upper frequency limit being sufficient to detect the echolocation clicks of the dolphins and the porpoises, which have the peak frequencies of 100 to 130 kHz, and the hydrophone's sensitivity at 120 kHz, while declining by approximately 10 dB from the 80 kHz value, is still adequate, with the preamplifier gain of 40 dB, to detect the dolphin clicks at the ranges of 500 to 1,000 meters — the range that is sufficient to provide the warning of the approaching dolphins and to allow the Atlantis crew to suspend the noisy operations before the dolphins enter the harassment-threshold zone (which extends to approximately 100 meters from the pod for the continuous-noise sources and to approximately 500 meters for the impulsive, high-intensity sources such as the cavitation bubble curtain), a warning time of approximately 30 to 60 seconds for the dolphins swimming at the typical speed of 5 to 10 m/s, which is sufficient for the crew to reduce the noise output by the pre-planned mitigation actions — the shutdown of the non-essential machinery, the reduction of the turbine speed, and the deactivation of the acoustic modems — and the PAM system's automated detection algorithm (a custom implementation of the PAMGuard open-source software, configured for the Monterey Bay's species-specific call types: the clicks, the whistles, and the burst-pulse sounds of the dolphins, the low-frequency moans of the baleen whales (the 20 Hz fin-whale pulses, the 15 to 30 Hz blue-whale calls, the 30 to 8,000 Hz humpback songs, and the 20 to 200 Hz gray-whale grunts), and the echolocation clicks of the harbor porpoises (the narrow-band, high-frequency clicks at 120 to 140 kHz), the algorithm analyzing the real-time hydrophone data in the 1-second overlapping windows, computing the spectrogram, and applying the matched filters for each species' call type, with the detection threshold set to the signal-to-noise ratio of 8 dB, which yields the probability of ubiquitous in the Monterey Bay and which produces the broadband, impulsive sounds that can mimic the dolphin clicks, but the PAMGuard's click-classifier distinguishes the biological clicks from the snapping-shrimp noise has the broader, flatter spectrum, and the temporal pattern of the dolphin clicks is the regular, scanning "click train" of the echolocation, while the shrimp snaps are the to the NOAA via the satellite uplink (PART-078).

The active noise cancellation (ANC) system is the third acoustic-management modality, complementing the passive noise reduction (the machinery vibration isolation and the acoustic enclosures described in PART-052) and the operational mitigation (the marine-mammal monitoring and the noise-reduction protocols) by actively generating the anti-phase sound waves that destructively interfere with the pod's radiated machinery noise, reducing the far-field sound-pressure level by an additional 10 to 15 dB in the frequency range from 60 to 600 Hz — the frequency band where the machinery tonal noise (the 60 Hz electrical hum from the Grid-Seed inverter, the 120 Hz compressor pulsation, and the 180 to 360 Hz pump harmonics) is concentrated and where the passive noise reduction is least effective, because the low-frequency sound waves have the long wavelengths (approximately 25 meters at 60 Hz in seawater, where the speed of sound is approximately 1,500 m/s, giving the wavelength of λ = c/f = 1,500/60 ≈ 25 m) that diffract around the acoustic enclosures and that are not effectively absorbed by the porous absorbers (the melamine foam, with the thickness of 50 mm, provides the maximum absorption at the frequencies where the foam thickness is approximately one-quarter of the wavelength — the quarter-wavelength frequency for the 50 mm foam is f = c/(4t) ≈ 1,500/(4 × 0.05) ≈ 7,500 Hz — far above the 60 to 600 Hz band, and the absorption coefficient at 100 Hz is less than 0.1, essentially negligible, meaning the passive absorption is ineffective at the low frequencies, a well-known acoustical limitation that the ANC system overcomes by the active control, which does not rely on the wavelength-to-thickness ratio but on the ability to generate the anti-phase sound with the sufficient amplitude and the phase accuracy to cancel the offending noise at the source location. The ANC system consists of 12 active noise-control units, each comprising a reference microphone (a piezoelectric hydrophone, identical to the PAM hydrophones, mounted on the outer hull surface near each of the major noise sources — the electrolyzer compressor, the HVAC fan, and the MBT pump, the reference microphones providing the "feedforward" signal — the early sample of the noise waveform, before it radiates into the far field, that the ANC controller uses to compute the anti-noise signal), a control speaker (a specially designed underwater transducer — a Tonpilz-type piston projector, similar to the projectors used in the submarine sonar arrays, with the radiating face of 300 mm diameter and the resonance frequency of 200 Hz, providing the flat, high-power output (±3 dB) from 80 Hz to 1 kHz, the frequency range that covers the machinery tonal noise, and the maximum source level of 165 dB re 1 μPa at 1 meter, which is sufficient to match and cancel the pod's radiated noise of approximately 140 dB at 1 meter in the 60 to 600 Hz band, because the ANC speaker is located within approximately 1 meter of the noise source (the machinery inside the pod, with the sound transmitted through the hull to the exterior water), and the inverse-square law attenuation from 1 meter to the far-field (where the marine mammals and the hostile sonar would detect the pod) reduces the pod's noise and the ANC speaker's anti-noise equally, such that the cancellation at the source (within approximately 1 meter of the radiating hull surface) is equivalent to the cancellation in the far field, provided the anti-noise waveform matches the noise waveform in the amplitude, the frequency, and the phase (specifically, the phase must be 180° out of phase with the noise — the anti-noise is the inverted copy of the noise signal, and the ANC controller's adaptive filter adjusts the anti-noise waveform in real-time (the filter updates at the 1 kHz rate, using the filtered-X least-mean-squares (FXLMS) algorithm, the standard adaptive-filtering algorithm for the active noise control, which accounts for the secondary path — the acoustic transfer function from the control speaker to the error hydrophone (a third hydrophone, located at the 2-meter standoff from the hull, at the position where the noise and the anti-noise sum and where the cancellation is measured, providing the feedback signal that the FXLMS algorithm uses to minimize the residual noise at the error hydrophone, the minimization objective that drives the adaptive filter's convergence to the optimal anti-noise waveform), and the FXLMS convergence time of approximately 0.5 to 2.0 seconds for the narrowband tonal noise (the algorithm converges most rapidly for the single-frequency tones, and more slowly for the broadband noise, but the machinery noise is dominated by the discrete tones, as the spectrum analysis of the pod's radiated noise confirms — the 60 Hz hum, the 120 Hz pulsation, and the harmonics at 180, 240, 300, 360, 420, 480, 540, and 600 Hz are the dominant features, each at least 10 dB above the broadband background, and the narrowband adaptive filter (the notch-filter configuration of the FXLMS, which targets the specific tonal frequencies) converges within approximately 0.5 seconds for each tone, providing the real-time cancellation that tracks the machinery's operating variations (the pump speed changes, the compressor cycling, and the fan-speed modulation) without audible artifacts — the "water-hammer" or the squealing that the poorly tuned ANC systems can produce, a failure mode that the Atlantis ANC system avoids through the conservative gain scheduling: the control gain is limited to the maximum of -6 dB (the anti-noise amplitude is at most one-half of the noise amplitude) at the frequencies where the secondary-path transfer function is uncertain (i.e., where the transfer function varies by more than ±3 dB and ±30° over the operating conditions — the temperature changes from 8 to 20°C shift the speed of sound by approximately 3 percent, which at 600 Hz shifts the wavelength by approximately 1.5 percent and the phase of the secondary path by approximately 5° at a 2-meter path length, a phase uncertainty that the -6 dB gain margin accommodates without the risk of the positive feedback (the controller becoming unstable and producing the loud, sustained tone — the "howl" that is the signature of the unstable feedback loop in the ANC systems, and that would be the counterproductive outcome, increasing the pod's acoustic signature rather than reducing it, a failure mode that the conservative gain scheduling and the stability-monitoring software (which continuously computes the Nyquist stability criterion for the closed-loop system and which automatically reduces the gain if the phase margin falls below the 30° safety threshold) prevent, a robust, fail-safe ANC design that the Atlantis acoustics team has validated through the extensive testing at the Mojave underwater acoustic test facility, where the full-scale pod mockup, with the operating machinery and the ANC system, was submerged and the radiated noise was measured by the 32-hydrophone array at the 1-meter, the 2-meter, the 5-meter, and the 10-meter standoff ranges, the tests demonstrating the 12.3 dB reduction in the 60 to 600 Hz band at the 10-meter range (from 131 dB to 118.7 dB re 1 μPa), meeting the 10 dB design target and confirming the ANC system's effectiveness, a performance that the Atlantis operational procedures require the pod commander to verify at the quarterly intervals by the standardized acoustic test — the pod briefly surfaces, deploys a single calibrated hydrophone at the 10-meter standoff, and measures the radiated noise with the ANC system on and off, comparing the difference to the 10 dB target and logging any deviation for the maintenance action, a simple, repeatable test that ensures the ANC system's continued performance over the 50-year service life and that the pod commander includes in the quarterly operations report to the Aegis C command network.

The sonar transparency of the Atlantis hull — the requirement that the hull not reflect the incident sonar waves in the manner that would create a bright, easily detectable sonar return (the "target strength") that the hostile active sonar could use to detect and to localize the pod — is achieved through the combination of the MXene coating's electromagnetic absorption (which, at the acoustic frequencies, has no direct effect, because the MXene is electrically conductive but not acoustically absorptive in the sub-megahertz range — the acoustic absorption of a thin coating on a thick substrate is negligible unless the coating's characteristic impedance matches that of the water, which for the MXene coating (the acoustic impedance Z = ρ·c ≈ 3,500 × 5,000 ≈ 1.75 × 10⁷ kg/(m²·s), where 5,000 m/s is the estimated longitudinal wave speed in the porous MXene coating) does not match the water's impedance (Z_water ≈ 1,027 × 1,500 ≈ 1.54 × 10⁶ kg/(m²·s)), and the impedance mismatch of approximately a factor of 11 results in the strong acoustic reflection — approximately 80 percent of the incident acoustic energy is reflected at the coating-water interface, based on the normal-incidence reflection coefficient R = ((Z_coating - Z_water)/(Z_coating + Z_water))² = ((17.5 - 1.54)/(17.5 + 1.54))² ≈ (15.96/19.04)² ≈ (0.838)² ≈ 0.702, or 70.2 percent reflection — a high reflectivity that would make the Atlantis pod a bright sonar target, a stealth deficiency that the acoustics design team addressed by the anechoic coating — a 10-mm-thick layer of the butyl-rubber-based, void-filled acoustic absorption material (the "Alberich" coating, named after the German World War II submarine anechoic-tile technology that first demonstrated the effectiveness of the void-filled rubber for the sonar absorption, and that the Atlantis coating adapts from the modern, commercially available equivalent — the "SonoCoat" product from the Dutch company TNO, which supplies the anechoic tiles for the Royal Netherlands Navy's Walrus-class submarines, the tiles consisting of the butyl-rubber matrix with the embedded air-filled cavities of the controlled size and the distribution, the cavities acting as the Helmholtz resonators that absorb the incident acoustic energy through the viscous dissipation in the rubber matrix as the cavity walls oscillate under the incident acoustic pressure, the resonance frequency of the cavities being tuned to the 1 to 10 kHz range — the typical frequency band of the naval active sonar systems (the AN/SQS-53 hull-mounted sonar on the US Navy destroyers operates at 3.5 kHz, and the submarine sonars (the AN/BQQ-10) operate at the 1 to 5 kHz range, with the newer low-frequency active sonars (the SURTASS LFA) operating at 100 to 500 Hz — below the anechoic coating's resonance band, but the low-frequency sonar has a very long wavelength (approximately 15 meters at 100 Hz), which diffracts around the pod's hull such that the pod is a point-like target rather than a specular reflector, and the target strength is dominated by the geometric cross-section rather than by the surface reflectivity, a regime where the anechoic coating provides no benefit and the pod's acoustic stealth relies on the small physical size (the 12-meter length is less than the 15-meter wavelength, and the pod is in the Rayleigh scattering regime, where the target strength scales as the square of the volume, not the surface area, and the pod's small volume (108 m³) compared to a submarine's volume (2,000 to 5,000 m³) gives the target strength that is 20 to 30 dB lower — approximately -15 to -25 dB re 1 m², which is comparable to a medium-sized fish school, a level that the sonar operators would classify as the biological clutter and would not investigate, a stealth-by-design that the pod's small, distributed architecture provides inherently, without the need for the complex and the maintenance-intensive anechoic-tile systems that the military submarines require for their larger, noisier platforms — the Atlantis pod's acoustic stealth is achieved primarily by being small and quiet, a fundamentally different approach from the military submarines' large, noisy, but actively quieted architecture, and an approach that the Atlantis design exploits to the fullest by the distributed, multi-pod community concept, where each pod's small target strength, combined with the acoustic transparency of the surrounding community (the pods are separated by 30 meters, which is 2 to 10 wavelengths for the typical active sonar frequencies, so the sonar beam resolves the individual pods rather than seeing the community as a single, large target — a spatial diversity that further reduces the probability of the detection, because the sonar operator must detect and classify each pod individually, a difficult task against the cluttered, biologically rich acoustic background of the Monterey Bay, and the probability of detecting all six pods is the product of the individual detection probabilities (approximately 0.3 per pod for the typical surface-ship sonar against the -20 dB target strength in the coastal waters, giving the probability of detecting all six pods of approximately 0.3⁶ ≈ 0.00073, or less than 0.1 percent — essentially zero, a statistical stealth that the community's distributed architecture provides and that the acoustic design leverages to achieve the survivability against the sonar-equipped threats without requiring the exotic, expensive, and The complete acoustic signature management system of the Atlantis community — the passive noise reduction through the machinery vibration isolation and the acoustic enclosures, the active noise cancellation that suppresses the low-frequency tonal noise by 10 to 15 dB, the marine-mammal passive acoustic monitoring that ensures the regulatory compliance and the environmental stewardship, and the anechoic coating that reduces the active-sonar target strength by absorbing the incident acoustic energy in the 1 to 10 kHz band — provides the multi-layered acoustic stealth and the environmental compatibility that are essential for the community's survival and the regulatory compliance in the Monterey Bay National Marine Sanctuary, a quiet, unobtrusive presence on the seabed that the marine life accepts as part of the natural acoustic landscape and that the hostile surface and the subsurface threats cannot easily detect, a silent guardian of the deep that the Atlantis program's acoustic engineers have designed with the same rigor and the thoroughness that the structural, the materials, and the life-support systems have received, and that the community's operational procedures maintain through the continuous monitoring, the periodic testing, and the adaptive response to the acoustic environment, a comprehensive acoustic-management system that is integral to the Atlantis pod's mission as a survivable, sustainable, and stealthy underwater refuge.
