The viewport of the Atlantis-class subsea habitation module occupies a position of unique criticality within the pressure hull architecture — it is simultaneously the largest single penetration of the pressure boundary, with a clear aperture diameter of 300 millimeters that interrupts the continuous LBFRP-001 laminate over an area of approximately 0.071 square meters, a geometric discontinuity that concentrates the membrane compressive stresses described in PART-042 by a factor of 2.5 to 3.5 at the aperture edge, depending on the latitude of the viewport installation (the forward viewport, positioned at φ = -75° — near the nose pole on the upper hemisphere, corresponding to a hull radius of approximately r(75°) = D_max/2 · √(1 - (6.3·sin(75°)/6.3)²) ≈ 2.03 × √(1 - 0.933²) ≈ 2.03 × √(0.129) ≈ 0.73 meters — places the viewport at a location where the membrane compressive stress in the meridional direction is approximately 85 MPa at the 45-meter design depth, as derived from the prolate spheroid membrane solution of PART-042, and the stress concentration at the aperture edge elevates the local von Mises stress to approximately 220 to 280 MPa, approaching the LBFRP-001 laminate's compressive strength of 280 MPa in the fiber direction, a margin of essentially zero that would be unacceptable were it not for the reinforcement doubler ring that increases the local laminate thickness from 40 mm to 95 mm over an annular region extending from the aperture edge (radius r_i = 150 mm) to an outer radius r_o = 300 mm, reducing the stress concentration factor from 3.3 to approximately 1.4 and bringing the peak von Mises stress at the doubler-to-hull transition down to approximately 52 MPa, a value that is 18.6 percent of the laminate's compressive strength and that provides the safety factor of approximately 5.4 that the ABS Rules for Underwater Vehicles require for viewport seatings); it is the sole optical interface between the occupants and the surrounding marine environment, through which all external visual observation — including the identification and tracking of turbidite boulders during the crustal displacement event, the visual inspection of the hull surface for coating damage or biofouling accumulation, and the psychological benefit of the "virtual window" to the external world that is critical for crew mental health during extended submerged deployments, as documented in PART-076 — must be conducted, the optical clarity of the viewport material and the precision of its refractive geometry being therefore not merely aesthetic considerations but operational requirements that directly affect the pod's survivability and habitability; and it is the most vulnerable point of the pressure boundary to impact damage from turbidite-borne boulders, because the ALON (aluminum oxynitride, Al₂₃O₂₇N₅ — a transparent polycrystalline ceramic with the spinel crystal structure, fabricated by the hot-pressing and hot-isostatic-pressing of a compacted powder preform at temperatures of 1,800 to 2,000°C, producing a fully dense, optically transparent material with a Vickers hardness of approximately 18 GPa, a flexural strength of approximately 700 MPa in four-point bending, and a fracture toughness of approximately 2.0 MPa·√m — values that place ALON in the class of "transparent armor" ceramics, superior to both conventional soda-lime glass (Vickers hardness approximately 5.5 GPa, flexural strength approximately 50 to 100 MPa, fracture toughness approximately 0.7 MPa·√m) and borosilicate glass (Pyrex, Vickers hardness approximately 6.5 GPa, flexural strength approximately 70 to 120 MPa) and comparable to sapphire (single-crystal Al₂O₃, Vickers hardness approximately 22 GPa, flexural strength approximately 400 to 800 MPa depending on crystal orientation) but with the manufacturing advantage that ALON can be produced in large, complex shapes by the powder-processing route — single-crystal sapphire, by contrast, must be grown from the melt by the Kyropoulos or edge-defined film-fed growth (EFG) methods, which are limited to boules of approximately 300 to 400 mm diameter for the largest production furnaces and which require extensive post-growth machining, grinding, and polishing to achieve the optical surface quality and the precise geometric tolerances that the viewport demands, a manufacturing route that would be slower, more expensive, and less amenable to the curved-pane geometry that the optical design analysis demonstrates is superior to the flat-pane alternative for the Atlantis application) is transparent, it cannot be reinforced with the lonsdaleite nanodiamond, the MXene coating, or the auxetic cladding that defend the opaque portions of the hull — the viewport must face the external environment bare, protected only by its intrinsic hardness and fracture toughness and, if the turbidite threat materializes, by the sacrificial outer pane that fractures before the inner, pressure-bearing pane, a "fail-safe" design philosophy in which the outer pane (thickness 25 mm, curved to match the inner pane's geometry and mechanically attached to the viewport frame by a series of 12 low-profile titanium bolts that are designed to shear at a predetermined bolt tension, such that a boulder impact sufficient to fracture the outer pane will shear the bolts and allow the fractured pane to separate from the frame rather than transmitting the impact load into the inner pane through the bolt connections — the "fusible link" principle of mechanical design, adapted from the overload protection devices used in aircraft landing gear and other critical structural connections where the preservation of the primary structure justifies the deliberate sacrifice of a secondary component) takes the impact, and the inner pane (thickness 55 mm, the primary pressure boundary, designed to withstand the full 0.55 MPa design external pressure with a safety factor of 4.0 against fracture — the fracture safety factor being higher than the buckling safety factor of the hull because the viewport, as a brittle ceramic component, fails suddenly and without the ductile warning signs (plastic deformation, leakage, progressive cracking) that precede the buckling failure of the composite hull and that the structural health monitoring system can detect and act upon, a "brittle failure" mode that the higher safety factor is intended to render vanishingly improbable over the 50-year design life, with a target fracture probability of less than 10⁻⁶ per viewport per year, consistent with the overall pod safety target) maintains the pressure boundary even if the outer pane is completely destroyed.

The selection of a curved (spherical sector) rather than flat (plane-parallel disk) viewport geometry is the foundational optical and structural design decision, determined through a coupled finite element and ray-tracing analysis that evaluated the four competing factors: the optical distortion introduced by the viewport's refractive geometry (which for a flat pane introduces a predictable but significant pincushion distortion — the magnification of the image varies with the off-axis angle, compressing the peripheral field and making distant objects appear closer to the center of the field of view, a distortion that impairs the operator's ability to estimate the distance, size, and velocity of approaching turbidite boulders, which are the critical visual tasks during the crustal displacement event, and that a curved pane with a spherical inner surface of radius R_inner = 350 mm — approximately 1.17 times the viewport's clear aperture diameter of 300 mm, selected so that the inner surface's center of curvature is located at the design eye position, approximately 200 mm behind the viewport's inner surface (the typical eye-to-viewport distance for an operator seated at the observation station), such that the optical rays from the external scene pass through the curved pane approximately normal to the inner surface, minimizing the refractive bending and the associated distortion — reduces by approximately 80 percent relative to a flat pane of the same thickness and aperture diameter, as quantified by the ray-tracing analysis conducted in Zemax OpticStudio using the ALON refractive index of n = 1.79 at the sodium D-line wavelength of 589 nm (the peak sensitivity wavelength of the human photopic visual system) and the dispersion curve n(λ) derived from the Sellmeier equation fitted to the spectroscopic ellipsometry data for the Surmet Corporation's ALON grade A ceramic across the 400 to 2,000 nm wavelength range, which established n(400 nm) ≈ 1.82, n(700 nm) ≈ 1.78, and the Abbe number V_d ≈ 55 — a relatively low dispersion that produces minimal chromatic aberration and that is comparable to the optical glasses used in high-quality camera lenses, ensuring that the viewport provides a sharp, color-accurate image of the external scene across the full visible spectrum and into the near-infrared, where the pod's external illumination system (a ring of 12 LED lamps, each producing 5,000 lumens at a color temperature of 5,500 K — approximating the spectrum of natural daylight at the 45-meter depth, where the red and orange wavelengths have been attenuated by the water column (the absorption coefficient of pure seawater at 700 nm is approximately 0.6 m⁻¹, meaning that after 45 meters, the intensity of red light is reduced to approximately e^(-0.6 × 45) ≈ 1.9 × 10⁻¹² of its surface intensity — essentially zero, so that the natural illumination at the Atlantis depth is a dim, monochromatic blue-green with a peak wavelength of approximately 480 nm and an intensity that, even at midday with a clear sky and calm seas, is only approximately 0.5 to 1.0 lux, equivalent to twilight conditions and insufficient for the detailed visual observation of boulder trajectories, marine life, or hull condition, necessitating the external illumination array that restores the full-spectrum apparent illumination to approximately 100 to 200 lux at a distance of 5 meters from the hull — a light level comparable to a well-lit office interior and sufficient for the visual acuity required for the boulder-tracking and inspection tasks) operates) and providing a field of view of approximately 120° in the horizontal direction and 90° in the vertical direction — a panoramic coverage that allows the operator to monitor the full forward hemisphere of the pod's surroundings from a single observation station, with the remaining blind spots (the aft hemisphere and the regions obscured by the inter-pod tunnel connections and the ballast tank protrusions) covered by the external cameras described in PART-078; the structural stress distribution in the viewport under the external pressure, which for a flat disk is predominantly a bending stress with a maximum tensile stress at the center of the outer (water-contacting) surface of approximately σ_max = 0.75·(3 + ν)·(p·a²/t²), where a = 150 mm is the radius of the unsupported aperture, t = 55 mm is the inner pane thickness, ν ≈ 0.24 for ALON (measured by the ultrasonic pulse-echo method at 5 MHz), and p = 0.55 MPa is the maximum design pressure, giving σ_max ≈ 0.75 × 3.24 × (0.55 × 10⁶ × 0.150²/0.055²) ≈ 2.43 × (12,375/0.003025) ≈ 2.43 × 4.09 × 10⁶ ≈ 9.94 MPa — a modest stress level that is only 1.4 percent of the ALON flexural strength and that indicates the flat pane is more than adequate from a strength perspective at the design depth, but this calculation neglects the stress concentration at the viewport seat, where the flat pane rests on an O-ring groove machined into the ALON surface (a configuration that is standard for flat viewports in subsea applications, where the O-ring provides the pressure seal and the pane is held in place by the external pressure alone, without mechanical clamping — the "pressure-seated" design that is the standard for acrylic viewports per ASME PVHO-1 (Safety Standard for Pressure Vessels for Human Occupancy) and that has been extended to the ceramic ALON viewport with modifications to the O-ring groove geometry to accommodate the higher elastic modulus and lower Poisson's ratio of ALON compared to acrylic, specifically by increasing the groove depth by 25 percent (to accommodate the reduced compressive strain of the ALON under the sealing pressure, because the O-ring must be compressed by approximately 15 to 25 percent of its cross-sectional diameter to achieve a reliable seal, and the stiffer ALON compresses less under a given pressure, requiring a deeper groove to achieve the same percentage compression) and by increasing the groove's corner radius to 1.5 mm (to reduce the stress concentration at the sharp corner of the groove, which in an acrylic viewport is typically left sharp because the acrylic's ductility accommodates the local stress concentration without cracking, but which in the brittle ALON would initiate a crack from the machining flaw at the groove corner under the sustained compressive load, a failure mode that the PVHO-1 standard explicitly warns against and that the ALON-specific groove geometry avoids through the generous corner radius, which reduces the stress concentration factor from approximately 4 to 5 (sharp corner) to approximately 2.0 to 2.5 (1.5 mm radius), a reduction that, when combined with the already-modest nominal stress of 10 MPa, yields a peak stress at the groove corner of approximately 25 MPa — still only 3.6 percent of the ALON flexural strength, a comfortable margin that confirms the flat pane's viability). However, the flat pane's optical distortion penalty — the pincushion effect described above — was assessed through a human-factors evaluation in which trained operators attempted to track a moving target (a scale model boulder, 0.15 meters in diameter, suspended from a motorized carriage and moving at a representative velocity of 5 m/s at a distance of 10 meters, simulating the visual conditions during the recession phase) through both a flat ALON pane and a curved ALON pane of the specified geometry, with the operators' tracking accuracy measured by the pointing error (the angular deviation between the operator's indicated target position and the true target position, measured by a laser pointer mounted on the operator's headset and a retroreflective target on the boulder model — a methodology adapted from the US Navy's Submarine Periscope Operator Training program and validated through the comparison of the tracking errors with the known optical distortion of the periscope optics, which are well-characterized and which provide a calibration baseline for the Atlantis viewport tracking test), and the results showed that the flat pane produced a root-mean-square tracking error of 3.8 ± 0.6 degrees at the 20° off-axis angle (the target positioned 20° from the center of the field of view), compared to 0.9 ± 0.3 degrees for the curved pane — a factor-of-4.2 improvement that the human-factors analysis translates directly into a probability of boulder impact detection: an error of 3.8 degrees corresponds to a lateral position uncertainty of approximately 0.66 meters at a distance of 10 meters, meaning that the operator cannot reliably determine whether a boulder is on a collision course with the hull (which has a maximum radius of 2.03 meters and therefore subtends an angle of approximately 11.5° at a range of 10 meters) or will pass by at a safe standoff distance, whereas the 0.9-degree error reduces the position uncertainty to approximately 0.16 meters — comparable to the boulder's own diameter — and allows the operator to reliably discriminate between a collision-course and a near-miss trajectory, a capability that is essential for the turbidite defense system's pre-impact warning function, which relies on the operator (or the automated boulder-tracking machine-vision system, which processes the video feed from the external cameras and the viewport-mounted borescope and which uses the same optical distortion correction algorithms that the curved pane's ray-tracing analysis validated) to identify the boulders that are on a collision course and to activate the acoustic cavitation bubble curtain at maximum power for the specific sector of the hull that the boulder will strike, focusing the defensive energy on the impact point and maximizing the probability of boulder deflection or fracture — a targeting function that the 0.9-degree tracking accuracy enables but that the 3.8-degree error of the flat pane would severely compromise, potentially missing the boulder entirely or directing the bubble curtain energy to the wrong sector and leaving the impact point unprotected.

The impact resistance of the ALON viewport against turbidite boulder strikes — the scenario in which a boulder of sufficient size and velocity to penetrate the auxetic cladding and the MXene coating impacts the viewport directly, a threat that the defense-in-depth architecture is designed to minimize but that cannot be eliminated entirely, as the viewport's transparent aperture cannot be covered by the opaque defensive layers and must rely on its intrinsic material properties and the sacrificial outer pane for protection — is quantified through the Hertzian contact mechanics model for the impact of a spherical indenter (the boulder, idealized as a sphere of radius R_boulder = 0.15 meters, representative of the 0.1 to 0.3 meter diameter range that the teardrop hull geometry allows to approach close enough to the hull surface to impact the viewport, with the larger boulders being deflected by the flow-channeling effect described in PART-041 and the smaller particles being stopped by the auxetic cladding and the CNT-polyurea coating) on a flat, elastic half-space (the ALON viewport outer surface, treated as an elastic half-space because the boulder radius is small compared to the viewport thickness of 55 mm — the stress field from the impact is localized to a region of diameter approximately 2 to 3 times the contact radius, which for the impact conditions described below is approximately 2 to 4 mm, far smaller than the viewport thickness, such that the half-space assumption is valid and the contact stress solution is not influenced by the presence of the inner surface at a depth of 55 mm). The maximum contact pressure at the center of the impact zone is given by the Hertz equation for elastic contact: p_0 = (3F/(2πa²)), where F is the impact force and a is the contact radius, with a = (3FR_eff/(4E_eff))^(1/3), where R_eff = (1/R_boulder + 1/R_viewport)^(-1) is the effective radius of curvature — the viewport outer surface is flat (infinite radius of curvature) for the purposes of the impact analysis, as the curvature of the outer pane over the 300 mm aperture is negligible compared to the boulder's radius, with the outer surface's spherical radius of R_outer = R_inner + t_pane = 350 + 80 = 430 mm (the combined thickness of the outer and inner panes plus the gap between them, which is 5 mm and is filled with an index-matching optical gel — a silicone-based material with a refractive index of n_gel = 1.53, intermediate between the ALON's 1.79 and the seawater's 1.34, and selected to minimize the reflection losses at the pane-to-gel interfaces, which would otherwise reduce the transmitted light intensity by approximately 8 to 12 percent per uncoated interface (the Fresnel reflection at a normal-incidence interface between two media of refractive indices n₁ and n₂ is R = ((n₁ - n₂)/(n₁ + n₂))², which for the ALON-seawater interface is ((1.79 - 1.34)/(1.79 + 1.34))² ≈ 0.021, or 2.1 percent, and for the three interfaces in the double-pane viewport (seawater-to-ALON_outer, ALON_outer-to-gel, gel-to-ALON_inner), the cumulative reflection loss without index matching would be approximately 6.3 percent, reducing the transmitted light and the image brightness accordingly, a loss that the index-matching gel reduces to approximately 0.5 percent per interface, preserving the image quality), such that the effective radius is essentially R_eff ≈ R_boulder = 0.15 m, and E_eff = ((1 - ν_boulder²)/E_boulder + (1 - ν_ALON²)/E_ALON)^(-1) is the effective elastic modulus of the boulder-ALON contact pair — the boulder is granite, with E_boulder ≈ 70 GPa and ν_boulder ≈ 0.25, and ALON has E_ALON ≈ 334 GPa and ν_ALON ≈ 0.24, giving E_eff = ((1 - 0.0625)/(70 × 10⁹) + (1 - 0.0576)/(334 × 10⁹))^(-1) = ((0.9375/70 + 0.9424/334) × 10⁻⁹)^(-1) = ((0.01339 + 0.00282) × 10⁻⁹)^(-1) = (0.01621 × 10⁻⁹)^(-1) ≈ 61.7 GPa — the effective modulus is dominated by the softer granite boulder, a result that is favorable for the ALON viewport because the boulder deforms more during impact, increasing the contact area and reducing the contact pressure compared to an impact with a harder boulder (such as a quartzite boulder, with E ≈ 90 to 100 GPa, which would increase E_eff to approximately 75 to 80 GPa and the contact pressure by approximately 10 to 15 percent, a variation that the impact analysis bounds by considering both granite and quartzite as the extreme boulder lithologies based on the mineralogical survey of the Monterey Canyon's catchment area — the Santa Lucia Range and the Gabilan Range, which are underlain primarily by the Salinian Block granitic rocks (quartz diorite, granodiorite, and granite) and the Franciscan Complex metasedimentary rocks (greywacke, argillite, and greenstone, with metamorphic grades from zeolite to blueschist facies, the higher-grade metamorphics producing quartz-rich lithologies with hardnesses approaching that of quartzite), such that the boulder population during a crustal displacement event will consist of a mixture of granitic and metasedimentary lithologies, and the impact analysis must conservatively assume the harder metasedimentary lithologies for the worst-case contact pressure. For a boulder of mass m = (4/3)πR_boulder³·ρ_boulder = (4/3)π × 0.15³ × 2,700 ≈ 38.2 kg, impacting the viewport at a velocity of v = 20 m/s (the mid-range of the 15 to 30 m/s turbidite flow velocities, and the most probable velocity for a boulder that has been entrained in the flow for a sufficient distance to reach the flow velocity — the particle Reynolds number Re_p = ρ_water·v·(2R_boulder)/μ = 1,027 × 20 × 0.3/(1.39 × 10⁻³) ≈ 4.43 × 10⁶, indicating that the boulder is in the fully turbulent drag regime and will accelerate to within approximately 80 to 90 percent of the flow velocity within a distance of approximately 10 to 20 body diameters — approximately 3 to 6 meters — which for a boulder entrained at the canyon head (approximately 8 km from the site) means it will have ample distance to reach the flow velocity well before arriving at the Atlantis site), the impact kinetic energy is KE = ½mv² = 0.5 × 38.2 × 400 = 7,640 J — approximately three-quarters of the 10⁷ J energy cited in the parent document's executive summary, which referred to the largest boulders of 3 meters diameter, not the 0.3-meter boulders that the viewport is fracture the outer pane (as intended, since the outer pane is sacrificial and is designed to fracture at impact loads far below the inner pane's fracture threshold), but that for the inner pane — which is protected by the outer pane's sacrificial fracture, the 5 mm index-matching gel gap (which attenuates the stress wave transmitted from the outer to the inner pane through the impedance mismatch between the ALON (acoustic impedance Z = ρ·c_L ≈ 3,680 × 10,200 ≈ 3.75 × 10⁷ kg/(m²·s)) and the gel (Z ≈ 1,200 × 1,200 ≈ 1.44 × 10⁶ kg/(m²·s)), a mismatch factor of approximately 26 that factor of 1/(1 + (2.84)²) ≈ 1/9.06 ≈ 0.11, reducing the transmitted stress to approximately 11 percent of the outer-surface peak, which when combined with the 7 percent transmission through the flexural strength, well within the elastic range and confirming that the inner pane will survive the worst-case boulder impact without damage) — the peak stress on the inner pane is further reduced by the fact that the outer pane's fracture absorbs the majority of the impact energy: the fracture energy of the ALON outer pane, with a fracture toughness of K_Ic ≈ 2.0 MPa·√m and a crack propagation distance of approximately 100 to 150 mm (the outer pane fractures into several large fragments rather than shattering into many small pieces — the "large-fragment" fracture mode of ALON, which is a 70 to 80 percent of the intragranular strength for hot-pressed ALON, a microstructural feature that is deliberately promoted by the addition of 0.1 weight percent of yttria (Y₂O₃) to the ALON powder before hot-pressing, which segregates to the grain boundaries and reduces the grain boundary strength slightly, promoting the intergranular fracture mode that produces larger fragments and absorbs more energy than the transgranular fracture mode, a toughening mechanism analogous to the "weak interface" toughening of fiber-reinforced ceramics, where the crack deflection at the grain boundaries increases the fracture surface area and the energy absorption), consumes approximately Γ = K_Ic²/E_ALON · A_fracture ≈ (2.0 × 10⁶)²/(334 × 10⁹) × (π × 0.15 × 0.1) ≈ (4.0 × 10¹²/(3.34 × 10¹¹)) × (0.0471) ≈ 11.98 × 0.0471 ≈ 0.564 J per unit fracture area, and for a total fracture area of approximately 0.05 m² (the outer pane fractures into approximately 4 to 6 fragments, with a total fracture surface area of approximately 0.04 to 0.06 m²), the total energy absorbed by the fracture is approximately 0.564 × 0.05 ≈ 0.028 J — a negligible fraction of the 7,640 J impact energy, indicating that the fracture of the outer pane absorbs almost none of the impact energy (the energy goes primarily into the elastic deformation of the boulder and the ALON, the kinetic energy of the ejected outer-pane fragments, and the plastic deformation of the boulder surface — granite is not entirely elastic at the contact pressures of 5 GPa, which exceed the granite's unconfined compressive strength of approximately 200 to 300 MPa by a factor of 17 to 25, and the boulder surface will undergo extensive crushing and pulverization during the impact, absorbing a significant fraction of the impact energy through the comminution work — the energy required to crush the rock to a given fragment size, which for granite is approximately 10 to 20 J/kg for a fragment size of 1 to 10 mm (the Bond work index for granite, a measure of the energy required to reduce the rock from a theoretically infinite feed size to a product with 80 percent passing a 100 μm sieve, is approximately 15 to 18 kWh/ton, or approximately 54 to 65 kJ/kg, but this value applies to the complete comminution of the rock to a fine powder, and the energy required for the localized crushing of the boulder surface during the impact — affecting only the outer 20 to 50 mm of the boulder's radius, or approximately 15 to 40 percent of the boulder's volume — is proportionally lower, approximately 8 to 26 kJ for the impacted boulder, which is comparable to the impact kinetic energy of 7.6 kJ and which confirms that the majority of the impact energy is absorbed by the boulder's crushing, not by the ALON viewport — a fortuitous result of the boulder being softer than the viewport, which makes the viewport the "anvil" and the boulder the "hammer" that breaks upon the anvil, rather than the reverse, a critical asymmetry that the ALON material selection exploits and that would be reversed if a softer viewport material (such as acrylic, with a Vickers hardness of approximately 0.2 GPa and a flexural strength of approximately 100 MPa) were used — in that case, the viewport would be the softer material, and the boulder would remain intact while the viewport fractured, a failure mode that the ALON's hardness and strength specifically prevent and that justifies the approximately 15-fold cost premium of ALON ($8,000 to $12,000 per viewport pane, including the hot-pressing, hot-isostatic-pressing, grinding, and polishing) over acrylic ($600 to $900 per viewport pane) for the Atlantis application, where the viewport's survivability during a boulder impact is not a desirable feature but an absolute requirement for the pod's continued habitability after the crustal displacement event.

The panoramic geometry of the viewport — the spherical sector that provides the 120° horizontal × 90° vertical field of view — is achieved by the curved inner pane geometry described above, supplemented by a fixed external mirror assembly that extends the field of view to cover the hull surface regions that are not directly visible through the viewport, specifically the regions below the pod's equator and aft of the viewport's tangent plane, which the operator cannot see directly but which are the most likely impact locations for turbidite boulders approaching from the aft direction (the boulders that have passed the pod's equator and are moving downstream, having been deflected by the hull's streamlining but still posing a threat to the aft penetrations — the inter-pod tunnel connections, the ballast system discharge ports, and the auxiliary thruster nozzles — which must be visually monitored during the event to confirm that they have not been damaged and are not leaking or flooding). The mirror assembly consists of three ALON-coated (the ALON coating on the mirror is a 0.5 μm thick layer of ALON deposited by the same EPD process used for the hull coating, providing the mirror with the same corrosion resistance and self-healing properties as the hull, ensuring that the mirror remains optically clear and reflective for the 50-year service life despite the continuous seawater immersion and the occasional biofouling — the mirror surface is oriented downward (the mirror is mounted above the viewport, looking downward at a 45° angle, such that the mirror's optical surface is
