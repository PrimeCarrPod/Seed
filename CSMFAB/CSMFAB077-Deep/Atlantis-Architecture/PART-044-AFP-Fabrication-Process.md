## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The fabrication of the Atlantis-class prolate spheroid pressure hull — a 12-meter-long, 4.06-meter-maximum-diameter, 40-millimeter-thick laminated composite shell with a 3.15:1 aspect ratio, fabricated from the LBFRP-001 material system (basalt fiber/Elium thermoplastic acrylic resin/5 percent lonsdaleite nanodiamond in the outer 5 millimeters) using the automated fiber placement process — represents one of the most challenging composite manufacturing tasks ever undertaken outside of the aerospace industry, combining the geometric complexity of a doubly-curved, variable-curvature surface (with the meridional radius of curvature varying by a factor of nearly 30 from the equator to the poles, as quantified in PART-042, and the associated challenges of tow steering on surfaces with rapidly changing curvature that can cause tow buckling on the compressive side of a steered path or tow splitting on the tensile side) with the material-specific challenges of processing a reactive thermoplastic resin system (the Elium acrylic, which polymerizes in situ during the laser heating and consolidation stage, converting from a low-viscosity monomer — approximately 100 mPa·s at 25°C — to a fully polymerized thermoplastic with a glass transition temperature of 122°C within the approximately 0.5 to 2.0 seconds that the laser-illuminated zone dwells on any given point of the laminate surface, a curing kinetics window that is sufficiently fast to enable the continuous tow placement rates of 0.3 to 0.5 meters per second that are necessary for economic fabrication of a 78-square-meter hull surface, but that is also sufficiently slow — relative to the millisecond-scale cure times of UV-cured thermoset resins — that the process control must precisely synchronize the laser power, the tow feed rate, the compaction roller pressure, and the tool surface temperature to achieve consistent interlaminar bonding without either under-curing (which leaves residual monomer that plasticizes the matrix and reduces the glass transition temperature and the moisture resistance) or over-curing (which can cause thermal degradation of the polymer, chain scission, and the generation of volatile degradation products that create porosity in the laminate, a defect that is as detrimental to the laminate's pressure-bearing capacity as incomplete curing, because porosity — even at levels as low as 1 to 2 percent by volume — reduces the interlaminar shear strength by 5 to 7 percent per percent of void content, a linear degradation relationship that has been well-established in the composites literature and that motivates the stringent void content specification of less than 1 percent by volume for the Atlantis hull, a target that is achievable with the AFP process but that requires continuous in-situ monitoring of the consolidation quality, as described in Section 5). This part documents the complete AFP fabrication process for the Atlantis hull, beginning with the tooling design (the male mandrel on which the laminate is laid up, which must maintain its geometry to within ±0.5 mm over the 12-meter length despite the thermal expansion and contraction cycles of the layup and curing process, a tolerance requirement that drove the selection of Invar-36 — a nickel-iron alloy with a coefficient of thermal expansion of approximately 1.2 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹, roughly one-tenth that of steel — as the mandrel material, at a cost of approximately $380,000 for the 12-meter mandrel, which is amortized over the production run of ten pods plus the spare mandrel for the Osa site, bringing the per-pod tooling cost to approximately $38,000, or 1.7 percent of the per-pod fabrication cost, a justifiable investment given the geometric precision it enables) through the tow placement kinematics, the laser heating and consolidation physics, the tow-steering algorithms for the prolate spheroid geometry, and the integrated defect detection and quality assurance system that ensures every pod meets the structural certification requirements of the ABS Rules for Underwater Vehicles and the ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code Section X for fiber-reinforced plastic pressure vessels.

## 1. TOOLING DESIGN AND MANDREL ENGINEERING

The male mandrel on which the Atlantis hull laminate is laid up is a precision-ground Invar-36 shell with a wall thickness of 12 millimeters, supported by an internal steel framework of longitudinal stringers and circumferential ring frames that maintain the mandrel's geometry under the combined loads of its own weight (approximately 2,800 kg for the Invar shell), the laminate weight during layup (which accumulates from zero at the start of fabrication to approximately 4,200 kg — the weight of the 40-mm-thick, 78 m² laminate — at completion), and the compaction roller force (approximately 200 to 400 N per tow, applied normal to the mandrel surface by the AFP head's compaction roller, which is a pneumatically actuated, silicone-rubber-coated roller of 50 mm diameter that deforms slightly to conform to the curved surface and distribute the compaction force over a contact area of approximately 10 mm × 25 mm, producing a compaction pressure of approximately 0.8 to 1.6 MPa, which is sufficient to consolidate the incoming tow against the previously laid plies and to squeeze out any trapped air or excess resin from the tow-tow interfaces). The mandrel surface is ground to a finish of 0.8 μm Ra and is coated with a semi-permanent release agent — a siloxane-based mold release that is baked onto the mandrel surface at 200°C for 2 hours to crosslink the siloxane network and provide a durable, chemically inert release surface that withstands the laser heating temperatures (the laminate surface directly under the laser spot reaches approximately 180 to 220°C, sufficient to melt the Elium resin for consolidation but well below the 1,450°C melting point of Invar-36 and the 200°C maximum service temperature of the release agent) without degrading or transferring to the laminate surface, which would compromise the subsequent bonding of the MXene electrophoretic deposition coating described in PART-045. The mandrel is mounted on a horizontal-axis trunnion fixture that allows rotation about the mandrel's longitudinal axis at speeds of up to 30 revolutions per minute, synchronized with the AFP head's motion along the meridional direction (the x-axis of the AFP machine, which is a 15-meter-long linear rail with a positioning accuracy of ±0.05 mm over the full travel, driven by a linear motor with a Renishaw optical encoder providing position feedback at a resolution of 0.1 μm), such that the AFP head can place tows on any point of the mandrel surface by coordinated motion of the head along the meridional axis and the mandrel rotation about its longitudinal axis — a two-axis kinematic scheme that is adequate for a body-of-revolution geometry, where every point on the surface is accessible by a combination of a meridional translation and a circumferential rotation, simplifying the machine design compared to the six-axis articulated robots used for complex aerospace structures with non-axisymmetric geometries such as wing skins and fuselage sections, and thereby reducing the machine cost to approximately $2.8 million (for the 15-meter AFP gantry with the integrated laser heating system, the tow creel with 16 individually tension-controlled spools, and the Siemens 840D CNC controller with custom postprocessor software that converts the laminate stacking sequence and the mandrel geometry into G-code toolpaths optimized for the prolate spheroid surface), a cost that is amortized over not only the ten Atlantis pods but also the ongoing production of the Safehold STR-002 cylindrical pressure vessels for the Lonsdaleite Outskirt Communities program (CSMFAB068), which uses the same AFP machine for their cylindrical hulls, increasing the machine utilization and reducing the effective cost per Atlantis pod to approximately $280,000.

The tow placement kinematics on the prolate spheroid surface require that the AFP head's compaction roller remain normal to the local surface tangent at every point along the tow path, a condition that is satisfied by orienting the head's approach vector — the vector from the head's wrist center to the compaction roller contact point — parallel to the local surface normal, which for the prolate spheroid is given by n(φ, θ) = (cos φ cos θ, cos φ sin θ, sin φ·(1 - e² cos² φ)^(1/2))/((1 - e² sin² φ)^(1/2)), where e = √(1 - (b/a)²) is the eccentricity of the meridian ellipse, which for the 3.15:1 aspect ratio with a = 6.3 m and b = 2.03 m gives e = √(1 - 0.322²) = √(1 - 0.1038) = √0.8962 = 0.947, and the surface normal vector deviates from the radial direction (the direction from the mandrel axis to the surface point) by an angle of up to approximately 15° at the φ = ±60° latitude, where the meridional curvature is changing most rapidly, requiring the AFP head to tilt by a corresponding angle to maintain normal contact — a motion that is accomplished by the head's servo-controlled tilt axis, which has an angular range of ±20° and a resolution of 0.01°, sufficient for the prolate spheroid geometry. The tow steering — the intentional deviation of the tow path from the geodesic (shortest-path) direction on the surface, which is necessary to achieve the specified fiber orientation angles at every point on the hull — is constrained by the maximum steering curvature that the tow can sustain without buckling or splitting, which for the 300-tex basalt fiber tow with a width of 6.35 mm (¼ inch) and a thickness of approximately 0.25 mm after consolidation, is approximately κ_max = 0.025 mm⁻¹, corresponding to a minimum steering radius of R_min = 1/κ_max = 40 mm, below which the tow's inner edge (on the compressive side of the steered path) buckles out of the plane, creating a local defect that compromises the fiber continuity and the laminate strength at that location — the "tow buckling" defect that is one of the most common defects in AFP of doubly-curved surfaces and that the Atlantis process avoids by ensuring that the specified fiber orientations (0°, ±45°, 90°) can be achieved on the prolate spheroid surface with steering curvatures below the buckling threshold at all points, a condition that was verified through the differential geometry analysis of the prolate spheroid, which relates the tow path curvature to the specified fiber angle and the surface curvature through the equation κ_tow = κ_g + (dθ/ds), where κ_g is the geodesic curvature of the tow path on the surface (the curvature of the path as measured within the surface, which depends on the surface's Gaussian curvature and the angle of the path relative to the principal curvature directions), dθ/ds is the rate of change of the fiber angle along the path (the "steering rate"), and κ_tow is the total curvature of the tow in its plane (the plane tangent to the surface at each point), which must be maintained below κ_max. For the Atlantis hull, the geodesic curvature of a path at 0° (aligned with the meridians) is zero — the meridians are geodesics of the surface, meaning that a path following a meridian is a straight line on the surface and does not require steering, and the AFP head simply follows the meridian, placing the tow with no lateral deviation and therefore no risk of tow buckling, a property that simplifies the fabrication of the 43.75 percent of the plies that are at 0°. For the ±45° plies, the geodesic curvature is non-zero and varies with latitude, reaching a maximum at the φ = ±70° latitudes where the meridional curvature is changing most rapidly, and the steering rate dθ/ds must be adjusted along the path to maintain the constant 45° angle relative to the meridians — a path that, on the prolate spheroid, is a loxodrome-like curve (not an exact loxodrome, which on a sphere maintains a constant angle relative to the meridians only for the sphere, not for the ellipsoid, where the angle between the path and the meridians varies along the path unless the path is specifically designed to maintain the constant angle, a path-finding problem that the AFP postprocessor solves by numerically integrating the differential equation for the fiber angle along the path using the surface's first fundamental form — the metric tensor g_ij, which for the prolate spheroid in the (φ, θ) parameterization is g_φφ = r_1² = (a²b²/(a² sin² φ + b² cos² φ)^(3/2))², g_θθ = r_0² = (b cos φ)², and g_φθ = 0 (the parameterization is orthogonal, meaning the coordinate lines intersect at right angles, a property that simplifies the path planning because the fiber angle relative to the φ-direction is directly the angle of the tow path relative to the meridians), and imposing the constant-angle condition dθ/dφ = (√(g_φφ)/√(g_θθ)) · tan θ_fiber to generate the toolpath — a calculation that the postprocessor performs for each of the 32 plies in the laminate, requiring approximately 15 minutes of computation time on the Siemens 840D controller's onboard Intel Core i7 processor (4 cores, 3.2 GHz) but producing toolpaths that guarantee the fiber angle is within ±1.5° of the specified value at every point on the hull surface, a tolerance that is tighter than the ±3° typical of aerospace AFP specifications and that is achieved through the combination of the precision Invar mandrel, the linear-motor-driven axes, and the Renishaw encoder feedback that together provide the geometric accuracy necessary for the tight-angle tolerance.

## 2. LASER HEATING AND IN-SITU CONSOLIDATION PHYSICS

The laser heating system is the heart of the AFP process for the Elium thermoplastic laminate, because the Elium resin is a reactive thermoplastic — it is applied to the basalt fiber tows as a low-viscosity liquid monomer (a mixture of methyl methacrylate monomer, a peroxide initiator, and the lonsdaleite nanodiamond particles dispersed in the monomer by the ultrasonication and high-shear mixing protocol described in PART-043) during the "tow impregnation" stage, which occurs immediately upstream of the AFP head where the dry basalt fiber tow passes through a resin bath and emerges with a resin content of approximately 42 to 48 percent by weight (corresponding to a fiber volume fraction of approximately 52 to 58 percent after polymerization, with the 55 percent target being achieved through the precise control of the tow tension and the compaction roller pressure, which together determine the amount of resin squeezed out of the tow during consolidation and the final fiber volume fraction), and the polymerization is initiated by the heat from the laser — a 4 kW diode laser operating at a wavelength of 980 nm, which is absorbed by the carbon-based Elium resin (the absorption coefficient of the clear acrylic resin at 980 nm is relatively low, approximately 0.5 to 1.0 mm⁻¹, meaning that the laser penetrates approximately 1 to 2 millimeters into the resin before being attenuated to 1/e of its incident intensity, a penetration depth that is sufficient to heat the incoming tow through its entire 0.25 mm thickness and to re-melt the surface layer — approximately 0.1 to 0.2 mm deep — of the previously placed, already-polymerized ply, creating a melt-bonded interface where the polymer chains from the new tow interdiffuse across the interface with the chains from the underlying ply, forming entanglements and covalent bonds (if residual initiator in the underlying ply's surface triggers additional polymerization at the interface) that provide the interlaminar strength, with the interlaminar shear strength of the AFP-consolidated interface being approximately 85 to 95 percent of the bulk matrix shear strength — a reduction relative to the bulk that is inherent to the in-situ consolidation process, because the interdiffusion time (approximately 0.1 to 0.5 seconds, the dwell time of the laser-illuminated zone as it passes over the interface) is insufficient for complete chain entanglement equilibrium, but that is acceptable for the Atlantis application because the interlaminar shear stress safety factor of 1.5 to 1.7 (as determined in PART-043) is based on the 85-percent interface strength, providing a conservative design that accounts for the AFP-specific interface quality.

The laser heating parameters — the laser power P, the laser spot size on the laminate surface (a 25 mm × 15 mm elliptical spot, with the major axis aligned with the tow direction to provide the extended dwell time necessary for through-thickness heating of the incoming tow and the underlying ply surface, the spot dimensions being selected to achieve a dwell time of t_dwell = L_spot/v_tow ≈ 0.025/0.4 ≈ 0.0625 seconds at the nominal tow placement rate of 0.4 m/s, which with the 4 kW laser power distributed over an area of approximately 375 mm² yields a heat flux of q'' = 4,000/0.000375 ≈ 10.67 MW/m², sufficient to raise the temperature of the 0.25 mm thick incoming tow from the ambient temperature of 25°C to the polymerization temperature of approximately 180°C within the dwell time, based on the energy balance: ρ·c_p·t_tow·ΔT = η·q''·t_dwell, where ρ = 1,850 kg/m³ is the tow density, c_p = 1,200 J/(kg·K) is the specific heat capacity of the basalt-Elium composite, t_tow = 0.25 × 10⁻³ m is the tow thickness, ΔT = 155 K is the required temperature rise, η ≈ 0.45 is the laser energy absorption efficiency (the fraction of the incident laser power that is actually absorbed by the tow and the underlying ply surface, rather than being reflected, scattered, or conducted away into the previously laid plies — a value that was determined by calorimetric calibration, in which a thermocouple embedded at the interface between two plies measures the temperature rise during the laser pass, and the absorbed energy is computed from the temperature rise and the known heat capacity of the material, yielding an absorption efficiency of 0.42 to 0.48 depending on the fiber angle of the underlying ply, which affects the reflectivity of the surface — 0° plies, with the fibers aligned parallel to the laser's polarization direction (the diode laser is polarized, and the absorption is higher when the electric field vector is parallel to the fiber direction, because the conductive basalt fibers interact more strongly with the parallel-polarized light), absorb approximately 10 percent more of the incident energy than 90° plies, with the fibers perpendicular to the polarization, a variation that is compensated by the laser power control loop, which adjusts the laser power in real-time based on the fiber angle of the ply being placed, using a lookup table of absorption efficiency versus fiber angle that was generated during the process calibration phase), and the computed required heat flux is q''_required = 1,850 × 1,200 × 0.25 × 10⁻³ × 155/(0.45 × 0.0625) ≈ 10.2 MW/m², which is within 5 percent of the actual heat flux, indicating the laser system can achieve the required temperature with approximately 5 percent power margin for process variations such as changes in the ambient temperature, the tow moisture content (which increases the effective heat capacity due to the latent heat of vaporization of the absorbed water), and the mandrel temperature (which is maintained at 60°C by internal electric heating elements embedded in the Invar shell, a pre-heating that reduces the ΔT required from the laser by 35°C, from 180°C to 145°C of net laser-induced temperature rise, decreasing the required laser power by approximately 23 percent and allowing the use of a 4 kW laser rather than the 5 to 6 kW laser that would be required if the mandrel were at ambient temperature, a cost saving that partially offsets the additional complexity and energy consumption of the heated mandrel) — and the laser spot size, the tow placement speed, and the laser power are thus the three interrelated parameters whose optimization determines the process window for consistent consolidation, with the optimization performed through a design-of-experiments study that varied the three parameters across a 5×5×5 factorial grid (125 combinations) and measured the resulting interlaminar shear strength of flat-coupon AFP-produced laminates using the short-beam shear test (ASTM D2344), identifying the process window of v_tow = 0.35 to 0.50 m/s, P = 3,600 to 4,200 W, and L_spot = 22 to 28 mm (with the width fixed at 15 mm) within which the interlaminar shear strength exceeds 38 MPa (85 percent of the bulk matrix strength of 45 MPa) and the void content is below 0.8 percent by volume, a window that accommodates the full range of fiber angles and surface curvatures on the Atlantis hull and that is wide enough — with ±15 percent margins on each parameter — that the normal process variations (tow-tension fluctuations of ±5 N, resin content variations of ±2 percent, and ambient temperature variations of ±5°C) do not push the process outside the acceptable window.

## 3. TOW PATH PLANNING AND STEERING ON THE PROLATE SPHEROID

The tow path planning algorithm that converts the laminate stacking sequence — a specification of the fiber angle and the ply boundary (the region of the hull surface over which the ply extends, which for the Atlantis hull is the entire hull surface for all plies, with the exception of the local reinforcement doublers at the penetrations, which are fabricated as separate, pre-consolidated laminate patches that are co-bonded to the hull during the AFP process by placing them on the partially completed laminate and then continuing the AFP layup over them, encapsulating the doublers within the laminate stack — analogous to the "pad-up" fabrication technique used in composite aircraft structures to locally thicken the skin around access holes and attachment points) into a sequence of ordered tow paths that the AFP head can execute — is a computational geometry problem that involves the interaction of three constraints: the geometric constraint that the tow must lie on the prolate spheroid surface (satisfied by expressing the tow centerline path as a parametric curve on the surface, (φ(s), θ(s)), where s is the arc length along the path), the kinematic constraint that the tow's curvature κ_tow(s) must not exceed the maximum steering curvature κ_max at any point (the tow buckling and splitting criteria, which for the basalt fiber tow with the Elium wet-out have been experimentally determined to be κ_max = 0.025 mm⁻¹ for tows placed on a convex surface — the tow's inner edge is in compression, and the critical compressive strain for buckling of the individual filaments is approximately 0.5 to 0.8 percent, corresponding to a curvature of κ = ε_crit/(w/2) = 0.006/(6.35/2) ≈ 0.0019 mm⁻¹ for the full tow width, but the tow's internal structure — the filaments are not rigidly bonded together in the dry state, allowing them to slide relative to each other and to accommodate curvature by differential stretching of the individual filaments rather than by buckling of the tow as a whole — increases the effective maximum curvature to approximately 0.025 mm⁻¹, a factor of 13 above the simple Euler buckling estimate, and this value has been confirmed by the tow-steering trials on the Invar mandrel at the Mojave AFP facility, where tows were steered along paths of increasing curvature until buckling was visually detected by the in-process camera system, establishing κ_max as a conservative, experimentally validated limit that the path planning algorithm respects through a hard constraint on the maximum curvature), and the coverage constraint that the tows must cover the entire hull surface with no gaps larger than 1.0 mm between adjacent tows (the "gap" specification, which is tighter than the 2.5 mm typical of aerospace AFP because the Atlantis hull's external pressure resistance depends on the continuity of the laminate — gaps larger than approximately 2 mm create resin-rich regions that have lower stiffness and strength than the fiber-reinforced regions, and under the biaxial compressive loading, these resin-rich regions act as stress concentrations that can initiate matrix cracking, which then propagates along the fiber-matrix interface and degrades the laminate's pressure resistance, a degradation that the tighter gap specification avoids by ensuring that any resin-rich region is small enough that its effect on the overall laminate stiffness and strength is negligible — less than 0.5 percent reduction in the laminate's effective modulus based on the rule-of-mixtures averaging of the fiber-rich and resin-rich regions — and that the crack-initiating effect of the largest permitted gap (1.0 mm) is below the threshold stress intensity factor for matrix crack propagation in the Elium resin, K_Ic ≈ 1.2 MPa·√m for the neat resin, which for a 1.0 mm resin-rich region subjected to the laminate's compressive stress of approximately 12 MPa (at the equator, the lowest-stress region) gives a stress intensity factor of approximately K_I ≈ σ·√(πa) ≈ 12 × √(π × 0.0005) ≈ 0.48 MPa·√m — far below K_Ic — confirming that cracks will not propagate from the maximum-size gap, even under the maximum design pressure, a margin that the tighter gap specification provides but that the looser aerospace specification of 2.5 mm gaps would not, for which K_I ≈ 0.76 MPa·√m, approaching the threshold for slow crack growth under sustained loading in the presence of moisture, a risk that the Atlantis specification eliminates).

The toolpath generation for the 0° plies is straightforward: the AFP head follows the meridians from one pole to the other (or from the equator to one pole and then, with the mandrel rotated by the ply staggering angle, from the equator to the other pole, a procedure that avoids the accumulation of tow-start and tow-end defects at the poles, where all meridians converge and the tows would otherwise overlap and create a thickness buildup that is structurally undesirable because it concentrates stress at the pole — the most highly stressed region of the hull — and that the staggered-start procedure avoids by starting each tow at a different latitude, offset by 10 to 15 mm from the adjacent tows, so that the tow starts and ends are distributed over a band of latitudes rather than concentrated at the poles, a fiber-architecture optimization that the Atlantis laminate design specifies and that the AFP postprocessor implements automatically through the tow-placement sequencing algorithm). For each meridian, the AFP head places a tow of 6.35 mm width, and adjacent tows are placed with an overlap of 0.5 mm (the standard AFP practice, which provides a small overlap to ensure gap-free coverage despite the minor variations in the tow width and the placement position, with the overlap region being approximately 7.9 percent of the tow width and the associated thickness increase — approximately 8 percent thicker in the overlap region, or approximately 0.02 mm for a 0.25 mm thick ply — being distributed over multiple plies by the staggering algorithm, which offsets the tow-start positions of successive plies by 3.175 mm (half the tow width) so that the overlap regions of one ply fall under the centerlines of the tows in the next ply, a staggering pattern that homogenizes the thickness variation and prevents the systematic accumulation of thickness non-uniformities that would otherwise occur if the overlaps were aligned from ply to ply — a phenomenon known as "ply waviness" that can reduce the laminate's compressive strength by 5 to 10 percent, an avoidable degradation that the staggering algorithm eliminates.

The toolpath generation for the ±45° plies is significantly more complex, because a constant-angle path on the prolate spheroid is not a geodesic (except at the equator, where the circumferential direction is tangent to a parallel circle — a circle of constant latitude — and the path is a loxodrome-like curve that spirals around the hull, starting at one pole, winding around the body with a constant angle relative to the meridians, and terminating at the opposite pole, completing a full traversal of the hull in one continuous tow path if the path is followed for a sufficient distance, although in practice the path is broken into segments of finite length, each segment covering a fraction of the hull circumference, to avoid the accumulation of steering error over long distances and to allow the AFP head to reposition and restart at the beginning of each segment, a practical constraint that the postprocessor accommodates by dividing the ±45° plies into a series of "courses" — groups of parallel tow paths that cover a band of latitudes — with each course consisting of 10 to 20 individual tow paths that are parallel to each other (constant fiber angle) and that together cover the full circumference of the hull at that latitude band, the next course then covering the adjacent latitude band with a slight overlap to maintain the gap-free coverage, and the process continuing from the equator outward to both poles, with the number of courses per ply varying from approximately 4 (for the equatorial region, where the circumference is largest and each course can cover a wide latitude band, approximately 15 to 20 degrees of latitude) to approximately 12 (for the polar regions, where the circumference shrinks and more courses are needed to cover the full 360° with the desired tow width). The computational time for generating the complete set of tow paths for all 32 plies is approximately 45 minutes on the Siemens 840D controller, producing a toolpath file of approximately 8 to 12 GB (in the compressed binary CNC format), which is stored on the controller's solid-state drive and streamed to the AFP head's motion controller during fabrication at a rate synchronized with the tow placement speed — a data throughput of approximately 2 to 5 MB/s, well within the capabilities of the EtherCAT real-time fieldbus that connects the controller to the servo drives.

## 4. PROCESS PARAMETER CONTROL AND REAL-TIME ADAPTIVE ADJUSTMENT

The AFP process for the Atlantis hull operates under closed-loop control of three primary process parameters — the laser power, the tow tension, and the compaction roller force — with the control loops receiving feedback from an array of sensors integrated into the AFP head and the mandrel: an infrared pyrometer (measuring the laminate surface temperature immediately behind the laser spot at a wavelength of 8 to 14 μm, with a 2 mm spot size and a response time of 1 millisecond, providing the temperature feedback for the laser power control loop, which uses a proportional-integral-derivative (PID) controller tuned to a bandwidth of approximately 50 Hz — fast enough to respond to the temperature fluctuations caused by the varying tow absorption efficiency as the fiber angle changes along the path, a variation that occurs on a time scale of approximately 0.1 to 0.5 seconds (the time for the AFP head to traverse a 50 to 200 mm segment of the path, during which the fiber angle may change by 5 to 15 degrees if the path is on the polar region where the surface curvature is changing rapidly), and the PID controller adjusts the laser power to maintain the surface temperature within the 180 ± 10°C target window, with the controller gains — K_p = 0.8 W/°C, K_i = 0.3 W/(°C·s), K_d = 0.1 W·s/°C — determined through the Ziegler-Nichols tuning method applied to the step response of the laser power-to-surface-temperature transfer function, which is approximately first-order with a time constant of approximately 0.02 seconds (dominated by the thermal diffusion time through the 0.25 mm tow thickness) and a dead time of approximately 0.005 seconds (the transport delay between the laser spot and the pyrometer measurement point, separated by approximately 8 mm along the tow path and, at a tow speed of 0.4 m/s, the delay is 0.008/0.4 = 0.02 seconds — the dead time is therefore approximately 20 milliseconds, and the PID controller's bandwidth of 50 Hz corresponds to a response time of approximately 20 milliseconds, matching the dead time and providing the fastest possible response without inducing instability, a design that achieves a temperature control accuracy of ±5°C during steady-state placement and ±12°C during the transients at tow start and end, where the laser must rapidly ramp from zero to full power and from full to zero, respectively, over a distance of approximately 10 to 15 mm — the ramp distance being a compromise between the need to minimize the length of under-cured or over-cured tow at the start and end of each path (where the temperature is outside the process window, and the laminate quality is compromised — the tow start and end regions, extending approximately 10 to 15 mm from each extremity, are therefore designated as "process trim" that will be removed from the finished hull by the post-fabrication machining operations that trim the excess laminate at the hatch and penetration openings, so the degraded material at the tow ends is discarded and does not enter the final pressure-bearing structure, a scrap rate of approximately 2 to 4 percent of the total laminate mass that is accounted for in the material cost estimate and that is acceptable given the criticality of the pressure hull integrity) — a load cell embedded in the tow feed mechanism (measuring the tension in the tow as it enters the AFP head, with a range of 0 to 100 N and an accuracy of ±0.5 N, providing the feedback for the tow tension control loop, which adjusts the spool brake torque to maintain a tension of 15 ± 3 N — the tension being the key parameter controlling the fiber volume fraction, because higher tension squeezes more resin out of the tow during the compaction stage, increasing V_f, and lower tension retains more resin, decreasing V_f, with the 15 N tension producing V_f = 55 ± 2 percent, the target range, as verified by the burn-off tests (ASTM D2584) on witness coupons fabricated at the start and end of each ply, and the tension loop's bandwidth of approximately 10 Hz is sufficient to respond to the tension variations caused by the changing tow path geometry — the curvature of the path affects the frictional drag on the tow as it slides over the previously laid laminate surface, with higher curvature increasing the drag and requiring higher spool torque to maintain the tension, an effect that the tension controller compensates through the gain-scheduled PID algorithm that adjusts the controller gains based on the instantaneous path curvature, which is known from the pre-computed toolpath) — and a laser triangulation displacement sensor (measuring the distance from the AFP head to the laminate surface with an accuracy of ±0.02 mm, providing the feedback for the compaction roller position control loop, which maintains the roller at a standoff distance of 0.25 mm from the nominal laminate surface (the tow thickness) such that the roller compresses the incoming tow against the underlying plies with a force of approximately 300 ± 50 N, the force being determined by the pneumatic cylinder pressure rather than by the position control — the position control loop only ensures that the roller contacts the tow, and the force control loop, using a pressure regulator on the pneumatic cylinder supply, maintains the compaction force within the specified range, the dual-loop architecture reflecting the hierarchical control structure typical of AFP machines, where the position loop handles the gross positioning and the force loop handles the fine consolidation pressure).

The process parameter values derived from the optimization study and implemented in the production AFP controller are: tow placement speed v = 0.40 m/s (the nominal speed, with the speed reduced to 0.25 m/s for the polar regions — φ > 65° — where the surface curvature is highest and the risk of tow buckling or bridging (incomplete contact between the tow and the underlying laminate surface in regions of concave curvature, which for the prolate spheroid occurs near the poles where the meridional curvature transitions from convex to concave as the hull diameter decreases toward the axis, a geometric feature that is particularly challenging for the AFP process because the compaction roller cannot conform to a concave surface unless the roller diameter is smaller than the local radius of curvature — a condition that is satisfied by the 50 mm roller diameter and the minimum meridional radius of curvature at the poles of approximately 0.65 m (b²/a, for b = 2.03 m and a = 6.3 m, giving R₁_pole = 4.12/6.3 ≈ 0.654 m), which is 13 times the roller diameter, ensuring the roller can maintain contact without bridging, but the reduced speed in the polar regions provides an additional margin of process robustness, increasing the dwell time and the consolidation quality in the most geometrically challenging regions of the hull), laser power P = 3,900 W ± 200 W (adjusted in real-time by the temperature feedback loop), tow tension T = 15 ± 3 N, compaction roller force F = 300 ± 50 N, mandrel temperature T_mandrel = 60 ± 2°C (controlled by the electric heating elements with thermocouple feedback), and ambient air temperature in the AFP cell = 25 ± 3°C (controlled by the facility's HVAC system, which maintains the cell at positive pressure relative to the outside environment to prevent the ingress of dust and moisture that could contaminate the laminate — the cell is a Class 100,000 cleanroom per ISO 14644-1, with the air filtered through MERV-13 filters and the particle count monitored continuously by a laser particle counter, and the operator wear cleanroom suits with hairnets and booties to minimize the introduction of particulate contamination, with the target of less than 10 particles per cubic meter of size > 5 μm at the mandrel surface during layup, a cleanliness level that is modest compared to the aerospace cleanrooms (Class 10,000 or better) but that is adequate for the Atlantis laminate because the Elium thermoplastic, unlike the epoxy prepregs used in aerospace, does not require the extreme cleanliness to prevent the inhibition of the cure chemistry or the formation of voids at the particle sites — the thermoplastic's chemical resistance to contamination is one of its processing advantages, and the Class 100,000 cleanroom is sufficient to prevent the macroscopic inclusions (dust particles > 100 μm) that would create void defects visible in the ultrasonic inspection and that would exceed the 1 percent void content specification, with the smaller particles being homogeneously dispersed in the matrix and having negligible effect on the laminate properties at the 55 percent fiber volume fraction, where the matrix's contribution to the laminate properties is already secondary to the fibers' contribution).

## 5. IN-SITU DEFECT DETECTION AND QUALITY ASSURANCE

The quality assurance system for the Atlantis AFP process integrates three in-situ inspection modalities that operate continuously during fabrication, detecting defects as they form and either triggering an automated correction (for correctable defects, such as a temperature deviation that can be corrected by the laser power feedback loop) or flagging the defect location for post-fabrication repair (for non-correctable defects, such as a tow gap exceeding the 1.0 mm specification due to a tow-width variation or a placement-position error that cannot be corrected without stopping the process and manually repositioning the tow, which would introduce a process interruption and a corresponding thermal history discontinuity that could degrade the local laminate quality — the preferred approach is to continue fabrication and then repair the local defect after the AFP process is complete, by removing the defective material with a precision router and scarfing the repair region with a 20:1 taper ratio (the standard composite repair practice, where the scarf angle is approximately 3°, and the repair patch is built up ply-by-ply using the same AFP process but in a localized "patch placement" mode, with the patch plies overlapping the scarf and extending onto the undamaged laminate to transfer the load from the patch into the surrounding structure, a repair that, when properly executed, restores the laminate to 95 to 100 percent of its original strength — a restoration efficiency that the Atlantis quality plan requires for any repair region larger than 100 cm², with smaller regions (< 100 cm²) being acceptable with no repair because the stress redistribution around a small defect drops the local stress concentration factor below the threshold for initiating damage propagation, a damage-tolerance allowance that is consistent with the "no-growth" damage philosophy of the ABS Rules for composite pressure vessels):

(1) The laser-line profilometer, which projects a laser line onto the laminate surface immediately behind the compaction roller (at a distance of approximately 15 mm from the roller contact line, providing a near-real-time measurement of the surface profile as the tow is placed) and captures the reflected line with a high-speed camera (2048 × 256 pixels, 500 frames per second, the high frame rate being necessary to capture the surface profile at the 0.4 m/s tow speed without motion blur — at 500 FPS, each frame covers a 0.8 mm advance of the surface, and the profilometer's resolution of 0.01 mm in the height direction is sufficient to detect tow gaps (a negative height deviation relative to the nominal surface, indicating that the tow did not fill the intended width), tow overlaps (a positive height deviation indicating that the tow width exceeded the nominal, or that the placement position was offset toward the adjacent tow), and tow wrinkles (short-wavelength height variations indicative of tow buckling, with a wrinkle amplitude as small as 0.05 mm being detectable by the profilometer's height resolution, allowing the system to flag a wrinkle defect long before it becomes visible to the human eye — which is the traditional quality-inspection method in hand layup, and which is replaced by the automated profilometer in the AFP process, a transition from subjective, operator-dependent inspection to objective, data-driven quality assurance that is one of the primary justifications for the AFP investment, because the automated inspection reduces the defect escape rate from approximately 1 in 500 m² of laminate (for hand layup with visual inspection) to approximately 1 in 50,000 m² (for AFP with laser profilometry), a factor-of-100 improvement in quality that directly translates into the structural reliability of the pressure hull — the probability of a critical defect (one that would cause the hull to fail at a pressure below the design buckling pressure) in a single pod is reduced from approximately 0.10 (hand layup) to approximately 0.001 (AFP), a 100-fold improvement that, when combined with the proof-test pressure-cycling protocol of PART-055, reduces the overall failure probability to approximately 10⁻⁶ per pod per pressure cycle, meeting the target reliability requirement for a manned subsea habitat.

(2) The infrared thermography camera, which images the laminate surface in the 3 to 5 μm mid-wave infrared band at a frame rate of 100 FPS, capturing the thermal history of every point on the hull surface as it passes through the laser-illuminated zone and cools to the ambient temperature — a thermal history that is recorded and compared to the nominal "thermal fingerprint" of a defect-free tow placement, which consists of a rapid temperature rise (the laser heating, lasting approximately 0.06 seconds), a peak temperature of 180 ± 10°C (the polymerization temperature), and an exponential decay (the cooling phase, with a time constant of approximately 0.5 to 1.0 seconds, determined by the thermal diffusivity of the laminate α = k/(ρ·c_p) ≈ 0.45/(1,850 × 1,200) ≈ 2.03 × 10⁻⁷ m²/s, and the cooling of a 0.25 mm thick layer on a 40 mm thick substrate — a semi-infinite solid cooling problem, for which the surface temperature decay is T(t) = T_ambient + (T_peak - T_ambient)·erf(h/(2√(αt))) where h is the effective depth of the heated layer, approximately equal to the tow thickness of 0.25 mm when the laser penetration depth is similar to the tow thickness, and the time for the surface temperature to drop to within 10°C of the ambient temperature is approximately t_cool = h²/(4α)·(erf⁻¹(0.9))² ≈ (0.25 × 10⁻³)²/(4 × 2.03 × 10⁻⁷) × (1.16)² ≈ 0.077 × 1.35 ≈ 0.104 seconds — a rapid cooling that confirms the Elium resin's suitability for AFP, as the polymer solidifies within approximately 0.1 seconds of leaving the laser zone, locking in the fiber orientation and the compaction quality before the AFP head's vibration or the mandrel rotation can disturb the tow position). Deviations from the nominal thermal fingerprint — such as a lower peak temperature (indicating under-heating and incomplete polymerization, which will reduce the interlaminar strength), a higher peak temperature (indicating over-heating and possible thermal degradation, which will embrittle the matrix), or a slower cooling rate (indicating a thicker-than-nominal tow, or the presence of a gap that is filled with resin rather than fiber, which reduces the local thermal diffusivity because the resin's thermal conductivity (approximately 0.2 W/(m·K)) is lower than the composite's (approximately 0.45 W/(m·K)), so a resin-rich region cools more slowly and the IR camera detects the thermal anomaly) — are flagged by the automated defect-detection algorithm, which compares the measured thermal history to the expected history using a dynamic time-warping algorithm that aligns the measured and expected temperature-versus-time curves and computes a dissimilarity score (the cumulative difference in the aligned curves, normalized by the standard deviation of the expected curve over the defect-free training dataset, which consisted of 500 tow placements on flat calibration coupons, all confirmed defect-free by subsequent ultrasonic C-scan inspection), with a threshold score (2.5 standard deviations) triggering a defect flag and the recording of the defect location (latitude, longitude, and ply number) in the pod's digital twin — a comprehensive data record of the fabrication process for each pod, stored in the Aegis C database and accessible by the structural health monitoring system described in PART-051 for correlation with the in-service acoustic emission and fiber Bragg grating data, enabling the operators to distinguish between fabrication defects (present from day zero) and service-induced damage (developing over time), a diagnostic capability that is essential for the damage-tolerance management of the hull over its 50-year design life.

(3) The ultrasonic phased-array inspection system, which is not truly "in-situ" — it operates after each ply is completed, rather than during the tow placement — but which provides the definitive volumetric inspection of the laminate for porosity, delamination, and foreign-object inclusions, using a 64-element linear phased-array probe operating at 5 MHz (a frequency selected to balance the spatial resolution — the wavelength at 5 MHz in the basalt-Elium composite is approximately λ = c/f ≈ 3,000/5×10⁶ ≈ 0.6 mm, providing a resolution of approximately 0.3 mm (half the wavelength) for the detection of individual voids and delaminations, sufficient to detect the 1 to 2 mm diameter voids that are the smallest defects of concern for the pressure hull integrity, because a void smaller than approximately 2 mm has a stress concentration factor — approximately 2 to 3 for a spherical void in a biaxial compressive stress field, based on the Eshelby inclusion theory — that is insufficient to initiate matrix cracking at the laminate's design stress level of 12 to 15 MPa (the equatorial membrane stress), requiring a defect of at least 3 to 5 mm to achieve a stress concentration factor of 3 to 5 and to exceed the matrix's fatigue endurance limit under cyclic loading, a damage-tolerance threshold that guides the inspection acceptance criteria: any defect larger than 3 mm in any dimension is cause for rejection and repair of the affected ply, a specification that the 5 MHz phased-array system can enforce with a probability of detection of approximately 0.95 for 3 mm voids and 0.99 for 5 mm voids, based on the system's signal-to-noise ratio of approximately 18 dB for a 3 mm flat-bottom hole calibration reflector embedded in a 40 mm thick LBFRP test block, a value that exceeds the ASME Section V requirement of 12 dB minimum signal-to-noise ratio for ultrasonic examination of composite pressure vessels, confirming the adequacy of the inspection system for the Atlantis hull certification. The phased-array probe is mounted on a motorized scanner that traverses the hull surface in a raster pattern — meridional passes at 2 mm increments in the circumferential direction, covering the full surface in approximately 3 hours for a 78 m² hull, with the scan data streamed to a workstation running the OmniScan X3 analysis software, which performs real-time C-scan imaging of the ply stack and automatically detects and sizes any indications exceeding the 3 mm threshold, flagging them for the operator's review and generating a defect map that guides the repair process if any indications are confirmed. The combination of the laser profilometer (surface profile and tow placement accuracy), the IR thermography (consolidation quality and thermal history), and the ultrasonic phased-array (volumetric integrity and porosity content) provides a multi-layered quality assurance system that effectively guarantees the structural integrity of every Atlantis hull at the time of fabrication, with the remaining risk — the risk that a defect escapes all three inspection modalities and subsequently grows under the service loading to a critical size — being managed by the structural health monitoring system (PART-051) and the proof-test protocol (PART-055), together reducing the failure probability to the 10⁻⁶ target required for manned subsea habitability.
