## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The 2-millimeter MXene (Ti₃C₂T_x) coating that envelops the entire exterior surface of the Atlantis-class pressure hull is not merely a protective paint in the conventional marine engineering sense — it is a multifunctional electrochemical and barrier system that exploits the unique two-dimensional carbide structure of the MXene family of materials to simultaneously provide active cathodic protection (the coating acts as a sacrificial anode with a corrosion potential of -0.6 V versus Ag/AgCl, approximately 200 mV more negative than the open-circuit potential of the basalt fiber reinforcement — approximately -0.4 V versus Ag/AgCl in aerated seawater at pH 8.2 — ensuring that the MXene, not the structural composite, is the anode in any galvanic couple that forms if the coating is breached and seawater contacts the underlying laminate, and the MXene's dissolution current is sustained for the 50-year design life by the coating's thickness and by the self-healing mechanism of the MXene's surface terminations, which re-form spontaneously when the coating is scratched or abraded, re-establishing the protective barrier within hours of the damage event), passive chloride-ion barrier protection (the two-dimensional Ti₃C₂ nanosheets, with a thickness of approximately 1 to 2 nanometers and lateral dimensions of 2 to 10 micrometers — an aspect ratio of 1,000:1 to 10,000:1 — overlap and interleave in the electrophoretically deposited coating to form a tortuous diffusion path that increases the effective chloride-ion diffusion length through the coating by a factor of approximately 500 to 2,000 compared to the coating thickness, extending the breakthrough time — the time for chloride ions to penetrate the coating and reach the underlying laminate — to approximately 65 to 95 years at the 10°C ambient seawater temperature and the 0.55 MPa hydrostatic pressure of the deployment depth, well beyond the 50-year design life and providing a comfortable safety margin against the chloride-induced degradation of the basalt fiber-matrix interface that is the primary long-term durability threat to the LBFRP laminate), and electromagnetic interference (EMI) shielding (the MXene coating's high electrical conductivity — approximately 8,000 to 12,000 S/cm for the electrophoretically deposited film, a value that is competitive with copper (58,000 S/cm) but achieved at a density of approximately 3.5 g/cm³ compared to copper's 8.96 g/cm³, providing a specific conductivity approximately 1.8 times that of copper and enabling the 2-millimeter coating to achieve a shielding effectiveness of 45 dB across the 1 to 18 GHz frequency range — sufficient to protect the internal S-Bus and FEATHER mesh electronics from the electromagnetic pulse associated with the crustal displacement event, which the geological analysis (PART-05 of the Crustal Displacement Analysis) estimates could generate electromagnetic fields of 10 to 50 kV/m across the 1 MHz to 10 GHz spectrum due to the piezoelectric effect in the quartz-rich crustal rocks undergoing rapid stress release during the lithospheric decoupling — a threat that the MXene coating mitigates by reflecting approximately 90 percent of the incident electromagnetic power at the coating surface (the reflection loss, SER = 10 log₁₀(σ/(16ωε₀μ_r)) ≈ 10 log₁₀(10,000/(16 × 2π × 10⁹ × 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 1)) ≈ 35 dB for a 1 GHz signal) and absorbing the remaining 10 percent within the coating thickness (the absorption loss, SEA = 20 log₁₀(e)·t/δ, where δ = √(2/(ωμ₀σ)) ≈ √(2/(2π × 10⁹ × 4π × 10⁻⁷ × 10,000)) ≈ 5.0 × 10⁻⁶ m = 5 μm is the skin depth, and for a 2 mm thickness, SEA ≈ 20 × 0.434 × 2,000/5 ≈ 3,474 dB — a staggering number that reflects the exponential decay of the electromagnetic field within a conductor, and that means the field at the inner surface of the coating is attenuated by a factor of e^(-2,000/5) ≈ e^(-400) ≈ 10⁻¹⁷⁴, essentially zero to any physically measurable precision, such that the EMI shielding is effectively perfect for frequencies above approximately 10 MHz, where the skin depth is less than 0.5 mm and at least four skin depths are contained within the 2 mm coating thickness, providing the 40 to 50 dB of shielding that the S-Bus electronics require for survival). This part documents the complete MXene coating engineering from nanoparticle synthesis through electrophoretic deposition process optimization, the chloride diffusion and barrier-breakthrough modeling that validates the 50-year service life, the galvanic protection mechanism and its long-term sustainability, the adhesion promotion treatment that bonds the MXene coating to the LBFRP laminate surface, and the quality assurance testing — including the 5,000-hour accelerated salt-spray corrosion test (ASTM B117 modified for the deep-sea temperature and pressure conditions using a pressurized autoclave capable of 6 MPa and temperature control from 2 to 30°C, with the test duration of 5,000 hours at 30°C and 0.55 MPa being equivalent to approximately 58 years at 10°C and 0.45 MPa based on the Arrhenius acceleration model for chloride diffusion with an activation energy of 38 kJ/mol, the acceleration factor being AF = exp[(E_a/R)·(1/T_service - 1/T_test)] = exp[(38,000/8.314)·(1/283 - 1/303)] = exp(4.57 × 0.00024) = exp(0.0011) ≈ 1.001 for the temperature effect alone — the primary acceleration is achieved through the increased chloride concentration gradient, with the test using a 5 weight percent NaCl solution (approximately 1.4 times the seawater salinity of 3.5 percent) to accelerate the diffusion, giving a concentration-gradient acceleration factor of approximately 1.4, and the combined acceleration factor of approximately 1.4, corresponding to an equivalent service life of approximately 7,000 hours, or approximately 0.8 years — far less than the 50-year requirement, indicating that the salt-spray test, even with the temperature and concentration accelerations, cannot fully validate the 50-year diffusion barrier in a practical test duration, a limitation that is addressed through the physics-based diffusion modeling described in Section 4, which provides the analytical validation that complements and extends the empirical testing) — that collectively establish the MXene coating as the primary long-term corrosion protection system for the Atlantis hull.

## 1. MXENE SYNTHESIS AND Ti₃C₂T_x NANOSHEET CHARACTERIZATION

The MXene Ti₃C₂T_x nanosheets that constitute the anti-corrosion coating are synthesized from the MAX-phase precursor Ti₃AlC₂ — a ternary carbide with the layered hexagonal structure characteristic of the MAX phases, in which layers of edge-sharing Ti₆C octahedra (the "M" and "X" components) are interleaved with planes of aluminum atoms (the "A" component), the Al layers being selectively etchable because the metallic Al-Al bonds are weaker than the covalent Ti-C bonds, allowing the aluminum to be removed by a fluoride-containing etchant without destroying the Ti₃C₂ layers that remain as two-dimensional nanosheets after the etching and the subsequent delamination, a top-down synthesis approach that is analogous to the production of graphene by the exfoliation of graphite — a chemical exfoliation rather than a mechanical one, with the fluoride ions intercalating between the Ti₃C₂ layers and reacting with the aluminum to form soluble AlF₄⁻ complexes, which are washed away during the purification steps, leaving behind the Ti₃C₂T_x nanosheets where T_x represents the surface terminations — a mixture of -F, -OH, and =O functional groups that are left on the nanosheet surfaces as a byproduct of the fluoride-based etching chemistry and that are essential to the coating's anti-corrosion performance because the -OH and =O terminations provide the reactive sites for the covalent bonding to the Elium matrix surface and for the electrophoretic deposition process, while the -F terminations enhance the nanosheets' hydrophobicity — the contact angle of water on a Ti₃C₂F₂ surface is approximately 108°, compared to approximately 45° for the hydroxyl-terminated Ti₃C₂(OH)₂ surface, and the mixed termination of the as-synthesized MXene provides a water contact angle of approximately 85° to 95°, which is slightly hydrophobic and which reduces the water absorption rate at the coating-seawater interface, slowing the moisture diffusion through the coating by an additional 10 to 15 percent beyond the tortuosity effect of the nanosheet morphology — a secondary benefit of the surface terminations that, while not the primary barrier mechanism, contributes to the coating's overall effectiveness and is included in the diffusion model described in Section 4.

The MAX-phase precursor Ti₃AlC₂ is synthesized by the pressureless sintering of a stoichiometric mixture of titanium (Ti, -325 mesh, 99.5 percent purity), aluminum (Al, -325 mesh, 99.8 percent purity), and titanium carbide (TiC, 2 to 5 μm particle size, 99 percent purity) powders, with the molar ratio Ti:Al:TiC = 2:1.1:1 — the slight excess of aluminum (1.1 instead of 1.0) compensates for the aluminum loss by evaporation during the high-temperature sintering, which is conducted at 1,450°C for 2 hours under flowing argon (99.999 percent purity, flow rate 200 sccm) in a graphite-element resistance furnace, with the temperature ramp rate controlled at 5°C/min to allow the aluminum to melt (T_m = 660°C) and wet the Ti and TiC particles before the solid-state reaction between Ti and TiC to form the Ti₃AlC₂ phase initiates at approximately 1,200°C, a reaction sequence that produces a sintered body with a density of approximately 97 percent of the theoretical density (4.25 g/cm³) and a phase purity of approximately 92 percent Ti₃AlC₂, with the remaining 8 percent consisting primarily of TiC and Al₂O₃ impurity phases that are removed during the subsequent etching step by the selective dissolution of the Ti₃AlC₂, which leaves the TiC and Al₂O₃ particles as solid residues that are separated from the colloidal MXene suspension by centrifugation (4,500 rpm for 20 minutes, after which the supernatant contains the dispersed MXene nanosheets and the sediment contains the unreacted precursor and impurity particles, which are discarded). The sintered Ti₃AlC₂ block is then crushed and sieved to a particle size of 25 to 45 μm — a coarse powder that provides a large surface area for the etching reaction while avoiding the excessively fine particles (< 10 μm) that would be completely consumed during the etching, leaving no unreacted MAX-phase core to support the exfoliated nanosheets and resulting in the fragmentation of the MXene into small, low-aspect-ratio flakes that are less effective as diffusion barriers than the large, high-aspect-ratio nanosheets produced from coarser precursor particles.

The selective etching of aluminum from the Ti₃AlC₂ is performed using a solution of lithium fluoride (LiF, 99.5 percent purity) in hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37 weight percent, ACS reagent grade), with the LiF concentration of 5 M (approximately 130 g of LiF per liter of 9 M HCl) — a formulation that generates hydrofluoric acid (HF) in situ through the reaction LiF + HCl → LiCl + HF, with the equilibrium concentration of HF in a 9 M HCl solution saturated with LiF being approximately 3 to 4 M, a concentration that is sufficient to rapidly etch the aluminum from the MAX phase while being less hazardous to handle than the concentrated HF (48 to 51 percent, approximately 28 M) that was used in the original MXene synthesis by Naguib et al. in 2011, the in-situ HF generation providing a safer synthesis route that has become the standard for MXene production and that the Carrington Storm Motors Nanomaterials Laboratory has scaled to the 5-kilogram-per-batch level — sufficient to produce the approximately 60 to 80 kilograms of MXene required for the ten-pod fleet, at a yield of approximately 40 to 50 percent (the mass of MXene produced divided by the mass of the MAX-phase precursor, the loss being due to the removal of the aluminum — which accounts for approximately 25 percent of the MAX-phase mass — and the incomplete conversion of the precursor to MXene), requiring approximately 150 to 200 kilograms of Ti₃AlC₂ precursor, which the Mojave nanomaterial synthesis facility can produce in twelve 20-kilogram batches over approximately 4 weeks, at a synthesis cost of approximately $420 per kilogram of MXene, for a total MXene material cost of approximately $28,000 to $34,000 per pod — comparable to the nanodiamond material cost and, at approximately 0.15 percent of the total pod fabrication cost ($2.24 million), a negligible increment that the MXene coating's multi-functionality (corrosion protection, EMI shielding, and biofouling resistance) amply justifies.

The etched MXene suspension is washed by repeated centrifugation and redispersion cycles — typically six to eight cycles, with each cycle involving centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 10 minutes (sufficient to sediment the multilayer MXene particles, which have lateral dimensions of 2 to 10 μm and thicknesses of 100 to 500 nm — the multilayer particles consisting of 50 to 250 stacked Ti₃C₂ layers that have not yet been exfoliated into individual nanosheets), decanting the acidic supernatant (which contains the dissolved aluminum fluoride complexes and the excess LiCl and HCl, and which is neutralized with calcium hydroxide before disposal to precipitate the fluoride as insoluble CaF₂), and redispersing the sediment in deionized water by shaking for 5 minutes on a vortex mixer, a process that gradually increases the pH of the suspension from the initial value of approximately 0 (the 9 M HCl provides a pH near 0) to a final value of approximately 5 to 6 after six to eight wash cycles, at which point the suspension is stable — the MXene particles remain dispersed and do not sediment over a period of days, because the surface terminations (primarily -OH and -F) impart a negative zeta potential of approximately -30 to -40 mV at pH 6, providing sufficient electrostatic repulsion between the negatively charged nanosheets to prevent agglomeration, a stability that is essential for the electrophoretic deposition process, which requires a stable, well-dispersed colloidal suspension to produce a uniform, dense coating.

The final exfoliation of the multilayer MXene particles into individual Ti₃C₂T_x nanosheets is achieved by sonication — the washed suspension is sonicated in an ice-water-cooled bath using a 20 kHz ultrasonic probe at a power of 250 W for 1 hour, with the ultrasound generating cavitation bubbles that, upon collapse, produce intense local shear forces sufficient to overcome the van der Waals forces that hold the Ti₃C₂ layers together in the multilayer particles and to delaminate them into individual nanosheets with thicknesses of 1 to 2 nanometers — corresponding to single or bilayer Ti₃C₂, given that the thickness of a single Ti₃C₂ layer (three atomic layers: Ti-C-Ti-C-Ti) is approximately 0.98 nm, and the additional 0.5 to 1.0 nm accounts for the surface terminations and a monolayer of intercalated water molecules that hydrate the interlayer space, increasing the effective thickness of the nanosheet in solution. The sonicated suspension is centrifuged at 3,500 rpm for 30 minutes to remove the unexfoliated multilayer particles (which sediment at this lower g-force, while the individual nanosheets remain in suspension due to their lower mass and higher surface charge-to-mass ratio), and the supernatant — a dark green to black colloidal suspension (the color depending on the nanosheet concentration and the sonication time, with longer sonication producing a darker suspension as the nanosheets are further fragmented and the average lateral size decreases from approximately 5 μm to approximately 2 μm — a trade-off between the barrier performance, which benefits from larger lateral dimensions (higher aspect ratio, more tortuous diffusion path), and the coating uniformity, which benefits from smaller nanosheets (better packing density, fewer voids), with the sonication time of 1 hour being the empirically determined optimum that balances these competing effects and produces an average lateral size of approximately 3.5 μm, an aspect ratio of approximately 2,000:1 (3.5 μm lateral size divided by 1.7 nm thickness), and a nanosheet concentration of approximately 5 mg/mL — is used directly as the electrophoretic deposition bath.

## 2. ELECTROPHORETIC DEPOSITION PROCESS PHYSICS AND OPTIMIZATION

The electrophoretic deposition (EPD) of the MXene coating onto the Atlantis hull surface exploits the negative surface charge of the Ti₃C₂T_x nanosheets — the zeta potential of approximately -35 mV at the deposition bath pH of 6 — to drive the nanosheets toward a positively biased electrode (the Atlantis hull, which serves as the deposition cathode in the EPD circuit) under an applied electric field, with the deposition rate being governed by the Hamaker equation for the mass of particles deposited per unit area: m = (ε₀ε_rζ/η)·E·C·t, where ε₀ is the permittivity of free space (8.85 × 10⁻¹² F/m), ε_r is the relative permittivity of the suspension medium (water, ε_r ≈ 80 at the deposition temperature of 25°C), ζ is the zeta potential (approximately -0.035 V), η is the viscosity of the suspension medium (approximately 0.89 mPa·s for water at 25°C), E is the applied electric field (V/m), C is the mass concentration of the MXene nanosheets in the suspension (5 kg/m³, equivalent to 5 mg/mL), t is the deposition time, and the term (ε₀ε_rζ/η) is the electrophoretic mobility μ_e, which for the MXene suspension is μ_e ≈ (8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 80 × 0.035)/(0.00089) ≈ 2.79 × 10⁻⁸ m²/(V·s), a value that is consistent with the literature values for MXene EPD (typically 10⁻⁸ to 10⁻⁷ m²/(V·s) depending on the nanosheet size and the surface termination chemistry) and that implies, for a target coating thickness of 2 mm, a density of the deposited MXene film of approximately 1,800 kg/m³ (the density of the MXene film after drying and annealing, which is approximately 50 percent of the theoretical density of Ti₃C₂ (approximately 4.25 g/cm³ × 0.85 phase purity ≈ 3.6 g/cm³) due to the porosity that remains after the evaporation of the intercalated water and the incomplete packing of the nanosheets — a porosity of approximately 50 percent that is deliberate, because the porous structure provides compliance for the thermal expansion mismatch between the MXene coating (coefficient of thermal expansion CTE ≈ 6 to 8 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹, typical of ceramic materials) and the LBFRP laminate (CTE ≈ 12 to 15 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹ in the fiber direction and 25 to 35 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹ in the transverse direction for the basalt-Elium composite, the anisotropy reflecting the much higher CTE of the polymer matrix compared to the basalt fiber), a mismatch that, for a fully dense, rigid ceramic coating on a polymer-matrix composite substrate, would generate thermal stresses of approximately σ_thermal = E_coating·Δα·ΔT/(1 - ν_coating) ≈ 150 × 10⁹ × (12 - 7) × 10⁻⁶ × 15/(1 - 0.2) ≈ 14 MPa for a 15°C temperature change (the maximum seasonal temperature variation at the 45-meter Monterey depth being approximately 12 to 15°C from the winter minimum of 8°C to the summer maximum of 20°C), which would exceed the coating's tensile strength (approximately 5 to 8 MPa for a porous MXene film) and cause cracking and spallation — but that the deliberate 50 percent porosity reduces by lowering the effective modulus of the porous coating by a factor of approximately (1 - porosity)² ≈ 0.25 according to the Gibson-Ashby model for the elastic modulus of porous ceramics, reducing the thermal stress to approximately 3.5 MPa and thereby accommodating the thermal expansion mismatch without cracking, a critical design consideration that the EPD process's deposition parameters directly control, because the porosity of the deposited film is determined by the electric field strength: higher fields produce denser films (the electrophoretic force compacts the nanosheets more tightly against the electrode surface), and lower fields produce more porous films, with the optimum field strength of E = 5 V/cm (applied across the 2-meter gap between the hull surface — the cathode — and the counter-electrode, which is a cylindrical stainless-steel mesh surrounding the hull at a standoff distance of 2 meters, producing a uniform electric field over the hull surface despite the curvature of the prolate spheroid geometry, the field uniformity being achieved by shaping the counter-electrode mesh to a slightly larger prolate spheroid of 3.3:1 aspect ratio, such that the distance from the hull to the counter-electrode is approximately constant at 2 meters over the entire surface, minimizing the field strength variation to less than ±8 percent — a variation that the EPD process tolerates, as the coating thickness uniformity of ±0.15 mm over the hull surface meets the specification and is achieved without the need for the conformal anode shaping that would be required for a more field-sensitive deposition process) producing a film density of 1,800 ± 150 kg/m³ and a porosity of 48 ± 5 percent, with the total deposition time for a 2 mm coating being t = m/(μ_e·E·C) = (1,800 × 0.002)/(2.79 × 10⁻⁸ × 500 × 5) = 3.6/(6.98 × 10⁻⁵) ≈ 51,600 seconds ≈ 14.3 hours — a process duration that is compatible with the 18-day AFP fabrication timeline, as the EPD is performed after the AFP layup is complete and the laminate has been trimmed and surface-prepared by the precision routing of the hatch and penetration openings, and the 14-hour EPD process can be run overnight, with the following annealing step (350°C for 2 hours under argon, to remove the residual water and to partially sinter the MXene nanosheets at their contact points, increasing the coating's mechanical integrity and electrical conductivity — the annealing temperature of 350°C is deliberately below the onset temperature of significant thermal degradation of the Elium matrix (approximately 380°C, as determined by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) at a heating rate of 10°C/min in nitrogen, showing the onset of mass loss at 375 to 385°C corresponding to the depolymerization of the acrylic), and the 350°C annealing is performed with the laminate protected by the Invar mandrel, which acts as a heat sink and limits the temperature of the laminate's inner surface to approximately 120 to 140°C — below the T_g of 122°C, ensuring that the matrix does not soften or lose its structural integrity during the annealing, and the thermal gradient through the 40 mm laminate thickness — approximately (350 - 120)/0.040 ≈ 5,750°C/m — is well within the allowable range, producing thermal stresses of less than 5 MPa, which the laminate can accommodate without damage) being performed in a separate furnace after the hull is removed from the EPD bath and dried at 80°C for 24 hours to remove the bulk water before the high-temperature annealing, a two-step post-deposition treatment that completes the MXene coating fabrication.

## 3. ADHESION PROMOTION: THE MXENE-TO-LBFRP INTERFACE

The adhesion of the MXene coating to the LBFRP laminate surface is the single most critical interface in the Atlantis hull's corrosion protection system, because any debonding of the coating — even over a small area — exposes the underlying laminate to seawater, creating a local corrosion cell where the basalt fiber (acting as a cathode, with an open-circuit potential of approximately -0.4 V vs Ag/AgCl) and the MXene coating (acting as an anode, at -0.6 V vs Ag/AgCl) form a galvanic couple that accelerates the MXene dissolution at the perimeter of the debonded region, consuming the coating locally and allowing the chloride ions to reach the fiber-matrix interface, where they hydrolyze the silane coupling agent and degrade the interfacial shear strength — the very mechanism that the coating is designed to prevent, now concentrated at the debonded region and proceeding at an accelerated rate because the galvanic current is focused on the relatively small anode area (the perimeter of the debonding) while the large cathode area (the exposed basalt fiber) supplies the cathodic reaction (oxygen reduction: O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻, which raises the local pH at the cathode surface, further accelerating the alkaline hydrolysis of the silane coupling agent — a synergistic degradation mechanism that combines the electrochemical and chemical attack on the interface, and that is prevented by maintaining the coating adhesion at a level sufficient to resist the mechanical and chemical driving forces for debonding over the 50-year service life).

The adhesion promotion treatment consists of three steps: (1) mechanical abrasion — the laminate surface is abraded with 220-grit silicon carbide sandpaper using a random orbital sander at 10,000 rpm, removing the resin-rich surface layer (the "peel ply" texture that forms on the outer surface of the AFP laminate, where the compaction roller's silicone rubber surface leaves a slight texture of approximately 5 to 10 μm Ra roughness, a texture that is beneficial for mechanical interlocking but that does not provide the chemical bonding sites for the covalent attachment of the MXene nanosheets) and exposing the underlying basalt fibers in a fraction of the surface area — approximately 20 to 30 percent of the surface being fiber-exposed after the abrasion, with the remaining 70 to 80 percent being the matrix-rich regions between the fibers, a surface composition that balances the mechanical interlocking benefits (the exposed fibers provide microscopic anchor points for the MXene coating, mechanically locking the coating to the substrate) with the chemical bonding benefits (the matrix-rich regions provide the polymer surface functional groups — primarily the ester carbonyl groups of the acrylic polymer, C=O, and the residual hydroxyl groups from the polymerization initiator — that will react with the silane coupling agent applied in the next step); (2) silane coupling agent application — the abraded surface is treated with a 2 weight percent solution of (3-aminopropyl)triethoxysilane (APTES) in a 95:5 ethanol:water mixture (the water being necessary to hydrolyze the ethoxy groups of the APTES, converting them to the reactive silanol groups, Si-OH, that will condense with the hydroxyl groups on the MXene nanosheet surfaces), applied by wiping the surface with a lint-free cloth saturated with the APTES solution, followed by drying at 80°C for 1 hour to complete the silanol condensation reaction between the APTES molecules and the Elium matrix surface — the amine group (-NH₂) of the APTES being oriented outward from the laminate surface after the condensation, providing the amine functionality that will react with the MXene surface terminations (-OH and -F groups are both capable of hydrogen bonding with the amine, and the -OH groups can also form covalent Si-O-Ti bonds through a condensation reaction analogous to the silane-to-glass bonding mechanism that has been extensively characterized in the fiber-reinforced composites literature, with the silane coupling agent acting as a molecular bridge between the organic polymer matrix and the inorganic MXene filler, exactly as it acts between the polymer matrix and the inorganic basalt fiber in the laminate itself — a symmetry of the interfacial chemistry that simplifies the coating adhesion development, because the same APTES chemistry that bonds the basalt fiber to the Elium matrix in the laminate also bonds the MXene coating to the Elium matrix surface, providing a chemical continuity that eliminates the need for a separate, specialized adhesion promoter for the coating and that has been validated by the lap-shear adhesion tests described below); (3) the EPD process itself, during which the MXene nanosheets are driven into intimate contact with the silane-treated laminate surface by the electrophoretic force, and the subsequent annealing at 350°C completes the condensation reactions between the APTES amine groups and the MXene surface hydroxyls, forming a covalently bonded interface that achieves a lap-shear adhesion strength of 8.5 ± 1.2 MPa (measured by the single-lap shear test (ASTM D3165) on MXene-coated LBFRP coupons bonded to an uncoated LBFRP adherend with a structural epoxy adhesive, the failure occurring cohesively within the MXene coating — not adhesively at the coating-laminate interface — for approximately 85 percent of the tested specimens, indicating that the interface strength exceeds the coating's cohesive strength and that the adhesion is not the limiting factor for the coating's durability), a value that exceeds the 5 MPa minimum adhesion strength specified by the ABS Rules for protective coatings on composite pressure vessels and that provides the safety margin against debonding over the 50-year service life, with the remaining risk being the slow, moisture-assisted degradation of the silane bonds at the coating-laminate interface — a degradation mechanism that the MXene coating itself mitigates by preventing the moisture from reaching the interface, creating a self-reinforcing protection system in which the coating's barrier property preserves the adhesion that keeps the coating in place, a virtuous cycle that the long-term durability analysis of Section 4 validates.

## 4. CHLORIDE DIFFUSION MODELING AND 50-YEAR BREAKTHROUGH PREDICTION

The chloride-ion transport through the MXene coating is modeled as a one-dimensional Fickian diffusion process through a heterogeneous medium consisting of impermeable, overlapping, high-aspect-ratio platelets (the Ti₃C₂T_x nanosheets) embedded in a permeable matrix (the water-filled porosity of the coating, which occupies approximately 48 percent of the coating volume and which provides the continuous pathways for chloride-ion diffusion), with the effective diffusion coefficient D_eff being related to the diffusion coefficient in the bulk water-filled porosity D_0 (the diffusion coefficient of chloride ions in dilute aqueous solution at 10°C, D_0 ≈ 1.2 × 10⁻⁹ m²/s, extrapolated from the measured value of 2.0 × 10⁻⁹ m²/s at 25°C using the Stokes-Einstein relationship D ∝ T/η, with the viscosity of water increasing from 0.89 mPa·s at 25°C to 1.31 mPa·s at 10°C, giving D_0(10°C) = 2.0 × 10⁻⁹ × (283/298) × (0.89/1.31) ≈ 1.29 × 10⁻⁹ m²/s) by the tortuosity factor τ and the constrictivity factor δ — the "tortuosity-constrictivity model" of diffusion in porous media, which is the standard approach for modeling barrier coatings in the corrosion engineering literature and which yields D_eff = D_0·(φ/τ)·δ, where φ = 0.48 is the porosity, τ is the tortuosity (the ratio of the actual diffusion path length through the porous medium to the straight-line distance through the medium, a quantity that for a medium containing impermeable, overlapping platelets is approximated by the Nielsen model for barrier composites: τ = 1 + (L/(2W))·φ_platelet, where L is the platelet lateral dimension (3.5 μm), W is the platelet thickness (1.7 nm), and φ_platelet = (1 - φ) = 0.52 is the volume fraction of the platelets in the coating — the model assumes that the platelets are perfectly aligned parallel to the coating surface, an idealization that the EPD process approximates because the electric field orients the nanosheets with their basal planes parallel to the electrode surface (the hull surface), as the electrophoretic torque on a high-aspect-ratio particle in an electric field aligns the particle's long axis with the field lines — which are perpendicular to the electrode surface — such that the nanosheets are deposited with their basal planes approximately parallel to the hull surface, the optimal orientation for barrier performance because the diffusion path is forced to follow a tortuous route around the edges of the impermeable platelets, maximizing the path length for a given platelet loading, and the Nielsen model gives τ = 1 + (3.5 × 10⁻⁶/(2 × 1.7 × 10⁻⁹)) × 0.52 = 1 + (1,029) × 0.52 ≈ 536, and δ is the constrictivity factor (accounting for the narrowing of the diffusion pathway at the platelet edges, which further reduces the effective diffusion coefficient, typically in the range 0.5 to 0.8 for platelet-reinforced barriers, and assumed to be 0.65 for the MXene coating based on the geometric analysis of the nanosheet overlap and the resulting pore-throat dimensions), giving D_eff = 1.29 × 10⁻⁹ × (0.48/536) × 0.65 ≈ 7.5 × 10⁻¹³ m²/s — a reduction of the chloride diffusion coefficient by a factor of approximately 1,720 relative to the bulk water diffusion coefficient, which is the quantitative expression of the barrier enhancement provided by the MXene nanosheets and which translates directly into the breakthrough time — the time required for the chloride concentration at the coating-laminate interface to reach a threshold value (assumed to be 10 percent of the external seawater chloride concentration, approximately 0.035 × 0.10 = 0.0035 weight fraction, or approximately 0.1 M Cl⁻, a concentration at which the chloride-induced degradation of the silane coupling agent begins to accelerate, as determined by the single-fiber pull-out tests on basalt-Elium specimens aged in chloride solutions of varying concentration, which showed that the interfacial shear strength degradation rate increases approximately linearly with the chloride concentration above a threshold of approximately 0.05 M) — as a function of the coating thickness L = 2 mm, using the solution of Fick's second law for a semi-infinite medium with a constant surface concentration C_s = C_seawater (0.035 weight fraction, approximately 1.0 M Cl⁻) and an initial concentration of zero throughout the coating: C(L, t) = C_s · erfc(L/(2√(D_eff·t))), and solving for t when C(L, t) = 0.1·C_s: erfc(L/(2√(D_eff·t))) = 0.1, so L/(2√(D_eff·t)) = erfc⁻¹(0.1) ≈ 1.16, and t = L²/(4·D_eff·(1.16)²) = (0.002)²/(4 × 7.5 × 10⁻¹³ × 1.346) = 4 × 10⁻⁶/(4.04 × 10⁻¹²) ≈ 9.90 × 10⁵ seconds ≈ 11.5 days — which seems alarming, implying that the chloride breaks through the coating in less than two weeks, but this calculation uses the steady-state effective diffusion coefficient, which applies only after the coating has reached moisture saturation — a transient condition that, based on the moisture diffusion model of PART-043 (Section 5), requires approximately 85 years for the full 40 mm laminate thickness, but for the 2 mm coating thickness, the time to reach 95 percent moisture saturation is approximately t_sat ≈ 0.5·L²/D_eff ≈ 0.5 × 4 × 10⁻⁶/(7.5 × 10⁻¹³) ≈ 2.67 × 10⁶ seconds ≈ 31 days, and the transient chloride concentration at the interface during the saturation period is much lower than the steady-state concentration — a more accurate breakthrough time estimate accounts for the transient moisture uptake using the full solution of the coupled moisture-diffusion and chloride-diffusion problem, which for the MXene coating with its low effective diffusivity yields a breakthrough time (for the 0.1 M Cl⁻ threshold at the interface) of approximately 52 to 68 years, with the range reflecting the uncertainty in the nanosheet alignment (perfect alignment gives the upper bound, and a 5° to 10° angular deviation — which the EPD process's alignment fidelity of approximately ±8° produces — gives the lower bound). The 50-year design life is therefore within the predicted breakthrough time range, but the margin is not large — approximately 2 to 18 years, depending on the alignment — and this margin motivates the additional protective measures that supplement the MXene coating: the hydrophobic surface treatment of the Elium resin (PART-043, Section 1), which reduces the moisture absorption rate and thereby extends the breakthrough time by an additional 10 to 15 percent (an additional 5 to 10 years), and the sacrificial anode function of the MXene coating itself, which provides active protection even after chloride breakthrough, as described in Section 5.

The 5,000-hour accelerated salt-spray testing, while insufficient to directly validate the 50-year breakthrough prediction as discussed in the Executive Summary, provides a critical empirical calibration point for the diffusion model: the coating specimens subjected to the accelerated test (30°C, 5 weight percent NaCl, 0.55 MPa hydrostatic pressure, simulating the worst-case crustal displacement event conditions) showed no measurable chloride penetration beyond the outer 0.3 to 0.5 millimeters of the 2 mm coating after 5,000 hours, as determined by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) line scans across the coating cross-section, which showed chlorine concentration dropping to below the detection limit (approximately 0.1 weight percent) at a depth of 0.4 ± 0.1 mm from the exposed surface, consistent with the diffusion model's prediction of a 0.35 mm penetration depth for the accelerated test conditions (D_eff(30°C) = D_eff(10°C) × (303/283) × (η_10°C/η_30°C) = 7.5 × 10⁻¹³ × 1.071 × (1.31/0.80) ≈ 1.31 × 10⁻¹² m²/s, and L_penetration = 2√(D_eff·t)·erfc⁻¹(C_detect/C_s) = 2√(1.31 × 10⁻¹² × 1.8 × 10⁷)·erfc⁻¹(0.001/0.035) ≈ 2√(2.36 × 10⁻⁵)·2.33 ≈ 2 × 0.00486 × 2.33 ≈ 0.382 mm), the agreement between the model and the experiment within 5 percent providing a strong validation of the Nielsen tortuosity model for the MXene coating and supporting the extrapolation to the 50-year service life prediction.

## 5. GALVANIC PROTECTION AND SELF-HEALING MECHANISM

The MXene coating's role as a sacrificial anode in the Atlantis hull's corrosion protection system is activated only if the coating's barrier function is locally compromised — by a scratch, an impact that fractures the coating, or the long-term chloride breakthrough that eventually reaches the laminate interface — at which point the electrochemical potential difference between the MXene (E_corr ≈ -0.6 V vs Ag/AgCl, as measured by the potentiodynamic polarization scan at a scan rate of 0.167 mV/s in aerated artificial seawater at 10°C, with the polarization curve showing active dissolution behavior — the anodic current increasing exponentially with the applied potential, with a Tafel slope of approximately 80 mV/decade, indicating that the MXene dissolution is activation-controlled (the rate-determining step is the charge transfer at the MXene-solution interface, specifically the oxidation of the Ti atoms from Ti²⁺ to Ti⁴⁺, which releases the surface terminations and exposes fresh Ti₃C₂ layers to the solution, continuing the dissolution process layer-by-layer — a dissolution mechanism that is analogous to the dealloying of the aluminum from the MAX phase precursor but occurring at a much slower rate because the Ti-C bonds are stronger than the Al-Al bonds and because the two-dimensional MXene structure presents fewer reactive edge sites per unit mass than the three-dimensional MAX phase particles, a characteristic that gives the MXene coating a low, steady dissolution rate that sustains the cathodic protection for the 50-year design life) and the basalt fiber reinforcement in the underlying laminate (E_corr ≈ -0.4 V vs Ag/AgCl, as measured by the open-circuit potential of a bare basalt fiber exposed to aerated seawater, with the fiber acting as a cathode at which the dissolved oxygen in the seawater is reduced, consuming the electrons that the MXene dissolution releases — the fundamental galvanic cell that drives the sacrificial anode protection, with the anode (MXene) dissolving to protect the cathode (basalt fiber) from corrosion, the same principle that governs the use of zinc anodes on steel ship hulls, but adapted to the composite material system where the "corrosion" of the basalt fiber is not a metal dissolution but the hydrolytic degradation of the silane coupling agent that bonds the fiber to the matrix, a degradation that the cathodic protection prevents not by supplying electrons to the fiber (which is an electrical insulator — the basalt fiber's electrical resistivity is approximately 10¹² Ω·m, making it an insulator, and the cathodic protection mechanism for a composite differs fundamentally from that for a metal: the protection is achieved by the alkaline pH generated at the cathode surface by the oxygen reduction reaction, which produces OH⁻ ions and raises the local pH from the seawater's approximately 8.2 to approximately 10.5 to 11.5, a pH range in which the siloxane bonds of the silane coupling agent are thermodynamically stable — the equilibrium constant for the hydrolysis reaction Si-O-Si + H₂O ⇌ 2 Si-OH is strongly shifted toward the Si-O-Si (polymerized) form at pH > 9, as the high OH⁻ concentration drives the reverse reaction, re-polymerizing any silanol groups that have been hydrolyzed by the water and thereby "healing" the interfacial degradation, a mechanism that is unique to the silane-based interface and that makes the cathodic protection of composite materials fundamentally feasible despite the insulating nature of the fiber reinforcement, a finding that is one of the novel contributions of the Atlantis coating research program to the broader field of composite marine structures).

The self-healing mechanism of the MXene coating — the ability to re-form the protective barrier after mechanical damage, without external intervention — arises from the mobile nature of the Ti₃C₂T_x nanosheets in the presence of water: when a scratch penetrates the coating and exposes the underlying laminate, the water that enters the scratch hydrates the MXene nanosheets at the scratch walls, and the electrostatic repulsion between the negatively charged nanosheets (the zeta potential remains negative at the seawater pH of 8.2, approximately -45 to -50 mV, even stronger than at the deposition pH of 6 because the increased OH⁻ concentration deprotonates additional surface hydroxyl groups, increasing the negative charge density) causes them to repel each other and to migrate into the scratch, gradually filling the scratch with loosely packed MXene nanosheets that, over a period of approximately 24 to 48 hours (the migration time being determined by the electrophoretic mobility — the nanosheets move at a velocity of approximately v = μ_e·E, where E is the local electric field generated by the galvanic couple between the exposed basalt fiber (cathode) and the MXene coating surrounding the scratch (anode), with the field strength at the scratch perimeter being approximately 50 to 100 V/m (based on a typical galvanic potential difference of 0.2 V across a 2 to 4 mm scratch width), giving v ≈ 2.79 × 10⁻⁸ × 75 ≈ 2.1 × 10⁻⁶ m/s, or approximately 0.18 meters per day, such that a scratch of 0.5 mm width is filled within approximately 0.0005/0.18 ≈ 2.8 × 10⁻³ days ≈ 4 minutes — the nanosheet migration is not the rate-limiting step; rather, the rate is limited by the chemical re-establishment of the covalent bonds between the newly deposited MXene nanosheets and the laminate surface at the scratch bottom, a process that requires the hydrolysis and re-condensation of the APTES silane molecules, which occurs over a time scale of hours to days at the ambient seawater temperature and pH, with the self-healing being approximately 80 percent complete after 24 hours and essentially 100 percent after 72 hours, as verified by the scratch-healing tests in which a 1 mm wide scratch was deliberately introduced into the MXene coating of a test coupon, the coupon was immersed in artificial seawater at 10°C, and the chloride permeation rate through the scratched region was monitored by the EDS line-scan technique, showing that the permeation rate returned to within 10 percent of the intact-coating baseline after 72 hours of immersion, confirming the self-healing effectiveness and providing the operational protocol for responding to coating damage: the pod's structural health monitoring system (PART-051) detects a coating breach (through the increased acoustic emission activity associated with the MXene dissolution — the hydrogen bubbles generated by the cathodic reaction at the exposed basalt fiber surface create characteristic acoustic emission signals in the 100 to 300 kHz frequency range, distinguishable from the structural acoustic emissions of the laminate, and the SHM system's pattern-recognition algorithm identifies the coating-breach signature and alerts the occupants, who then monitor the self-healing progress and, if the permeation rate does not return to baseline within 72 hours — indicating a scratch too wide for the self-healing mechanism to fill, or a coating detachment rather than a scratch — initiate a manual repair using the on-board MXene patch kit, which consists of a pre-mixed MXene suspension in a squeeze tube, analogous to an epoxy syringe, that is applied to the damaged area by a diver or an ROV and that cures in situ at the ambient seawater temperature within 24 hours, restoring the coating's barrier function without requiring the pod to be retrieved to the surface — a repair capability that is essential for the 50-year service life, as occasional coating damage from marine-life interactions (shark bites, whale rubs, or the attachment of barnacles and other fouling organisms — the MXene coating's slightly hydrophobic surface, with its 85° to 95° water contact angle, provides moderate biofouling resistance, but it is not a biocide, and the accumulation of hard fouling (barnacles, tubeworms, oysters) is expected to occur at a rate of approximately 2 to 5 percent surface coverage per year at the Monterey site based on the fouling rate data from the Monterey Bay Aquarium Research Institute's long-term mooring deployments, such that after 10 years, approximately 30 to 50 percent of the hull surface will be covered with fouling organisms, and the periodic removal of this fouling by an ROV-mounted rotary brush — a maintenance operation that is conducted annually and that is specified in the Atlantis operational procedures — will occasionally damage the MXene coating, necessitating the self-healing and manual repair capabilities) is inevitable over a 50-year deployment.

The complete MXene anti-corrosion coating system, with its 2-millimeter-thick Ti₃C₂T_x layer applied by electrophoretic deposition onto a silane-primed LBFRP laminate surface and annealed at 350°C under argon, provides a chloride-ion diffusion barrier with an effective diffusion coefficient of approximately 7.5 × 10⁻¹³ m²/s — a factor of 1,720 reduction relative to bulk water — sufficient to delay the chloride breakthrough at the laminate interface to approximately 52 to 68 years, exceeding the 50-year design life with a modest but adequate margin, and supplemented by the galvanic cathodic protection that activates in the event of a local coating breach, raising the interfacial pH to the range where the silane coupling agent is thermodynamically stable and the interfacial degradation is arrested, and the self-healing mechanism that re-establishes the barrier within 72 hours of mechanical damage, together providing a multi-layered corrosion protection system that is integral to the Atlantis hull's 50-year durability and that has been validated through the accelerated salt-spray testing, the scratch-healing immersion tests, and the physics-based diffusion modeling, establishing the MXene coating as the primary corrosion protection strategy for the Atlantis-class subsea habitation module.
