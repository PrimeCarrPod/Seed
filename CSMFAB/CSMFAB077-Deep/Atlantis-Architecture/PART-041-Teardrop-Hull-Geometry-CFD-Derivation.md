## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The teardrop hull geometry of the Atlantis-class subsea habitation module is not an aesthetic choice nor a loose biomimetic gesture toward the streamlined forms of marine organisms — it is a rigorously derived hydrodynamic optimization that emerges directly from the governing partial differential equations of fluid mechanics as applied to a pressure vessel that must simultaneously minimize drag during turbidite flow passage, maximize internal habitable volume for a given structural mass, and maintain laminar boundary layer attachment over the widest possible range of Reynolds numbers characteristic of crustal displacement recession flows. This part documents the complete computational fluid dynamics derivation of the 3:1 prolate spheroid geometry, beginning with the fundamental Navier-Stokes equations in their incompressible form for seawater at 10°C and 35 PSU salinity (density ρ = 1,027 kg/m³, dynamic viscosity μ = 1.39 × 10⁻³ Pa·s, kinematic viscosity ν = 1.35 × 10⁻⁶ m²/s), proceeding through the Reynolds-averaged formulation with the shear stress transport (SST) k-ω turbulence closure model selected after a systematic comparison of seven turbulence models against experimental data from the David Taylor Model Basin tow-tank tests of prolate spheroid bodies at length-based Reynolds numbers ranging from 10⁵ to 10⁸, and culminating in the precise specification of the hull's three-dimensional surface coordinates as a parametric equation set suitable for direct input to the automated fiber placement (AFP) toolpath generation software that controls the Mojave fabrication line. The analysis demonstrates that the 3:1 aspect ratio represents the intersection of three competing optimization curves: the internal volume-to-surface-area ratio (which favors spherical geometry, maximizing volume per unit structural mass), the drag coefficient versus Reynolds number curve (which favors increasingly elongated geometries as the Reynolds number increases, reflecting the transition from pressure-dominated to skin-friction-dominated drag regimes), and the structural buckling resistance under external hydrostatic pressure (which favors moderate aspect ratios that avoid the long-column buckling modes of highly elongated cylinders while still providing sufficient streamlining to reduce the form drag component below the threshold where anchoring system loads become economically prohibitive). The resulting geometry achieves a drag coefficient of C_D = 0.04 at the design Reynolds number of Re_L = 5 × 10⁷, corresponding to a flow velocity of 20 m/s past a hull of characteristic length L = 12 meters, a value that has been independently validated through three separate computational methodologies — Reynolds-Averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) with the SST k-ω model in OpenFOAM v2212 on a 42-million-cell unstructured hex-dominant mesh with prism layers resolving the boundary layer to y⁺ < 1 at the wall, detached eddy simulation (DES) with the Spalart-Allmaras one-equation model in ANSYS Fluent 2023 R2 on a 28-million-cell hybrid mesh with adaptive mesh refinement triggered by the Q-criterion vortex identification parameter exceeding a threshold of 10⁵ s⁻², and large eddy simulation (LES) with the wall-adapting local eddy-viscosity (WALE) subgrid-scale model in the spectral-element code Nek5000 at polynomial order N = 9 on a mesh of 150,000 spectral elements, yielding drag coefficient predictions of C_D = 0.038, 0.041, and 0.039 respectively — and through 1:25 scale physical model testing in the Carrington Storm Motors tow tank at Mojave, where a 0.48-meter-long fiberglass model instrumented with a six-axis force balance measured a drag coefficient of 0.043 ± 0.003 across five repeat tests at Re_L = 2.7 × 10⁶, applying the ITTC 1957 model-ship correlation line for skin friction correction to extrapolate the measured model-scale drag to the full-scale Reynolds number, yielding a predicted full-scale C_D of 0.040 that matches the computational prediction to within the experimental uncertainty.

## 1. GOVERNING EQUATIONS AND TURBULENCE MODELING FRAMEWORK

The hydrodynamic analysis of the Atlantis teardrop hull begins with the conservation of mass and momentum for an incompressible Newtonian fluid — seawater at the design conditions of 10°C, 35 PSU salinity, and hydrostatic pressure of 5.5 bar absolute (corresponding to the 45-meter deployment depth at Monterey plus one standard atmosphere at the sea surface) — expressed through the continuity equation ∂u_i/∂x_i = 0, which enforces divergence-free flow under the assumption that density variations due to temperature and salinity gradients are negligible over the spatial scale of the hull boundary layer (the Boussinesq approximation is valid because the temperature difference between the ambient seawater and the hull surface, which is actively cooled to ambient temperature by the thermal management system described in PART-072, is maintained at less than 0.5°C, and the salinity difference due to the forward osmosis desalination brine discharge described in PART-066 is diluted to less than 1 PSU above ambient within 2 meters of the discharge port by the ambient current, eliminating any significant buoyancy-driven flow effects on the hull's hydrodynamic performance), and the Navier-Stokes momentum equation ρ(∂u_i/∂t + u_j ∂u_i/∂x_j) = -∂p/∂x_i + μ∂²u_i/(∂x_j∂x_j) + ρg_i, where u_i represents the three components of the velocity vector in the Cartesian coordinate system aligned with the hull's principal axes (x aligned with the major axis pointing into the flow, y aligned horizontally perpendicular to the major axis, and z aligned vertically upward, with the gravitational body force term ρg_i acting only in the negative z-direction), p is the static pressure, μ is the dynamic viscosity, and the summation convention over repeated indices is implied throughout. The flow regime of interest — turbidite recession flows at velocities of 15 to 30 m/s past a hull of characteristic length L = 12 meters, corresponding to a Reynolds number Re_L = ρUL/μ = (1,027 × 20 × 12)/(1.39 × 10⁻³) ≈ 1.77 × 10⁸, which is two orders of magnitude beyond the critical Reynolds number for boundary layer transition on a smooth streamlined body placed in low-turbulence free-stream flow — is fully turbulent over essentially the entire hull surface except for a small laminar region near the stagnation point at the leading edge, which the CFD analysis estimates to extend approximately 0.15 to 0.3 meters from the leading-edge stagnation point, corresponding to a momentum-thickness Reynolds number Re_θ of approximately 300 to 400, well within the range where natural transition via Tollmien-Schlichting wave amplification is expected for a free-stream turbulence intensity of approximately 5 percent (the estimated turbulence intensity of turbidite flows based on acoustic Doppler current profiler measurements of natural sediment-laden density currents in the Monterey Canyon, which show turbulence intensities of 3 to 8 percent in the flow core and 8 to 15 percent in the near-bed region where sediment entrainment generates additional turbulent kinetic energy through vortex shedding from bedforms and through the density stratification effects that suppress vertical turbulent exchange while enhancing horizontal turbulent intensity through shear production at the interface between the sediment-laden lower layer and the clearer water above).

The Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) formulation is employed as the primary computational methodology for the parametric geometry optimization because it provides an acceptable balance between computational cost and predictive accuracy for the integral quantities of interest — drag coefficient, pressure distribution, and boundary layer separation location — while the higher-fidelity DES and LES methodologies are reserved for validation of the final optimized geometry and for investigation of unsteady flow phenomena such as vortex shedding from the hull's trailing edge, which although not a primary design driver for the Atlantis hull (the teardrop form is specifically designed to suppress organized vortex shedding by providing a gradual pressure recovery that avoids the adverse pressure gradient magnitudes that trigger boundary layer separation on bluffer body forms), must be characterized to ensure that vortex-induced vibration (VIV) does not excite structural resonances of the hull or of the inter-pod tunnel connections during the sustained flow conditions of the recession phase. The RANS equations are derived by applying the Reynolds decomposition u_i = U_i + u'_i, where U_i = ⟨u_i⟩ is the ensemble-averaged velocity and u'_i is the fluctuating component with zero mean, substituting into the Navier-Stokes equations, and ensemble-averaging to obtain ∂U_i/∂x_i = 0 and ρ(∂U_i/∂t + U_j ∂U_i/∂x_j) = -∂P/∂x_i + ∂/∂x_j(μ∂U_i/∂x_j - ρ⟨u'_i u'_j⟩), where the term -ρ⟨u'_i u'_j⟩ is the Reynolds stress tensor, an apparent stress arising from the momentum transport by turbulent fluctuations that must be modeled through a turbulence closure — the choice of which constitutes the single most consequential modeling decision in the entire CFD derivation, because different closure models predict significantly different separation behavior for streamlined bodies at high Reynolds numbers, and the prediction of whether the boundary layer remains attached over the aft 30 percent of the hull, where the pressure gradient becomes adverse as the flow decelerates toward the trailing stagnation point, determines whether the drag coefficient is dominated by skin friction (attached flow, C_D ≈ 0.04) or by pressure drag (separated flow, C_D ≈ 0.12 to 0.25), a factor of 3 to 6 difference that would fundamentally alter the anchoring system requirements and potentially render the entire Atlantis architecture nonviable if the higher drag values proved correct.

Seven turbulence models were evaluated in a systematic comparison against the benchmark experimental data for a 3:1 prolate spheroid at 0° angle of attack compiled by the NATO Research and Technology Organization's AVT-183 Working Group on Reliable Prediction of Separated Flow Onset, which conducted coordinated experiments at six wind tunnel and water tunnel facilities worldwide using a common 1.2-meter-long aluminum model with a surface finish of 0.8 μm Ra, pressure taps at 216 locations distributed over 12 meridional rays, and oil-film flow visualization for separation detection, with the experimental conditions spanning Reynolds numbers from 1.2 × 10⁶ to 8.4 × 10⁶ and free-stream turbulence intensities from 0.1 percent (low-turbulence wind tunnel with screens and a 16:1 contraction ratio) to 7.5 percent (water tunnel with a turbulence-generating grid of 6 mm bar diameter and 30 mm mesh spacing). The seven models evaluated were: (1) the one-equation Spalart-Allmaras model, which solves a single transport equation for the modified eddy viscosity ν̃ and which was originally developed for aerospace applications — particularly for attached and mildly separated boundary layers on wings and fuselages — and which performs well for streamlined bodies at low to moderate angles of attack but tends to overpredict the eddy viscosity in regions of strong adverse pressure gradient, leading to delayed separation prediction, a known deficiency that is particularly problematic for the aft portion of a prolate spheroid where the adverse pressure gradient magnitude is sufficient to cause separation in the experimental data but is often predicted as attached by the Spalart-Allmaras model unless a rotation/curvature correction term is added, which doubles the number of calibration constants and introduces its own uncertainties; (2) the standard k-ε model with wall functions, the workhorse of industrial CFD for three decades, which solves transport equations for the turbulent kinetic energy k and its dissipation rate ε, and which is known to be unreliable for flows with adverse pressure gradients because the ε equation's source term coefficients were calibrated for equilibrium turbulent boundary layers and grid-generated turbulence, not for the non-equilibrium turbulence that develops in decelerating boundary layers approaching separation, a deficiency that the Atlantis CFD team confirmed by comparing the model's separation prediction for the 6:1 prolate spheroid at 10° angle of attack against the AVT-183 data, finding that the standard k-ε model predicted separation at x/L = 0.92 compared to the experimentally observed separation at x/L = 0.78 — an error that would translate to a 35 to 40 percent underprediction of the pressure drag component if applied to the Atlantis hull optimization, an error magnitude that is unacceptable for the anchoring system design margins; (3) the realizable k-ε model, which modifies the ε equation coefficient C_μ from a constant to a function of the mean strain and rotation rates, satisfying the realizability constraints that the normal Reynolds stresses must remain positive and that the Schwarz inequality for shear stresses must be satisfied — constraints that the standard k-ε model violates in regions of large strain rate, leading to unphysical negative normal stresses in stagnation regions — and which improves the separation prediction modestly, predicting separation at x/L = 0.84 for the 10° angle-of-attack case, still unacceptable for the Atlantis application; (4) the standard k-ω model, which replaces the ε equation with an equation for the specific dissipation rate ω = ε/k, and which has the significant advantage that it can be integrated through the viscous sublayer to the wall without requiring wall functions (provided the near-wall mesh resolution is sufficient to achieve y⁺ < 1 at the wall-adjacent cell centers), eliminating the wall-function empiricism that introduces uncertainties of 5 to 15 percent in the skin friction prediction for flows with pressure gradients, but which has the well-known deficiency of extreme sensitivity to the free-stream value of ω — changing the free-stream ω by an order of magnitude can change the predicted separation location by 10 to 15 percent of the body length, an unacceptable sensitivity for a design optimization that must converge to a single geometry; (5) the baseline (BSL) k-ω model, a blending of the k-ω model near the wall (where its viscous sublayer integration is advantageous) and the k-ε model in the outer region (where its insensitivity to free-stream ω values is advantageous), using a blending function F_1 that transitions smoothly from 1 at the wall to 0 in the free stream, with F_1 constructed from the turbulent length scale and the wall distance such that the blending occurs in the logarithmic region of the boundary layer where both models produce similar eddy viscosity distributions when calibrated to the same flat-plate boundary layer data, eliminating the free-stream sensitivity problem while retaining the near-wall accuracy, and predicting separation at x/L = 0.81 for the AVT-183 benchmark, a moderate improvement over the standard k-ε but still insufficient for the required accuracy; (6) the shear stress transport (SST) k-ω model, a further refinement of the BSL model that adds a limiter to the eddy viscosity formulation in the form μ_t = ρk/ω · 1/max(1, SF_2/(a_1ω)), where S is the invariant measure of the strain rate tensor, F_2 is a second blending function that limits the eddy viscosity in adverse pressure gradient regions based on Bradshaw's assumption that the turbulent shear stress in a boundary layer is proportional to the turbulent kinetic energy with a proportionality constant a_1 = 0.31 derived from experimental data spanning a wide range of pressure gradient conditions, and the max function ensures that the eddy viscosity does not exceed the value that would produce shear stresses exceeding Bradshaw's limit, which is the key physical insight that enables the SST model to predict separation with significantly greater accuracy than the standard k-ω or BSL models because the overprediction of eddy viscosity in adverse pressure gradients — the mechanism that delays separation prediction in most two-equation models — is directly suppressed by the shear stress limiter; and (7) the Reynolds stress transport model (RSTM) with the SSG pressure-strain closure, the most physically complete model evaluated, which solves individual transport equations for all six independent components of the Reynolds stress tensor and an equation for the dissipation rate ε, requiring the solution of seven additional transport equations beyond the mean flow equations and thereby increasing the computational cost by approximately a factor of 4 to 5 compared to two-equation models, but capturing the anisotropy of the Reynolds stresses that is particularly important for flows with streamline curvature — such as the flow around the curved surface of a prolate spheroid, where the convex streamline curvature on the forward portion of the hull suppresses turbulent mixing through a centrifugal stabilization effect analogous to the Richardson number effect in stratified flows, reducing the turbulent shear stress below the level that an isotropic eddy viscosity model would predict based on the local strain rate alone, while the concave curvature on the aft portion enhances turbulent mixing through a centrifugal destabilization effect, increasing the turbulent shear stress and promoting earlier separation than an isotropic model would predict — an effect that the RSTM captures through the exact production term for each Reynolds stress component, P_ij = -⟨u'_i u'_k⟩∂U_j/∂x_k - ⟨u'_j u'_k⟩∂U_i/∂x_k, which automatically accounts for streamline curvature through the mean velocity gradient tensor without requiring ad-hoc curvature correction terms.

The systematic comparison against the AVT-183 data, conducted by running each turbulence model on identical computational meshes with identical boundary conditions and numerical schemes, yielded the following quantitative ranking based on the root-mean-square error of the pressure coefficient C_p distribution along the top meridian of the prolate spheroid at 0° angle of attack, integrated over the entire body length and weighted by the local surface area to emphasize the aft region where separation prediction accuracy is most critical: RSTM with SSG closure, RMS error = 0.018 (in C_p units, where C_p = (p - p_∞)/(½ρU²_∞) ranges from 1.0 at the forward stagnation point to approximately -0.15 in the region of maximum flow acceleration near x/L = 0.3, then rising to approximately 0.1 to 0.2 at the trailing edge depending on whether the boundary layer remains attached or separates), representing an error of approximately 1.6 percent of the maximum C_p variation over the body; SST k-ω, RMS error = 0.022; BSL k-ω, RMS error = 0.029; Spalart-Allmaras with rotation/curvature correction, RMS error = 0.031; standard k-ω, RMS error = 0.038; realizable k-ε, RMS error = 0.041; standard k-ε, RMS error = 0.056. The RSTM's superior performance is attributable primarily to its accurate prediction of the Reynolds stress anisotropy in the aft region, where the experimental data show that the streamwise normal stress ⟨u'²⟩ exceeds the cross-stream normal stresses ⟨v'²⟩ and ⟨w'²⟩ by a factor of approximately 2 to 3, an anisotropy that the isotropic eddy viscosity models cannot reproduce because they assume ⟨u'²⟩ = ⟨v'²⟩ = ⟨w'²⟩ = (2/3)k — the isotropic turbulence assumption that is explicitly violated in decelerating boundary layers, where the streamwise fluctuations are amplified by the adverse pressure gradient through a mechanism analogous to the stretching of vortex lines in a diverging flow, while the cross-stream fluctuations are damped by the continuity constraint that couples them to the streamwise divergence. However, the RSTM's computational cost — approximately 5,200 core-hours per geometry evaluation on 256 cores of the Mojave CFD cluster (dual-socket AMD EPYC 7763, 64 cores per node, 256 GB DDR4-3200 per node, InfiniBand HDR100 interconnect) compared to approximately 1,100 core-hours for the SST k-ω model — renders it impractical for the parametric optimization phase, which requires the evaluation of approximately 200 to 300 distinct geometries (varying aspect ratio, nose radius, tail cone angle, and surface curvature distribution) to identify the Pareto-optimal front of the drag-versus-volume trade-space. The SST k-ω model was therefore selected as the primary turbulence closure for the parametric optimization, with the RSTM reserved for final validation of the optimized geometry and for the off-design angle-of-attack cases (the hull at yaw angles of 0° to 30° relative to the turbidite flow direction, representing the range of possible flow approach angles given the uncertainty in the exact orientation of the turbidite flow path relative to the pod's installed orientation) that determine the lateral force and yawing moment transmitted to the anchoring system, quantities that are less sensitive to separation prediction accuracy than the drag coefficient but that must be characterized to ensure the anchor system can resist the maximum expected lateral load without exceeding the pullout capacity of the grouted helical pile foundations.

## 2. MESH GENERATION AND BOUNDARY CONDITION STRATEGY

The computational mesh for the parametric geometry optimization was generated using the snappyHexMesh utility in OpenFOAM v2212, a hex-dominant unstructured mesh generator that starts from a background hexahedral mesh and iteratively refines, snaps to the body surface, and adds boundary layer prism cells through a process controlled by a dictionary of refinement parameters that were tuned through a systematic mesh convergence study. The computational domain extends 15 body lengths upstream of the hull's leading edge, 25 body lengths downstream of the trailing edge, and 10 body lengths in the lateral and vertical directions — dimensions that were determined by placing a series of pressure probes at increasing distances from the hull and observing when the deviation of the local pressure coefficient from the free-stream value fell below 0.1 percent, indicating that the domain boundaries were sufficiently far from the hull that blockage effects (the acceleration of the flow around the body that occurs when the body occupies a non-negligible fraction of the computational domain's cross-sectional area, which artificially increases the predicted drag by increasing the velocity at the body's surface relative to the free-stream velocity) were negligible, with the final domain providing a blockage ratio — the ratio of the hull's frontal projected area to the domain's cross-sectional area — of 0.08 percent, well below the 2 percent threshold at which blockage corrections (typically based on the Maskell correction for bluff bodies or the Glauert correction for streamlined bodies) become necessary for engineering accuracy. The background mesh consists of a Cartesian grid with cell dimensions doubling at each successive refinement level away from the hull, with the coarsest level (level 0, cell size 1.28 meters) at the domain boundaries and the finest level (level 5, cell size 0.04 meters, or approximately 40 millimeters) in the refinement region surrounding the hull, extending 0.5 body lengths in all directions from the hull surface, a region within which the mesh is isotropic (cell aspect ratios less than 1.2) to minimize numerical diffusion of momentum, which in unstructured finite-volume solvers is proportional to the cell skewness and to the misalignment between the cell faces and the local flow direction, effects that are minimized when cells are approximately cubical and oriented with faces normal to the local coordinate axes.

The boundary layer mesh consists of 35 prism layers extruded from each triangular face on the hull surface, with the first layer thickness set to 8 × 10⁻⁶ meters (8 micrometers), which, for the design freestream velocity of 20 m/s and the boundary layer edge velocity of approximately 24 m/s at the location of maximum flow acceleration near x/L = 0.3 (where the local velocity reaches approximately 1.2 times the freestream velocity due to the displacement effect of the body, which accelerates the flow to satisfy continuity as the streamlines converge around the body's maximum cross-section), yields a wall-adjacent cell centroid y⁺ value of approximately 0.6 to 0.9 over the forward 80 percent of the hull, rising to approximately 1.2 to 1.8 in the aft 20 percent where the boundary layer thickens and the local skin friction decreases as the flow approaches separation, values that are within the y⁺ < 1 requirement for integration through the viscous sublayer without wall functions — a requirement of the SST k-ω model that, while computationally expensive (the 35 prism layers increase the total cell count by approximately 2.3 million cells compared to the 12-layer mesh that would be sufficient for wall-function-based models at y⁺ ≈ 30, representing a computational cost increase of approximately 35 percent), eliminates the need for the logarithmic law-of-the-wall empiricism that wall functions rely upon, an empiricism that is known to degrade in accuracy when the flow deviates from the equilibrium turbulent boundary layer assumptions — specifically, when the pressure gradient parameter β = (δ^*/τ_w)(dp/dx), where δ^* is the displacement thickness and τ_w is the wall shear stress, exceeds approximately 0.01 in magnitude, which it does over the aft 40 percent of the hull where the adverse pressure gradient parameter reaches values of 0.05 to 0.15, well into the regime where the logarithmic law is no longer valid and the velocity profile deviates systematically from the log-law form, an effect that the SST model's viscous sublayer integration captures automatically through the ω boundary condition at the wall, which is set to ω_wall = 60μ/(ρβ_1 y₁²) where y₁ is the distance from the wall to the first cell center and β_1 = 0.075 is a model constant, a boundary condition derived from the asymptotic behavior of ω in the viscous sublayer, ω → 6ν/(β_1 y²) as y → 0, ensuring the correct near-wall asymptotic behavior without requiring additional modeling assumptions. The prism layer growth ratio is set to 1.15, meaning that each successive prism layer is 15 percent thicker than the preceding one, which provides a smooth transition from the highly anisotropic prism cells near the wall (aspect ratios of 500:1 to 1,000:1 in the streamwise-to-wall-normal direction) to the isotropic hexahedral cells in the outer region, minimizing the numerical error associated with the interpolation of face fluxes at the prism-hex interface — an error that, if the cell size jump exceeds approximately 1.5, can introduce spurious numerical diffusion equivalent to adding an unphysical turbulent viscosity of approximately 0.1 to 0.3 times the molecular viscosity, which would contaminate the skin friction prediction in the very region where accuracy is most critical.

The boundary conditions applied to the computational domain are: at the inlet (the upstream face of the domain, normal to the x-direction), a uniform velocity profile U_x = U_∞, U_y = U_z = 0, with the turbulent kinetic energy k set to 1.5(I·U_∞)² where I = 0.05 (5 percent) is the estimated free-stream turbulence intensity of turbidite flows as discussed above, and the specific dissipation rate ω set to k/(ν_t) where the turbulent viscosity ratio ν_t/ν is set to 10, a value that produces a free-stream ω sufficiently small that the eddy viscosity in the free stream is approximately 10 times the molecular viscosity — large enough to prevent numerical instability in the k and ω equations during the iterative solution process (which can occur if ω is set too low, causing the turbulence equations to become numerically stiff because the production and dissipation terms have widely disparate magnitudes) but small enough that the free-stream eddy viscosity does not artificially enhance the turbulent diffusion of momentum in the far field, which would suppress the natural development of the wake and reduce the predicted pressure recovery in the aft region, thereby under-predicting the drag; at the outlet (the downstream face), a zero-gradient condition for all primitive variables and a fixed-value condition for the static pressure p = 0 gauge, which allows the flow to exit the domain without generating spurious reflections of pressure waves that would contaminate the near-field solution; at the lateral and top/bottom boundaries, a symmetry condition (zero normal gradient for all variables except the normal velocity, which is set to zero), which approximates the far-field condition of a body in an unbounded fluid more accurately than a slip-wall condition (which would impose a fictitious constraint on the tangential velocity) and which is appropriate because the domain boundaries are sufficiently far from the body that the flow at the boundaries is essentially parallel to the boundary surfaces, with the normal velocity component less than 0.1 percent of the freestream velocity; and at the hull surface, a no-slip condition with zero velocity for all components (U_i = 0), zero turbulent kinetic energy (k = 0, because the turbulent fluctuations vanish at a solid wall), and the ω boundary condition described above, with the pressure gradient at the wall derived from the momentum equation in the wall-normal direction, ∂p/∂n = 0, which is the standard boundary layer approximation that the pressure is constant across the boundary layer thickness — an approximation that is valid for boundary layers that are thin relative to the body's radius of curvature (which they are, with the boundary layer thickness at the trailing edge, δ_99 ≈ 0.08 meters, being less than 2 percent of the hull's maximum radius of 2 meters at the 3:1 aspect ratio and L = 12 meters, corresponding to a maximum diameter of 4 meters and a radius of 2 meters) — but that must be applied with caution in regions of strong convex curvature where the centripetal acceleration of the curved flow induces a pressure gradient across the boundary layer that can modify the separation behavior, an effect that the RSTM captures through the exact production terms but that the SST model approximates only through the eddy viscosity limiter, which is calibrated to reproduce the correct separation behavior for a range of pressure gradient conditions without explicitly modeling the curvature-induced pressure gradient across the boundary layer.

## 3. PARAMETRIC GEOMETRY DEFINITION AND OPTIMIZATION STRATEGY

The Atlantis teardrop hull geometry is parameterized as a prolate spheroid — a surface of revolution generated by rotating an ellipse about its major axis — modified by a set of four shape functions that provide the design freedom necessary to optimize the drag coefficient while maintaining the internal volume constraint of 45 cubic meters (corresponding to the requirement that the pod accommodate four persons at standard capacity plus life support equipment, with the volume allocation derived from the interior architecture analysis detailed in PART-052). The base prolate spheroid is defined by the parametric equation in cylindrical coordinates aligned with the body axis: r(x) = (D_max/2) · √(1 - (x/a)²), where x is the axial coordinate measured from the body's geometric center, with x = -a at the leading edge and x = +a at the trailing edge, D_max is the maximum diameter of the body (occurring at x = 0, the mid-body station), and a = L/2 is the semi-major axis length. The aspect ratio AR = L/D_max = 2a/D_max is the primary design variable, with the internal volume of the prolate spheroid given by V = (4/3)πa(D_max/2)² = (π/6)L D_max², which for a target volume of 45 m³ yields the relationship D_max = √(270/(π·L)), linking the maximum diameter to the body length for any given aspect ratio. The base prolate spheroid geometry is then modified by four shape functions:

(1) The nose profile function f_nose(x), defined for -a ≤ x ≤ 0, which modifies the elliptical profile to control the location and magnitude of the minimum pressure coefficient — the "suction peak" that occurs where the flow accelerates around the nose curvature and that determines the laminar-to-turbulent transition location (a sharper nose produces a stronger suction peak, which destabilizes the laminar boundary layer through the adverse pressure gradient that follows the suction peak, promoting earlier transition and thereby reducing the extent of the laminar boundary layer, which in turn increases skin friction — the laminar skin friction coefficient on a flat plate is C_f,lam = 0.664/√(Re_x) compared to C_f,turb = 0.027/Re_x^(1/7) for turbulent flow, meaning that for Re_x = 10⁷, the turbulent skin friction is approximately 3.5 times the laminar value, a difference that, integrated over the nose region where the laminar boundary layer persists if transition is delayed, can affect the total drag by 8 to 15 percent — but that also reduces the risk of laminar separation bubbles, which can form if the adverse pressure gradient following the suction peak is sufficiently strong to separate the laminar boundary layer before it transitions to turbulence, creating a localized region of recirculating flow that increases the pressure drag and, in severe cases, can fail to reattach, causing the entire aft boundary layer to be turbulent but with a thickened, distorted profile that separates prematurely at the trailing edge). The nose profile function is implemented as f_nose(x) = r(x) · [1 + A_nose · sin²(π(-x/a)^(p_nose))], where A_nose controls the deviation magnitude (with A_nose = 0 reproducing the base elliptical profile, A_nose > 0 producing a blunter nose with a larger radius of curvature at the stagnation point, which spreads the suction peak over a larger surface area and reduces its magnitude, stabilizing the laminar boundary layer and delaying transition, and A_nose < 0 producing a sharper nose with a smaller radius of curvature, which concentrates the suction peak and promotes earlier transition), and p_nose controls the axial extent of the modification, with p_nose = 1 confining the modification to the immediate nose region and larger values of p_nose (2 to 4) extending the modification further aft along the hull, allowing the designer to trade off between nose bluntness and overall body fineness.

(2) The mid-body profile function f_mid(x), defined for the full body length -a ≤ x ≤ a, which modifies the elliptical profile to control the body's fullness — the distribution of cross-sectional area as a function of axial position, which determines the pressure distribution over the central portion of the body and, through the pressure gradient that this distribution imposes on the boundary layer, the location at which the boundary layer begins to decelerate and eventually separates. The mid-body profile function is implemented as f_mid(x) = r(x) · [1 + A_mid · (1 - (x/a)²)^(p_mid)], where A_mid controls the magnitude of the mid-body fullness modification (A_mid > 0 produces a fuller mid-body with a larger maximum diameter for the same length, increasing the internal volume but also increasing the maximum cross-sectional area and thereby the form drag — the drag component associated with the pressure distribution integrated over the body surface, which for a streamlined body at zero angle of attack is dominated by the pressure recovery in the aft region, where the flow must decelerate from its maximum velocity at the mid-body to near-stagnation conditions at the trailing edge, and any failure of the pressure to recover fully to the trailing-edge stagnation pressure produces a net pressure force in the downstream direction, i.e., a pressure drag increment), and p_mid, typically set to 1.0 to 1.5, controls the sharpness of the transition between the modified mid-body profile and the nose and tail regions, with larger values producing a sharper transition that may generate localized adverse pressure gradients triggering premature separation.

(3) The tail cone angle function f_tail(x), defined for 0 ≤ x ≤ a, which modifies the elliptical profile in the aft region to control the pressure recovery gradient — the rate at which the static pressure increases from its minimum value near the mid-body to the trailing-edge stagnation pressure — and thereby the separation location, the most critical single parameter governing the pressure drag of the hull. The tail cone angle is defined as the included half-angle of the cone that is tangent to the body profile at the trailing edge, β_tail = tan⁻¹(dr/dx|_{x=a}), and is related to the pressure recovery gradient through the inviscid flow approximation: for a body of revolution in incompressible flow, the pressure coefficient distribution is related to the body radius distribution through the slender-body theory approximation C_p(x) = 1 - (1 + (r'(x))²), where r'(x) = dr/dx is the local slope of the body profile, a relationship that is exact only for inviscid, incompressible flow over a slender body (AR ≫ 1) but that provides a useful first-order estimate of the effect of tail cone angle on the pressure distribution. For the Atlantis hull, the tail cone angle is the primary shape parameter varied in the parametric optimization, with values ranging from 6° (a very gradual taper that produces a nearly cylindrical aft section, which maximizes internal volume but produces a weak pressure recovery, with the static pressure at the trailing edge recovering to only approximately 0.7 to 0.8 of the stagnation pressure, leaving a significant pressure drag component) to 18° (a steep taper that maximizes the pressure recovery — approaching the ideal value of 1.0, i.e., full stagnation pressure recovery at the trailing edge, which would produce zero pressure drag — but that reduces the internal volume in the aft region, potentially compromising the equipment layout that depends on the full volume envelope of the prolate spheroid, and that may produce an adverse pressure gradient sufficiently steep to cause boundary layer separation despite the streamlined form, negating the pressure recovery benefit). The tail cone angle that optimizes the balance between drag reduction and volume retention is the central output of the parametric optimization.

(4) The trailing edge closure function f_close(x), defined for 0.95a ≤ x ≤ a (the final 5 percent of the body length), which modifies the elliptical profile at the extreme aft end to control the trailing edge geometry — whether the body closes to a sharp point (the ideal streamline body that eliminates the low-pressure base region entirely, achieving zero pressure drag in inviscid theory but requiring infinite surface curvature at the trailing edge, which in a real viscous flow produces a region of extremely steep adverse pressure gradient that will separate the boundary layer, creating a finite-thickness wake with its own associated pressure drag), closes to a finite-radius rounded trailing edge (which avoids the infinite curvature singularity and produces a finite-thickness wake, with a base pressure that depends on the ratio of the trailing-edge radius to the boundary layer thickness at separation — a larger radius produces a larger, lower-pressure separated region and therefore higher base drag — and that can be optimized to balance skin friction reduction against pressure drag increase), or is truncated at a finite diameter to create a blunt base (which produces the highest base drag but also the simplest fabrication geometry and, importantly for the Atlantis application, provides a flat surface at the aft end suitable for mounting the inter-pod tunnel connection flange, the electrical feedthrough penetrator plate, and the auxiliary thruster mounting bracket — practical considerations that may override the purely hydrodynamic optimization). The trailing edge closure function is implemented as f_close(x) = r(x) · [1 - A_close · ((x - 0.95a)/(0.05a))^(p_close)], where A_close = 0 corresponds to the sharp trailing edge of the base ellipse, A_close = 1 corresponds to a flat-faced cylindrical truncation (a blunt trailing edge with a radius equal to the prolate spheroid radius at x = 0.95a, i.e., the radius at the station where the closure function begins, which for the 3:1 aspect ratio at L = 12 meters is r(0.95a) = r(5.7 m) = 2m × √(1 - 0.95²) = 2 × √(0.0975) ≈ 0.624 meters, producing a blunt base of 1.25 meters diameter), and intermediate values of A_close produce a rounded trailing edge with a finite radius that transitions smoothly from the tail cone angle to a near-flat base, controlling the trade-off between base drag (which increases approximately as the square of the base diameter for a given freestream velocity) and fabrication simplicity (which increases as the base geometry approaches a simple flat flange).

The parametric optimization proceeds through a two-stage process. Stage 1 is a design-of-experiments (DOE) exploration using a Latin hypercube sampling of the four shape function parameters (A_nose, p_nose, A_mid, β_tail, A_close, p_close — six parameters total, since p_mid is held constant at 1.0 based on preliminary sensitivity analysis showing less than 2 percent variation in drag for p_mid values between 0.8 and 1.5), with 128 sample points distributed across the six-dimensional parameter space and evaluated using the RANS-SST methodology on a medium-resolution mesh (22 million cells, y⁺ < 2, 25 prism layers) to identify the region of the parameter space containing the global optimum and to construct a response surface using a Gaussian process regression (kriging) model with a Matérn 5/2 covariance kernel — a kernel that provides the smoothness necessary for gradient-based optimization while still capturing the nonlinear interactions between parameters that characterize aerodynamic shape optimization problems, and that outperforms the more commonly used squared-exponential kernel for CFD-based surrogate modeling because the Matérn class provides finite differentiability (the 5/2 kernel is twice differentiable, matching the smoothness of the underlying CFD solution's dependence on the geometry parameters, which is typically C² continuous except at parameter values where topology changes occur, e.g., when a change in tail cone angle causes the separation point to jump from the trailing edge to a mid-body location, a discontinuity in the drag-versus-parameter function that the kriging model captures through the nugget term in the covariance, which models the noise variance associated with physical discontinuities that are not representable by a smooth function). Stage 2 is a constrained gradient-based optimization using the sequential quadratic programming (SQP) algorithm as implemented in the SNOPT library, with the objective function defined as the volumetric drag efficiency parameter η_V = V^(2/3) / (C_D · A_frontal), where V^(2/3) is the internal volume raised to the 2/3 power (proportional to the surface area of a sphere of equivalent volume, providing a length-scale normalization that makes η_V dimensionless and independent of the absolute size of the body, allowing geometries to be compared purely on the basis of their hydrodynamic efficiency independently of the absolute volume requirement), C_D is the drag coefficient based on the frontal projected area A_frontal = π(D_max/2)², and the constraint is that the internal volume V ≥ 45 m³ ± 1 m³ (the ±1 m³ tolerance accommodates the volume variations introduced by the shape function modifications without requiring the optimizer to hit the volume target exactly, which would produce an overly constrained optimization that might miss drag-reducing shape modifications that slightly reduce the volume but that could be compensated by a small increase in the body length, a parameter that is adjusted in an outer optimization loop that varies the length L between 10 and 14 meters to achieve the exact volume target for each candidate shape).

The optimization converged after 47 iterations of the SQP algorithm, requiring approximately 94,000 core-hours of computation on the Mojave CFD cluster over a period of 8 days (utilizing 512 cores, or 8 nodes, with each RANS evaluation on the high-resolution final mesh — 64 million cells, y⁺ < 1, 35 prism layers — requiring approximately 2,000 core-hours, a computational cost driven primarily by the need to resolve the boundary layer to y⁺ < 1 over the entire hull surface, which mandates the 35-layer prism mesh with its associated increase in cell count and in the stiffness of the discretized equations due to the high aspect ratio of the near-wall cells, which degrades the convergence rate of the algebraic multigrid preconditioner that OpenFOAM employs for the pressure equation, requiring approximately 35,000 to 45,000 iterations of the pressure-velocity coupling loop per simulation to reduce the residuals by six orders of magnitude from their initial values). The optimized geometry that emerged from this process has the following parameters: aspect ratio AR_opt = 3.15 (slightly higher than the nominal 3:1, the additional slenderness reducing the form drag by approximately 3 percent compared to the exact 3:1 geometry, at the cost of a 2 percent reduction in internal volume for the same maximum diameter, which is compensated by increasing the maximum diameter from 4.00 to 4.06 meters, maintaining the 45 m³ volume target); nose shape parameter A_nose = -0.12 (a moderately sharper nose than the base ellipse, which shifts the minimum pressure coefficient from x/L = 0.15 to x/L = 0.11 and increases its magnitude from C_p,min = -0.18 to -0.22, promoting boundary layer transition at approximately x/L = 0.08 to 0.12 — earlier than the natural transition location of x/L = 0.18 to 0.25 for the base ellipse, as estimated from the e^N transition prediction method with N = 9 based on the Tollmien-Schlichting envelope method — which increases the skin friction on the nose by approximately 12 percent (the penalty of earlier transition) but reduces the form drag by approximately 11 percent (the benefit of a more stable, less separation-prone turbulent boundary layer over the aft region), for a net drag reduction of approximately 2 percent compared to the base ellipse); mid-body fullness parameter A_mid = 0.08 (a slightly fuller mid-body than the base ellipse, which increases the maximum diameter by approximately 1.5 percent — from 4.00 to 4.06 meters — to maintain the internal volume after the sharper nose and steeper tail cone reduce the volume by approximately 3 and 4 percent respectively, the three shape modifications producing a net volume change of approximately zero); tail cone half-angle β_tail = 12.8° (significantly steeper than the base ellipse's effective tail cone angle of approximately 9°, producing a pressure recovery coefficient — the ratio of the trailing-edge pressure to the stagnation pressure — of 0.91, compared to 0.84 for the base ellipse, and reducing the pressure drag component by approximately 28 percent, which is partially offset by a 9 percent increase in the skin friction due to the higher near-wall velocities in the steeper pressure gradient, which increase the wall shear stress through the direct relationship τ_w = μ(∂u/∂y)|_wall and through the indirect effect of the pressure gradient on the boundary layer velocity profile shape, which becomes less full in an adverse pressure gradient, reducing the wall shear stress for a given boundary layer thickness but — counterintuitively — increasing it for a given displacement thickness because the less-full profile implies a larger velocity gradient at the wall for the same boundary layer thickness as the flow adjusts to conserve mass flux); and trailing edge closure parameter A_close = 0.35 (producing a rounded trailing edge with a base diameter of approximately 0.52 meters, representing a compromise between the sharp trailing edge ideal — A_close = 0, base diameter = 0, zero base drag in inviscid theory but separation in the real viscous flow producing an effective base diameter of approximately 0.3 to 0.4 meters due to the displacement effect of the separated wake, with an associated base drag coefficient of approximately 0.002 to 0.004 — and the fabrication requirement for a flat mounting surface of at least 0.5 meters diameter to accommodate the inter-pod tunnel flange standard derived from the Safehold STR-002 pressure vessel's 0.6-meter hatch diameter, the flange requiring a sealing surface of at least 0.7 meters outer diameter with a bolt circle of 0.55 meters diameter, thus demanding a minimum flat section of approximately 0.55 meters diameter, which A_close = 0.35 provides while maintaining a base drag coefficient of only 0.006, adding approximately 0.8 percent to the total drag compared to the ideal sharp trailing edge but enabling the practical integration of the tunnel connection that is essential to the multi-pod community architecture).

## 4. DRAG DECOMPOSITION AND REYNOLDS NUMBER SENSITIVITY

The drag of the optimized Atlantis teardrop hull at the design Reynolds number of Re_L = 5 × 10⁷ (U_∞ = 20 m/s) is decomposed into its physical components through the far-field wake integration method, which is superior to the near-field surface integration method for CFD-based drag prediction because the surface integration method — integrating the pressure and shear stress distributions over the body surface — is sensitive to numerical errors in the near-wall pressure and velocity gradient fields, which are amplified by the high aspect ratio of the near-wall cells, while the far-field method integrates the momentum deficit in a plane normal to the flow in the wake region, where the mesh is isotropic, the flow gradients are much smaller than at the wall, and the momentum deficit is directly related to the drag through the conservation of momentum applied to a control volume enclosing the body, D = ∬_wake ρU_∞(U_∞ - u) dA, where the integral is taken over a plane perpendicular to the freestream direction and located sufficiently far downstream — typically 2 to 5 body lengths — that the static pressure has recovered to within 0.1 percent of the freestream value, eliminating the pressure integral term that would otherwise complicate the drag extraction from the wake survey. The total drag coefficient decomposed into its physical components is: C_D,skin = 0.0283 (skin friction drag, 70.8 percent of total), C_D,form = 0.0091 (form/pressure drag, 22.8 percent), C_D,base = 0.0026 (base drag from the blunt trailing edge closure, 6.5 percent of total), with the sum C_D = 0.0400 matching the previously stated design value and representing the drag that the anchoring system must resist — a force of F_D = ½ρU_∞² A_frontal C_D = 0.5 × 1,027 × (20)² × (π × (2.03)²) × 0.040 = 0.5 × 1,027 × 400 × 12.94 × 0.040 = 106,200 N, or approximately 10.8 metric tons-force, at the design flow velocity of 20 m/s, rising to 239,000 N (24.4 metric tons-force) at the maximum design velocity of 30 m/s, values that are well within the capacity of the eight grouted helical pile anchors each rated at 50 kN in tension and 30 kN in lateral shear, providing a factor of safety of approximately 3.8 on the total drag load at the maximum design condition.

The Reynolds number sensitivity of the drag coefficient is a critical consideration because the turbidite flow velocity is uncertain — the 15 to 30 m/s range represents the 90th percentile confidence interval from the Monte Carlo simulation of recession phase hydrodynamics, not a deterministic prediction — and the drag coefficient varies with the Reynolds number in a manner that is non-monotonic for streamlined bodies, reflecting the competing effects of boundary layer transition movement, turbulent skin friction variation, and separation location shifts. A series of 15 RANS-SST simulations was conducted at Reynolds numbers spanning Re_L = 10⁶ to 10⁸ (corresponding to velocities of 0.4 to 40 m/s at the fixed body length of 12 meters, although in practice the velocity cannot exceed approximately 30 m/s based on the recession phase flow modeling) to characterize this sensitivity. The results show that C_D decreases from 0.052 at Re_L = 10⁶ (where the boundary layer is largely laminar over the forward 40 percent of the hull, producing a thick, separation-prone boundary layer that separates at approximately x/L = 0.72 — far forward on the aft body, producing a large separated wake region with high pressure drag — a phenomenon known as the "drag crisis" for streamlined bodies, analogous to the better-known drag crisis for spheres and cylinders at Re ≈ 3 × 10⁵, but occurring at a higher Reynolds number for streamlined bodies because the favorable pressure gradient over the forward portion suppresses Tollmien-Schlichting wave amplification, delaying transition to higher Reynolds numbers) to 0.044 at Re_L = 5 × 10⁶ (where transition has moved forward to approximately x/L = 0.15, the boundary layer is turbulent over most of the body, and the separation point has moved aft to x/L = 0.88 — approaching the trailing edge but not yet reaching it — producing a substantially lower form drag than at the lower Reynolds number, with the pressure drag coefficient dropping from 0.018 to 0.011), to a minimum of 0.038 at Re_L = 2 × 10⁷ (where transition occurs at approximately x/L = 0.05, essentially at the leading edge, maximizing the extent of the turbulent boundary layer and its associated resistance to separation — the turbulent boundary layer's fuller velocity profile, with a shape factor H = δ^*/θ ≈ 1.3 to 1.4 compared to H ≈ 2.6 for a laminar boundary layer, means that it can sustain a significantly stronger adverse pressure gradient before separating, and the transition from the favorable to the adverse pressure gradient occurs at x/L ≈ 0.3 for the Atlantis hull, such that a laminar boundary layer entering this adverse pressure gradient would separate almost immediately, at x/L ≈ 0.35 to 0.40, producing a massive separated region over 60 percent of the body length, while the turbulent boundary layer that has transitioned at x/L ≈ 0.05 is well-established and robust, separating at x/L ≈ 0.94 to 0.97 depending on the exact flow conditions — essentially at the trailing edge, achieving the minimum possible form drag for the given geometry), and then increases slowly to 0.042 at Re_L = 10⁸, a slight increase of approximately 10 percent from the minimum value, attributable to the increase in the turbulent skin friction coefficient — which scales approximately as C_f ∝ Re_L^(-1/7) for a turbulent boundary layer on a flat plate at high Reynolds number, but the increase in the Reynolds number also thins the boundary layer (δ ∝ Re_x^(-1/7)), which for a body of fixed length means that the trailing-edge boundary layer thickness decreases, reducing the displacement effect of the boundary layer on the inviscid outer flow, which slightly improves the pressure recovery and reduces the form drag, partially offsetting the skin friction increase — and to a slight forward movement of the separation point from x/L ≈ 0.96 at Re_L = 5 × 10⁷ to x/L ≈ 0.93 at Re_L = 10⁸, a phenomenon observed in experiments on streamlined bodies at very high Reynolds numbers and attributed to the "supercritical" flow regime in which the boundary layer, although fully turbulent, becomes locally supersonic in the region of maximum flow acceleration — not literally supersonic in the incompressible water flow at U_∞ = 40 m/s (Mach number Ma = U_∞/c_water ≈ 40/1,500 ≈ 0.027, firmly subsonic), but analogous in the sense that the local flow velocity exceeds the speed at which disturbances can propagate upstream through the boundary layer, leading to a sudden change in the pressure distribution and, in some cases, the formation of a weak shock-like compression region that triggers boundary layer separation — an effect that is well-documented for transonic aerodynamic flows but that has been observed less frequently in hydrodynamic flows, where cavitation rather than compressibility effects limits the maximum velocity, and that for the Atlantis hull at 40 m/s (well below the cavitation inception velocity at the 45-meter depth, which would require local pressures below the vapor pressure of water at 10°C, approximately 1.2 kPa absolute, corresponding to a local velocity of √(2(p_ambient - p_vapor)/ρ) = √(2 × (550,000 - 1,200)/1,027) ≈ 32.7 m/s, so cavitation would occur on the hull at approximately U_∞ = 27 m/s based on the maximum local velocity of 1.2U_∞ near the mid-body, providing a natural upper bound on the operating velocity that is more restrictive than the supercritical boundary layer effects).

## 5. VALIDATION AGAINST EXPERIMENTAL DATA AND FULL-SCALE EXTRAPOLATION

The CFD predictions were validated against three independent data sources: (1) the 1:25 scale tow-tank tests at the Carrington Storm Motors Mojave facility, using a fiberglass model of the optimized teardrop geometry (L_model = 0.48 m, D_max = 0.152 m) instrumented with a six-axis ATI Mini40 force/torque sensor (resolution 0.005 N in the drag direction, 0.01 N in the lateral directions) mounted on a streamlined sting that enters the model from the aft end and is aligned with the model axis to minimize sting interference, with the model towed through the 60-meter-long, 3-meter-wide, 2.5-meter-deep tank at velocities of 0.5 to 3.0 m/s (corresponding to full-scale Reynolds numbers of Re_L = 1.1 × 10⁶ to 6.7 × 10⁶ after accounting for the higher kinematic viscosity of the fresh water used in the tow tank, ν_freshwater = 1.00 × 10⁻⁶ m²/s at 20°C, compared to the seawater value of ν = 1.35 × 10⁻⁶ m²/s at 10°C — the full-scale Reynolds number equivalency is achieved not by matching Re_L exactly, which would require a towing velocity of approximately 70 m/s, far beyond the tank's capability, but by operating in the fully turbulent regime where the drag coefficient is only weakly dependent on Re_L for Re_L > 2 × 10⁶, as shown by the Reynolds number sensitivity analysis, and applying the ITTC 1957 model-ship correlation line for skin friction correction to extrapolate the measured model-scale drag to the full-scale Reynolds number). The tow-tank tests measured C_D = 0.043 ± 0.003 at Re_L = 2.7 × 10⁶ (five repeat tests, the uncertainty representing the 95 percent confidence interval), and after applying the ITTC skin friction correction C_D,full = C_D,model + (C_f,ITTC(Re_full) - C_f,ITTC(Re_model)), where C_f,ITTC(Re) = 0.075/(log₁₀(Re) - 2)² is the ITTC 1957 friction line based on the Schoenherr mean line for turbulent skin friction on a smooth flat plate, yielding C_f,ITTC(5 × 10⁷) = 0.00238 and C_f,ITTC(2.7 × 10⁶) = 0.00397, the corrected full-scale drag coefficient is C_D,full = 0.043 + (0.00238 - 0.00397) = 0.0414 ± 0.003, encompassing the CFD prediction of 0.040 within the experimental uncertainty. The remaining discrepancy of approximately 3.5 percent is attributed to three effects not fully captured by the ITTC correction: the form factor, which for a streamlined body is not exactly unity as the ITTC correlation assumes but is closer to k = 0.05 to 0.10 (i.e., the pressure drag of the model is 5 to 10 percent higher than the pressure drag of the full-scale body because the thicker boundary layer at the lower Reynolds number of the model produces a larger displacement effect and a less complete pressure recovery), a correction that would reduce the extrapolated full-scale C_D by approximately 0.001 to 0.002; the surface roughness effect, with the model's surface finish of approximately 0.8 μm Ra (smooth paint) compared to the full-scale hull's expected surface roughness of approximately 5 to 10 μm Ra (the AFP-fabricated LBFRP surface coated with the 2-millimeter MXene electrophoretic deposition layer, which is not as smooth as polished paint but is substantially smoother than the 150-micrometer roughness that would trigger the onset of roughness-induced drag increase — approximately 30 to 40 percent increase in skin friction at Re_L = 5 × 10⁷ for a roughness height of 150 μm according to the Colebrook-White roughness function, a penalty that the Atlantis hull avoids by maintaining the surface finish within the hydraulically smooth regime, defined by the condition that the roughness Reynolds number Re_k = u_τ k/ν < 5, where u_τ = √(τ_w/ρ) is the friction velocity and k is the equivalent sand-grain roughness height, which for the Atlantis hull at U_∞ = 20 m/s is u_τ ≈ 0.45 m/s, yielding Re_k = 0.45 × 10 × 10⁻⁶ / (1.35 × 10⁻⁶) ≈ 3.3 < 5, confirming that the 10 μm surface roughness is hydraulically smooth); and the turbulence intensity effect, with the tow-tank water having a turbulence intensity of approximately 0.5 to 1.0 percent (typical for a well-designed tow tank with a rest period of at least 30 minutes between runs to allow residual turbulence to decay), compared to the turbidite flow's estimated 5 percent intensity, a difference that could shift the transition location and modify the turbulent boundary layer development, with the higher free-stream turbulence promoting earlier transition — already captured by the CFD's transition specification at x/L ≈ 0.05 for the design condition — and possibly modifying the turbulent boundary layer's entrainment rate, an effect that the SST model captures through the turbulence production terms but that may not be fully accurate at the extreme turbulence intensities of turbidite flows, which are beyond the range of the flat-plate boundary layer experiments on which the SST model's closure coefficients were calibrated.

(2) The AVT-183 prolate spheroid database, which provides pressure coefficient distributions and separation locations for a 3:1 prolate spheroid at 0° angle of attack across the Reynolds number range 1.2 × 10⁶ to 8.4 × 10⁶ — a range that, while lower than the Atlantis design Reynolds number of 5 × 10⁷, provides a critical validation point for the CFD methodology's ability to predict the pressure distribution and separation behavior, which are the dominant determinants of the drag coefficient, at Reynolds numbers where the boundary layer is in the process of transitioning from partially laminar to fully turbulent — exactly the regime where turbulence modeling errors are most significant. The Atlantis CFD prediction for the AVT-183 geometry — running the same OpenFOAM-SST methodology on a mesh of 28 million cells generated for the 1.2-meter AVT-183 model geometry — yields a pressure coefficient distribution that matches the experimental data with a root-mean-square error of 0.019 in C_p units over the forward 80 percent of the body (x/L from 0 to 0.8), where the flow is attached and the pressure distribution is primarily determined by the inviscid outer flow, and an error of 0.041 over the aft 20 percent, where the viscous-inviscid interaction through the displacement effect of the thickening boundary layer modifies the pressure distribution in a manner that depends sensitively on the turbulence model's prediction of the Reynolds stress distribution, with the SST model's shear stress limiter producing a slightly earlier separation (x/L ≈ 0.83 compared to the experimental x/L ≈ 0.86 at Re_L = 6.5 × 10⁶) that depresses the pressure recovery in the final 10 to 15 percent of the body, a systematic error that is conservative for the Atlantis application — predicting slightly higher drag than the experimental data would suggest — and that is within the acceptable margin for the anchoring system design.

(3) The full-scale Reynolds number extrapolation using the Granville method, a semi-empirical procedure that combines the ITTC skin friction correction with a form factor determined from the Prohaska method — plotting C_D/C_f,ITTC against C_F^n/C_f,ITTC for n = 4, where C_F is the frictional resistance coefficient calculated for the equivalent flat plate at the same Reynolds number and wetted area as the body, and extrapolating to C_F = 0 to obtain the form factor (1 + k) as the intercept, a method that is standard practice in naval architecture for extrapolating ship resistance from model scale to full scale and that has been validated across hundreds of ship types spanning Reynolds numbers from 10⁶ to 10⁹. Applying the Granville method to the Atlantis hull using the five lowest-turbulence data points from the tow-tank tests (Re_L = 1.1 × 10⁶ to 4.5 × 10⁶, at which the free-stream turbulence has minimal effect on transition) yields a form factor of (1 + k) = 1.08 ± 0.02, meaning that the viscous pressure drag (the form drag component that is correlated with the skin friction through the form factor assumption) is approximately 8 percent of the skin friction drag, and the full-scale drag coefficient at Re_L = 5 × 10⁷ is C_D,full = (1 + k) · C_f,ITTC(5 × 10⁷) = 1.08 × 0.00238 = 0.00257 for the viscous component (skin friction plus viscous pressure drag) plus an additional wave-making resistance component of approximately 0.0 (the hull is submerged at 45 meters depth, where the free-surface effect — wave-making resistance, which accounts for 30 to 50 percent of a surface ship's total resistance at design speed — is entirely absent, because the exponential decay of the wave-induced velocity field with depth, u_wave(z) ∝ e^(kz), where k = 2π/λ is the wave number and for the hull length of 12 meters at a Froude number Fr = U/√(gL) = 20/√(9.81 × 12) ≈ 1.84, the generated waves have a wavelength of λ ≈ 2πU²/g ≈ 256 meters and a wave number k ≈ 0.0245 m⁻¹, such that at z = -45 meters the wave-induced velocity is attenuated by a factor of e^(-0.0245 × 45) ≈ 0.33, and the wave-making resistance — which is proportional to the square of the wave amplitude at the free surface, which itself decays exponentially with depth — is reduced by a factor of e^(-2kz) ≈ 0.11, i.e., to approximately 11 percent of the surface-level wave-making resistance, and the absolute magnitude of the wave-making resistance for a submerged streamlined body at this Froude number is already only approximately 2 to 5 percent of the total resistance, so the submerged wave-making resistance is approximately 0.2 to 0.6 percent of the total drag — well below the threshold of engineering significance), plus the base drag from the truncated trailing edge (C_D,base ≈ 0.0026 as previously determined), yielding C_D,full ≈ 0.0052 for the form-dependent drag components, which added to the skin friction drag — not multiplied by the form factor because the form factor method removes the skin friction from the form drag correlation through the Prohaska extrapolation — gives C_D,full ≈ 0.00257 × 1.00 (the skin friction itself, not form-factored) + 0.00257 × 0.08 (the viscous pressure drag from the form factor) + 0.0026 (base drag) = 0.00257 + 0.00021 + 0.0026 = 0.00538, which is substantially lower than the CFD prediction of 0.040 — a discrepancy that arises because the Granville method, as a low-fidelity empirical extrapolation derived from surface ship data, does not capture the physics of the pressure drag on a fully submerged streamlined body, which is dominated not by the viscous pressure drag (the component correlated with skin friction through the form factor) but by the inviscid pressure drag arising from the limited pressure recovery of the teardrop geometry — the pressure at the trailing edge does not recover fully to the stagnation pressure, even in inviscid flow, because the body has finite thickness at the trailing edge (the blunt closure) and because the inviscid pressure recovery of a prolate spheroid is inherently incomplete due to the geometry's finite-length constraint, an effect that the CFD captures through the Euler (inviscid) component of the pressure distribution but that the Granville method, calibrated for surface ships with transom sterns and immersed transoms where the pressure recovery physics is fundamentally different (involving the hydrostatic pressure of the water surface at the stern rather than the Bernoulli recovery along a closed streamline), does not represent. The Granville method is therefore not directly applicable to the Atlantis hull, and its output is included here only for completeness and to illustrate the limitations of traditional naval architecture extrapolation methods when applied to deeply submerged streamlined bodies — a regime for which the towing-tank community has developed specialized procedures (such as the submarine resistance extrapolation method described in ITTC Recommended Procedure 7.5-02-03-01.4) that combine the ITTC friction line with a form factor determined from the bare-hull resistance test at Froude numbers below 0.4 (to exclude wave-making effects) and a roughness allowance of 0.15 × 10⁻³ to 0.40 × 10⁻³ added to the full-scale friction coefficient to account for the surface roughness of operational submarines.

In summary, the complete CFD derivation of the Atlantis-class teardrop hull geometry converges on a 3.15:1 prolate spheroid with a moderately sharp nose (A_nose = -0.12), a slightly fuller mid-body (A_mid = 0.08), a 12.8° tail cone half-angle, and a 0.52-meter-diameter rounded trailing edge closure, achieving a drag coefficient of C_D = 0.040 at the design Reynolds number of Re_L = 5 × 10⁷, with the drag approximately 71 percent skin friction, 23 percent form drag, and 6 percent base drag, a decomposition that has been validated against RANS-SST, DES, and LES computational methodologies and against 1:25 scale tow-tank experiments, and that provides the hydrodynamic loading basis for the anchoring system design (PART-055), the structural analysis (PART-042), and the turbidite defense integration (PARTS 056-065).
