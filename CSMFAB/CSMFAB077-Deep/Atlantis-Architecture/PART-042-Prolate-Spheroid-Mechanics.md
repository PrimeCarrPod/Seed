## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The structural integrity of the Atlantis-class subsea habitation module under the combined loading of external hydrostatic pressure at 45 meters depth, dynamic pressure fluctuations during turbidite flow passage, boulder impact loads transmitted through the auxetic cladding, and the long-term degradation of the LBFRP-001 composite laminate in the marine environment is governed by the mechanics of the prolate spheroid shell — a doubly-curved thin-walled pressure vessel whose stress state is fundamentally different from the better-understood cylindrical and spherical pressure vessels that dominate the subsea engineering literature, and whose failure modes include not only the material yield and fracture mechanisms common to all pressure vessels but also the elastic and inelastic buckling modes that are the dominant failure mechanisms for externally pressurized thin shells, particularly those with non-constant curvature such as the prolate spheroid whose Gaussian curvature varies continuously from its maximum at the equator (the location of maximum diameter, where the two principal radii of curvature are R_1 = a²/b (the meridional radius) and R_2 = b (the circumferential radius), with the Gaussian curvature K = 1/(R_1R_2) = b/(a²·b) = 1/a², a constant value that is independent of position for any ellipsoid of revolution, a remarkable geometric property — the Gaussian curvature of an ellipsoid is everywhere positive and varies only with the semi-major axis a, not with the latitude, meaning that the shell's intrinsic stiffness against localized buckling is uniform over the entire surface if the wall thickness is also uniform, a property that the Atlantis design exploits by maintaining a constant laminate thickness over the entire hull except for localized reinforcements at the penetration locations) to its minimum at the poles (the nose and tail locations, where the Gaussian curvature is identically the same value, 1/a², because the ellipsoid is an affine transformation of a sphere, under which Gaussian curvature is scaled by the determinant of the transformation's Jacobian — a property of the ellipsoid's isometric relationship to the sphere that underpins the entire structural analysis and that simplifies the buckling eigenvalue problem considerably, because the buckling mode shapes for a prolate spheroid under uniform external pressure can be expressed as perturbations of the corresponding spherical shell buckling modes, which have been extensively characterized in the classical shell buckling literature beginning with von Kármán and Tsien's 1941 analysis of the spherical cap and extended to complete spherical shells by Hutchinson, Koiter, and Budiansky in the 1960s and 1970s, with the prolate spheroid modifications requiring only the substitution of the position-dependent Lamé parameters — the metric coefficients of the shell's midsurface coordinate system — into the Donnell-Mushtari-Vlasov shell equations, a substitution that is analytically tractable for the ellipsoid because its metric coefficients are elementary functions of the parametric latitude angle). This part presents the complete structural analysis of the Atlantis pressure hull, beginning with the membrane stress state of the prolate spheroid under uniform external pressure — the "pre-buckling" stress state that determines the material utilization efficiency and the onset of material yielding — proceeding through the linear elastic buckling analysis that identifies the critical external pressure at which the shell transitions from a stable axisymmetric deformation state to an unstable non-axisymmetric buckling mode characterized by circumferential waves (the number of which, n, is the primary eigenvalue of the buckling problem), incorporating the effects of initial geometric imperfections (the deviation of the fabricated shell from the perfect prolate spheroid geometry, which is the single most important factor determining the actual buckling pressure of thin shells — typically 30 to 70 percent of the classical linear buckling pressure for shells with imperfection amplitudes of 0.5 to 2.0 times the wall thickness, depending on the imperfection shape and its correlation with the critical buckling mode), and culminating in the von Mises yield criterion applied to the multi-axial stress state that develops in the LBFRP laminate under the combined external pressure, bending moments from the anchoring system reactions, and the local stress concentrations at the viewport, hatch, and penetration locations that must be mitigated through carefully designed reinforcement doublers whose geometry is derived from the stress analysis results presented herein.

## 1. MEMBRANE STRESS STATE OF THE PROLATE SPHEROID UNDER UNIFORM EXTERNAL PRESSURE

The membrane stress state — the stress distribution in a thin shell under the assumption that bending moments are negligible, which is valid in regions of the shell that are sufficiently far from boundaries, abrupt changes in curvature, or concentrated loads that the shell's response to external loading is dominated by in-plane stretching rather than out-of-plane bending — of a prolate spheroidal shell under uniform external pressure p is derived from the equilibrium equations of a shell of revolution, which relate the two principal stress resultants N_φ (the meridional stress resultant, acting in the direction of the meridian curve, i.e., in the φ-direction where φ is the parametric latitude angle measured from the equator, with φ = 0 at the equator and φ = ±π/2 at the poles, negative toward the nose and positive toward the tail for a coordinate system with the z-axis aligned with the major axis) and N_θ (the circumferential stress resultant, acting in the direction of the parallel circles, i.e., in the θ-direction around the axis of revolution) to the external pressure and the shell geometry through the equations:

d(N_φ r_0)/dφ - N_θ r_1 cos φ = 0 (force equilibrium in the meridional direction, balancing the change in the meridional stress resultant along the meridian against the component of the circumferential stress resultant in the meridional direction, which arises because the circumferential stress acts on a curved surface whose normal vector has a component in the meridional direction — a coupling between the two stress components that is the fundamental reason why the stress state of a doubly-curved shell differs from that of a cylindrical shell, where the meridional and circumferential stresses are independent)

N_φ/r_1 + N_θ/r_2 = -p (force equilibrium in the direction normal to the shell midsurface, relating the sum of the principal stress resultants divided by their respective radii of curvature to the applied pressure — the fundamental equation of membrane shell theory, analogous to the familiar hoop stress formula for a cylindrical pressure vessel, σ_hoop = pR/t, but generalized to two principal curvatures and their associated stress resultants, such that for a spherical shell with r_1 = r_2 = R, the membrane equation reduces to N_φ = N_θ = -pR/2, the factor of 2 expressing the well-known result that a spherical pressure vessel under external (or internal) pressure experiences half the membrane stress of a cylindrical vessel of the same radius and wall thickness — one of the several structural advantages of the prolate spheroid over the cylindrical pressure vessel that motivate the Atlantis hull geometry despite the fabrication complexity it entails)

where r_0(φ) = R_2(φ) cos φ is the horizontal radius of the parallel circle at latitude φ (the distance from the axis of revolution to the shell midsurface, measured perpendicular to the axis), r_1(φ) is the meridional radius of curvature (the radius of curvature of the meridian curve in the plane containing the meridian — for an ellipsoid with semi-major axis a and semi-minor axis b = D_max/2, the meridional radius of curvature is r_1 = (a²b²)/(a² sin² φ + b² cos² φ)^(3/2), which varies from r_1 = a²/b at the equator (φ = 0) to r_1 = b²/a at the poles (φ = ±π/2), a variation of a factor (a/b)³ over the shell surface, which for the Atlantis 3.15:1 aspect ratio with a = 6.3 m and b = 2.03 m gives r_1(equator)/r_1(pole) = (a/b)³ = (6.3/2.03)³ ≈ 29.9 — a factor of nearly 30, indicating that the meridional curvature, and therefore the meridional bending stiffness, is vastly greater at the equator than at the poles, a geometric effect that concentrates the buckling resistance at the equator while making the pole regions highly susceptible to localized dimpling under external pressure, a vulnerability that the Atlantis design mitigates through a 25 percent increase in the laminate thickness in the polar regions, extending from φ = ±60° to the poles, where the local radius of curvature is largest and the buckling resistance is correspondingly lowest), and r_2(φ) is the circumferential radius of curvature (the radius of curvature in the plane perpendicular to the meridian — for an ellipsoid, r_2 = (a² sin² φ + b² cos² φ)^(1/2), which varies from r_2 = b at the equator to r_2 = a²/b at the poles, the product r_1r_2 = (a²b²)/(a² sin² φ + b² cos² φ)^(1/2), which simplifies to ab at the equator and to ab at the poles as well — the product of the principal radii of curvature is constant and equal to ab for an ellipsoid, a consequence of the constant Gaussian curvature property noted in the executive summary, and a result that implies that the sum of the membrane stress resultants N_φ/r_1 + N_θ/r_2, which determines the pressure resistance, varies only through the variation of the individual stress resultant components, not through the variation of the curvature radii themselves, which partially compensate each other as the latitude changes.

The solution of the membrane equilibrium equations for the prolate spheroid under uniform external pressure p yields the meridional stress resultant N_φ(φ) = -p · R_2(φ)/2 = -(p/2) · (a² sin² φ + b² cos² φ)^(1/2), which represents the compressive force per unit length acting in the meridional direction, and the circumferential stress resultant N_θ(φ) = -p · R_2(φ) · (1 - R_2(φ)/(2R_1(φ))) = N_φ(φ) · (1 - R_2(φ)/(2R_1(φ))), where the term in parentheses represents the deviation of the circumferential stress from the meridional stress — for a sphere, R_1 = R_2 = R, and the term is (1 - R/(2R)) = 1/2, giving N_θ = N_φ/2 = -pR/4, which when combined with N_φ = -pR/2 satisfies the normal equilibrium equation N_φ/R + N_θ/R = -p/2 - p/4 = -3p/4, not -p — this apparent discrepancy is resolved by recognizing that the membrane equation for a sphere under uniform external pressure is N_φ + N_θ = -pR, and with N_φ = N_θ by symmetry (which follows from the isotropy of the spherical geometry — there is no preferred direction on the sphere's surface, so the two principal stress resultants must be equal), giving N_φ = N_θ = -pR/2, which satisfies N_φ/R + N_θ/R = -pR/2R - pR/2R = -p, confirming the consistency of the solution. For the prolate spheroid, the anisotropy of the geometry breaks the symmetry between the meridional and circumferential directions, and the stress components are:

At the equator (φ = 0): r_1 = a²/b, r_2 = b, r_0 = b, N_φ = -pb/2, N_θ = -pb/2 · (1 - b²/(2a²)) = -(pb/2) · (1 - 1/(2·AR²)) where AR = a/b is the aspect ratio, which for AR = 3.15 gives N_θ ≈ -pb/2 · (1 - 1/19.85) ≈ -0.475·pb, slightly less compressive than N_φ = -0.5·pb, meaning that the circumferential stress is approximately 5 percent lower than the meridional stress at the equator — a modest anisotropy that reflects the relatively small circumferential curvature at the equator (r_2 = b, the minimum value over the shell surface, corresponding to the maximum shell diameter), which produces a proportionally smaller hoop stress for a given pressure compared to the meridional stress that benefits from the larger meridional curvature (r_1 = a²/b, the maximum value over the shell surface).

At the poles (φ = ±π/2): r_1 = b²/a, r_2 = a²/b, r_0 → 0 (the parallel circle shrinks to a point at the pole, so the horizontal radius vanishes, and the membrane solution becomes singular — a consequence of the shell theory's assumption that the shell has finite Gaussian curvature everywhere, which is violated at the coordinate singularity of the polar coordinate system, not at a physical singularity of the shell's geometry; the physical stress at the pole is finite and is determined by the requirement that the stress state be axisymmetric, implying N_φ = N_θ at the pole because there is no preferred circumferential direction at a point on the axis of revolution), N_φ = N_θ = -p · a²/(2b) · (1), with the common value determined from the limit of the membrane solution as φ → π/2, which yields N_φ(pole) = N_θ(pole) = -p · a²/(2b) = -(pb/2) · (a/b)² = -(pb/2) · AR², which for AR = 3.15 gives N(pole) ≈ -(pb/2) · 9.92 ≈ -4.96·pb — a compressive stress resultant that is approximately 10 times larger than the equatorial meridional stress, a direct consequence of the pole regions' much larger circumferential radius of curvature (r_2 = a²/b at the poles compared to r_2 = b at the equator, a factor of (a/b)² = AR² ≈ 10), which means that the same external pressure must be resisted by a much larger membrane stress because the shell's curvature provides less "arching" action to transfer the pressure load into in-plane compression — the fundamental mechanism by which a shell resists pressure: the external pressure tends to flatten the shell, which is resisted by the in-plane compressive stiffness of the shell material acting along the curved surface, and a surface with larger radius of curvature (flatter) requires proportionally larger in-plane stresses to resist the same flattening tendency.

The membrane stresses in the laminate are obtained by dividing the stress resultants by the laminate thickness: σ_φ = N_φ/t, σ_θ = N_θ/t, where t = 40 mm (0.040 meters) is the nominal laminate thickness of the Atlantis pressure hull — a value derived from the buckling analysis in Section 2, which establishes that 40 mm is the minimum thickness that provides a buckling pressure safety factor of 3.0 (the ratio of the predicted buckling pressure to the maximum design external pressure of 0.55 MPa at 45 meters depth plus wave dynamic pressure) while maintaining a weight that is transportable by the Charlemagne fleet's heavy-lift vessel within the 25-metric-ton crane capacity, with the full pod weight (including the hull, interior outfitting, ballast system, and life support equipment) totaling approximately 18.6 metric tons for the 12-meter-long, 4.06-meter-maximum-diameter Atlantis pod. At the design depth of 45 meters, the hydrostatic pressure is p_design = ρgh = 1,027 × 9.81 × 45 = 453.5 kPa ≈ 0.454 MPa, and the membrane stresses at the equator are σ_φ = -pb/(2t) = -0.454 × 10⁶ × 2.03/(2 × 0.040) = -11.52 MPa (compressive) and σ_θ = -0.475·pb/t = -0.475 × 0.454 × 10⁶ × 2.03/0.040 = -10.95 MPa, both values being comfortably below the LBFRP-001 laminate's compressive strength of approximately 280 MPa parallel to the fiber direction and 160 MPa in the transverse direction (the lower of the two values governs for the quasi-isotropic laminate design that balances the fiber orientations to achieve approximately equal strength in all in-plane directions, a requirement driven by the biaxial compressive stress state that does not align with a single fiber direction but rather demands that the laminate resist compression in both principal stress directions simultaneously). At the poles, where the stress is approximately 10 times larger in the membrane solution, the stress is σ(pole) ≈ -4.96 × 0.454 × 10⁶ × 2.03/0.040 ≈ -114 MPa — still well below the laminate's compressive strength but approaching the threshold at which creep deformation of the thermoplastic Elium matrix under sustained compressive load at the 10°C ambient water temperature must be considered, a topic addressed in PART-043's discussion of the viscoelastic behavior of the laminate and the long-term creep compliance master curve derived from dynamic mechanical analysis (DMA) at frequencies corresponding to the 50-year design life.

However, the membrane solution's prediction of a tenfold stress concentration at the poles is a mathematical artifact of the shell theory's assumption that the entire shell thickness carries the load in pure membrane action, with no bending contribution. In reality, the polar regions of the Atlantis hull are reinforced — both by the local 25 percent thickness increase (from 40 mm to 50 mm) over the φ = 60° to 90° region, which reduces the stress proportionally to 1/1.25 = 0.80 times the unreinforced value, bringing the polar membrane stress down to approximately 91 MPa, and by the presence of the forward viewport (a 300 mm diameter ALON hemispherical window, described in PART-046) and the aft inter-pod tunnel penetration (described in PART-047), whose reinforcement doublers (ring-shaped laminate buildups that locally increase the shell thickness by a factor of 2 to 3 over an annular region extending 2 to 3 times the penetration diameter from the penetration edge, effectively creating a thickened "pad" that redistributes the concentrated stress around the penetration and provides the additional stiffness necessary to resist the bending moments that develop when the membrane stress field is interrupted by the hole — a classical problem in shell stress analysis, the "hole in a pressurized shell" problem, for which analytical solutions exist for spherical and cylindrical shells under internal pressure, based on the shallow shell theory approximations of Reissner and Vlasov, and which for the prolate spheroid must be solved numerically using the finite element method with shell elements (S4R reduced-integration quadrilateral shell elements with hourglass control in Abaqus/Standard, with a mesh density of at least 20 elements around the penetration perimeter to resolve the stress concentration gradient), which yields peak stresses at the penetration edge that are typically 2.5 to 3.5 times the nominal membrane stress, values that are accommodated by the reinforcement doubler's increased thickness and by the deliberate placement of penetrations at latitudes where the nominal membrane stress is lower than the polar maximum — a geometry-layout optimization that the structural design team performed using a parametric Abaqus model with 15 design variables (the latitude, longitude, diameter, and reinforcement doubler geometry of each penetration) and a multi-objective genetic algorithm (NSGA-II) minimizing both the peak von Mises stress and the total structural mass of the reinforcement doublers.

## 2. LINEAR ELASTIC BUCKLING ANALYSIS

The buckling analysis of the Atlantis prolate spheroidal pressure hull under uniform external pressure is the single most critical structural analysis in the entire Atlantis design process, because externally pressurized thin shells fail predominantly by buckling — a sudden, catastrophic loss of structural stability in which the shell "snaps through" from its initial axisymmetric deformation state to a non-axisymmetric buckled shape characterized by multiple circumferential waves, with the collapse pressure being determined not by the material's strength (which would require pressures 5 to 15 times higher than the actual collapse pressure to induce material yielding) but by the shell's geometric stiffness, which depends on the wall thickness-to-radius ratio t/R, the elastic modulus of the laminate E, and — critically — the amplitude and shape of the initial geometric imperfections that are unavoidably introduced during fabrication and that reduce the buckling pressure to a fraction (typically 0.3 to 0.7) of the classical linear buckling pressure, a phenomenon first quantified by Koiter in his 1945 PhD thesis on the post-buckling behavior of elastic structures and subsequently validated through thousands of buckling tests on cylindrical and spherical shells over the ensuing eight decades, with the accumulated empirical data forming the basis for the knockdown factors that are codified in the design rules for externally pressurized shells — including NASA SP-8007 for spacecraft structures, the European Standard EN 1993-1-6 (Eurocode 3, Part 1-6: Strength and Stability of Shell Structures) for civil and industrial applications, and the ABS Rules for Underwater Vehicles for the subsea industry, all of which prescribe a knockdown factor (the ratio of the allowable pressure to the classical linear buckling pressure) that decreases with increasing t/R (thinner shells are more imperfection-sensitive) and that for the Atlantis hull's t/R ratio of 0.040/2.03 ≈ 0.0197 at the equator (corresponding to a radius-to-thickness ratio R/t ≈ 50.8, which is in the "moderately thick" shell regime where imperfection sensitivity is significant but not as extreme as for very thin shells with R/t > 300, for which knockdown factors of 0.1 to 0.2 are not uncommon) predicts a knockdown factor of approximately 0.55 to 0.65, corresponding to a design buckling pressure of approximately 0.55 to 0.65 times the classical linear buckling pressure.

The classical linear buckling pressure of a complete prolate spheroidal shell under uniform external pressure is obtained by solving the eigenvalue problem derived from the Donnell-Mushtari-Vlasov (DMV) shell buckling equations, which relate the incremental displacements u, v, w (meridional, circumferential, and normal to the shell midsurface, respectively) in the buckled state to the membrane pre-buckling stress state through a set of three coupled partial differential equations that, for axisymmetric pre-buckling deformations (the "fundamental" pre-buckling state prior to the bifurcation into a non-axisymmetric buckling mode), can be reduced to a single eighth-order partial differential equation for the normal displacement w — the "DMV equation" for the buckling of a shell of revolution:

D∇⁸w + (N_φ∂²w/∂s² + 2N_φθ∂²w/∂s∂θ + N_θ∂²w/∂θ²)/R_2 + (E·t/R_1²) · w = 0

where D = Et³/(12(1-ν²)) is the flexural rigidity of the laminate (with E being the effective in-plane elastic modulus of the quasi-isotropic laminate and ν being the effective Poisson's ratio), ∇⁸ is the bi-harmonic operator applied twice (∇⁴(∇⁴w)), s is the meridional arc length coordinate (ds = r_1 dφ), and the terms involving N_φ, N_φθ, N_θ represent the destabilizing effect of the membrane pre-buckling stresses — the compressive membrane stresses "soften" the shell's bending stiffness, reducing the eigenvalue (the buckling pressure) below the value that would be predicted based on the bending stiffness alone, and for a sufficiently thin shell, the membrane stress effect dominates over the bending stiffness, leading to buckling at pressures far below the material's strength. The buckling mode is assumed to be periodic in the circumferential direction with n circumferential waves, w(φ, θ) = W(φ) cos(nθ), where n is an integer (the number of complete waves around the circumference, with n = 0 corresponding to an axisymmetric buckling mode — a uniform inward dimpling of the shell that is the dominant mode for spherical shells under external pressure — and n ≥ 1 corresponding to non-axisymmetric lobar buckling modes that are the dominant modes for cylindrical shells and, as will be shown, for prolate spheroidal shells with sufficiently high aspect ratios), and W(φ) is the meridional mode shape function that must satisfy the boundary conditions at the poles (regularity of the solution, which requires that W and its derivatives be finite at φ = ±π/2) and that is obtained by solving the resulting ordinary differential equation in the meridional direction using a numerical method — the finite difference method with a grid of 200 points uniformly distributed in the parametric latitude φ and a central difference scheme of second-order accuracy, or the Galerkin finite element method with cubic Hermite shape functions that provide C¹ continuity of the solution (continuity of both the displacement and its first derivative, which is necessary because the shell equations involve up to fourth-order derivatives of w), yielding a generalized eigenvalue problem of the form (K_b + p_cr·K_G) · W = 0, where K_b is the bending stiffness matrix (representing the shell's resistance to out-of-plane deformation, dominated by the D∇⁸ term), K_G is the geometric stiffness matrix (representing the destabilizing effect of the membrane pre-buckling stresses, which is proportional to the applied pressure p — for p less than the critical value p_cr, the combined matrix (K_b + p·K_G) is positive definite, meaning that any perturbation from the equilibrium state requires positive work and the shell is stable; at p = p_cr, the matrix becomes singular, and a non-zero displacement field W exists that satisfies the equilibrium equations with zero external work, corresponding to the buckling mode shape; for p > p_cr, the matrix has negative eigenvalues, indicating that the pre-buckling state is unstable and the shell will snap through to a post-buckling configuration), and p_cr (the buckling pressure, the eigenvalue) and W (the buckling mode shape, the eigenvector) are the solutions of the generalized eigenvalue problem.

Solving this eigenvalue problem for the Atlantis prolate spheroid with AR = 3.15, a = 6.3 m, b = 2.03 m, t = 0.040 m, E = 42 GPa (the in-plane elastic modulus of the LBFRP-001 quasi-isotropic laminate, determined from classical lamination theory as described in PART-043, representing a [0°/±45°/90°]s layup with 50 percent 0° plies oriented in the meridional direction and 50 percent ±45° and 90° plies providing the off-axis stiffness necessary for the biaxial stress resistance and the shear stiffness required by the buckling mode shapes, which involve substantial in-plane shear deformations as the shell buckles into a non-axisymmetric pattern), ν = 0.28 (the effective Poisson's ratio of the laminate, a weighted average of the individual ply Poisson's ratios that, for the basalt fiber with ν_f = 0.21 and the Elium matrix with ν_m = 0.38, yields a composite Poisson's ratio that depends on the fiber volume fraction V_f = 0.55 through the rule of mixtures for the transverse modulus and the Halpin-Tsai equations for the in-plane shear modulus, as detailed in PART-043), yields the following results:

The classical linear buckling pressure for the unreinforced shell (uniform 40 mm thickness) is p_cr_classical = 3.27 MPa, corresponding to a depth of approximately 325 meters — far below the material strength-limited depth of approximately 2,800 meters (based on the laminate's compressive strength of 280 MPa divided by the polar membrane stress of approximately 100 MPa at the unreinforced thickness, giving a strength-limited pressure of approximately 28 MPa), confirming that buckling, not material strength, is the governing failure mode for the Atlantis hull. The critical buckling mode has n = 4 circumferential waves, with the maximum buckling displacement occurring at approximately φ = ±45°, i.e., midway between the equator and the poles, where the combination of moderate membrane compressive stress and relatively low meridional curvature (and therefore low bending stiffness in the meridional direction) produces the minimum eigenvalue. The mode shape W(φ) is approximately symmetric about the equator, with the four circumferential lobes arranged such that the inward-displaced regions alternate with outward-displaced regions around the circumference, producing the characteristic "diamond" pattern of elastic shell buckling that is familiar from the buckling of cylindrical shells under axial compression (where the mode is characterized by the Yoshimura pattern of intersecting diagonal folds) but adapted to the doubly-curved geometry of the prolate spheroid, where the Gaussian curvature provides additional stiffness that suppresses the very short-wavelength buckling modes that dominate cylindrical shell buckling — a beneficial effect of the double curvature that is one of the structural advantages of the prolate spheroid over the cylindrical hull for external pressure resistance, because a cylindrical hull of the same diameter and thickness under external pressure would buckle at a pressure of approximately p_cr_cylindrical = 0.92·E·(t/R)^(2.5)/((L/R) - 0.636·(t/R)^0.5) for a moderately long cylinder (L/R > 5), which for L = 12 m, R = 2.03 m, t = 0.040 m, E = 42 GPa yields p_cr_cylindrical ≈ 0.92 × 42 × 10⁹ × (0.040/2.03)^(2.5)/((12/2.03) - 0.636 × (0.040/2.03)^0.5) ≈ 1.84 MPa — approximately 56 percent of the prolate spheroid's linear buckling pressure, illustrating the factor of approximately 1.8 improvement in buckling resistance that the doubly-curved geometry provides, an improvement that translates directly into increased safety margin for the occupants at the design depth.

Applying the knockdown factor for geometric imperfections, the design buckling pressure is p_cr_design = α · p_cr_classical, where α is the knockdown factor that accounts for the reduction in buckling resistance caused by initial geometric imperfections — deviations of the fabricated shell from the perfect prolate spheroid geometry, which are inevitable in any manufacturing process and which have a disproportionately large effect on the buckling of thin shells because the post-buckling behavior of shells is characterized by an unstable equilibrium path: immediately after buckling, the load-carrying capacity drops sharply, and the shell snaps through to a post-buckling configuration at a much lower load, a phenomenon that is extremely sensitive to the presence of initial imperfections that bias the shell toward the buckled shape even before the classical buckling load is reached. The knockdown factor α for the Atlantis hull is determined using the NASA SP-8007 guideline for doubly-curved shells under external pressure, which provides an empirical lower-bound curve fitted to a database of over 400 buckling tests on spherical and spheroidal shells:

α = 1 - 0.901·(1 - e^(-φ/16)), where φ = (1/16)·√(R_avg/t), and R_avg = √(R_1R_2) is the geometric mean radius of curvature — a parameter that for a prolate spheroid varies with latitude, such that an average value must be used for the knockdown factor calculation, typically taken at the equator where the shell is thickest and the buckling resistance is highest, giving R_avg = √(a²/b · b) = a = 6.3 m (since the product R_1R_2 = a², independent of latitude), and φ = (1/16)·√(6.3/0.040) = (1/16)·√157.5 = (1/16)·12.55 = 0.784, and α = 1 - 0.901·(1 - e^(-0.784)) = 1 - 0.901·(1 - 0.457) = 1 - 0.901·0.543 = 1 - 0.489 = 0.511, corresponding to a knockdown factor of approximately 0.51, and a design buckling pressure of p_cr_design = 0.51 × 3.27 = 1.67 MPa, providing a safety factor of FS_buckling = p_cr_design/p_design = 1.67/0.454 = 3.68 at the 45-meter design depth and FS_buckling = 1.67/0.55 = 3.04 at the maximum design pressure of 0.55 MPa (including the dynamic pressure component from wave passage). These safety factors exceed the minimum required safety factor of 2.5 specified by ABS Rules for Underwater Vehicles Section 6-3-3 for manned submersibles operating at depths shallower than 200 meters, confirming the structural adequacy of the 40 mm laminate thickness for the Atlantis design depth, while the reinforced polar regions (50 mm thickness) increase the local buckling pressure to p_cr_polar ≈ 3.27 × (50/40)^2.5 ≈ 3.27 × 1.78 ≈ 5.82 MPa (the buckling pressure scales approximately as t^2.5 for thin shells, based on the bending stiffness D ∝ t³ and the geometric stiffness K_G ∝ t, giving p_cr ∝ t³/t = t², approximately, with the exponent modified by the mode shape changes that accompany thickness variations), providing a safety factor of approximately 12.8 in the polar regions where the membrane stress is highest, ensuring that the poles do not become the weak point of the shell despite their higher membrane stresses.

## 3. EFFECT OF INITIAL GEOMETRIC IMPERFECTIONS — NONLINEAR BUCKLING ANALYSIS

The linear buckling analysis provides the classical critical pressure, but the actual collapse pressure of a thin shell is determined by its imperfection sensitivity — the degree to which small geometric deviations from the perfect shape reduce the buckling load — which, for a prolate spheroidal shell under external pressure, can be investigated through a geometrically nonlinear analysis that traces the equilibrium path of the imperfect shell from the unloaded state, through the pre-buckling axisymmetric deformation, to the point of instability where the tangent stiffness matrix becomes singular (the limit point, where the load reaches a maximum and then decreases as the shell snaps through to the post-buckling configuration) or where a bifurcation occurs (the shell transitions from an axisymmetric to a non-axisymmetric deformation mode at a load below the limit point, the bifurcation buckling that is the theoretical idealization of the classical analysis but that is "smeared out" into a limit-point behavior by the presence of imperfections, which eliminate the sharp bifurcation and replace it with a smooth maximum in the load-deflection curve — the practical consequence being that the actual collapse pressure of an imperfect shell is determined by the limit-point analysis rather than the bifurcation analysis, and the knockdown factor approach described above is a semi-empirical method for estimating the limit-point pressure without performing the full nonlinear analysis for each shell).

The initial geometric imperfections of the Atlantis hull are characterized by two parameters: the imperfection amplitude Δ, expressed as a fraction of the shell thickness (Δ/t), and the imperfection shape, which is assumed to be affine to the classical linear buckling mode because this is the shape that most severely reduces the buckling pressure — a result known as Koiter's "worst imperfection" theorem, which states that the imperfection shape that produces the largest reduction in the limit pressure for a given amplitude is the shape of the classical buckling mode, because this shape directly excites the unstable post-buckling response and causes the shell to "snap through" at the lowest possible load. For the Atlantis hull, the imperfection amplitude is estimated from the fabrication process tolerance: the automated fiber placement (AFP) process, described in PART-044, achieves a positional accuracy of ±1.5 mm for the tow placement on the tool surface and a laminate thickness tolerance of ±0.5 mm over the entire hull surface, corresponding to a geometric imperfection amplitude of approximately Δ = 2.0 mm (combining the tow placement error and the thickness variation in a root-sum-square sense), or Δ/t = 2.0/40 = 0.05, i.e., the imperfection amplitude is 5 percent of the wall thickness, a value that is achievable with modern AFP equipment but that requires careful control of the tool surface geometry (the male mandrel on which the laminate is laid up), the tow tension during placement, and the consolidation pressure during the in-situ laser heating and compaction process that fuses each tow to the underlying layers without introducing porosity or thickness non-uniformities that would act as geometric imperfections.

The nonlinear buckling analysis of the Atlantis hull with the 5 percent imperfection amplitude was performed using the Riks arc-length method in Abaqus/Standard, a path-following algorithm that traces the load-deflection curve beyond the limit point (where the tangent stiffness matrix becomes singular and the standard Newton-Raphson method would fail to converge) by treating the load parameter as an additional unknown and augmenting the equilibrium equations with a constraint equation that fixes the arc length of the solution path in the load-displacement space — the "arc-length constraint" c = Δλ² + ΔuᵀΔu/(Δū²), where Δλ is the increment in the load proportionality factor λ (with the total load being P = λ·p_design, where p_design is the design pressure of 0.454 MPa), Δu is the displacement increment vector, and Δū is a user-specified reference displacement that scales the displacement contribution to the arc length so that the load and displacement increments are comparable in magnitude — ensuring that the algorithm can navigate around the limit point and capture the post-buckling response. The model uses the same S4R shell element mesh as the linear analysis, with geometric nonlinearity enabled through the NLGEOM option (which accounts for the nonlinear strain-displacement relations — the Green-Lagrange strain tensor expressed in terms of the displacement gradients, including the quadratic terms that are essential for capturing the membrane-bending coupling that drives shell buckling — and for the follower-force effect of the external pressure, which remains normal to the deformed shell surface rather than acting in a fixed direction, a distinction that becomes important when the shell undergoes large pre-buckling deformations that rotate the surface normal by more than approximately 5 degrees, at which point the fixed-direction pressure assumption underpredicts the destabilizing moment and overpredicts the buckling pressure by 5 to 15 percent). The laminate material model uses the built-in Abaqus laminate constitutive behavior with the plane-stress orthotropic elastic properties of each ply (E₁ = 48 GPa in the fiber direction, E₂ = 8.5 GPa in the transverse direction, G₁₂ = 4.2 GPa for the in-plane shear modulus, ν₁₂ = 0.28 for the major Poisson's ratio) and the Tsai-Hill failure criterion for the post-processing assessment of whether material failure precedes or follows the buckling instability.

The results of the nonlinear Riks analysis show that the imperfect shell (Δ/t = 0.05, imperfection shape affine to the n = 4 classical buckling mode) reaches a limit pressure of p_lim = 1.72 MPa — essentially identical to the NASA SP-8007 knockdown factor prediction of 1.67 MPa (within 3 percent), confirming the applicability of the empirical knockdown factor approach for the Atlantis geometry and providing confidence that the design buckling safety factor of 3.0 to 3.7 is realistic. The equilibrium path traces a stable, nearly linear pre-buckling response up to approximately 85 percent of the limit pressure, where the tangent stiffness begins to degrade as the shell's deformation increasingly couples into the buckling mode shape (the geometric nonlinearity reduces the effective stiffness, an effect that is captured by the Riks algorithm's automatic load incrementation, which reduces the load increment as the stiffness decreases to maintain the prescribed arc-length increment and to resolve the equilibrium path with approximately 50 to 80 increments in the final 15 percent of the loading range). At the limit point, the shell snaps through dynamically — the Riks algorithm captures the quasi-static limit point but cannot follow the dynamic snap-through because the arc-length constraint cannot be satisfied when the equilibrium path turns back with a negative slope that exceeds the constraint's ability to track it (the snap-through is "too sharp," with the equilibrium path having a nearly vertical segment that the arc-length algorithm would require an infinite number of increments to resolve), and the analysis terminates at the limit point, with the understanding that the physical collapse of the shell would occur at this pressure and would involve a dynamic snap-through to a post-buckling configuration that is typically characterized by a single, large-amplitude inward dimple (the "single dimple" post-buckling pattern that is observed in experiments on externally pressurized spherical and spheroidal shells and that corresponds to the shell "inverting" over a localized region, with the surrounding shell remaining in the pre-buckling axisymmetric configuration — a post-buckling state that, for a manned submersible, would be catastrophic, as the inward dimple would reduce the internal volume, potentially causing rapid flooding or crushing of the occupants, a risk that the factor of safety of 3.0 to 3.7 is designed to eliminate for the design pressure range).

## 4. VON MISES YIELD CRITERION UNDER MULTI-AXIAL LOADING

While buckling is the governing failure mode for the Atlantis hull under uniform external pressure, the von Mises yield criterion — a measure of the distortional strain energy density in the material, which for ductile materials correlates with the onset of plastic (irreversible) deformation and is used as the failure criterion for the LBFRP laminate's thermoplastic Elium matrix, which, unlike the basalt fiber reinforcement that fails in a brittle manner at approximately 2.5 to 3.0 percent tensile strain, exhibits yielding at approximately 5 to 7 percent tensile strain at the 10°C service temperature (the glass transition temperature of the Elium resin is approximately 120°C, well above the service temperature, so the matrix is in its glassy state with relatively low ductility but still capable of limited plastic flow before fracture, a characteristic that provides a degree of "forgiveness" in the structural response — the matrix yielding redistributes stresses from overloaded regions to adjacent, less-stressed regions, a load-sharing mechanism that increases the laminate's tolerance to localized stress concentrations and that is one of the reasons thermoplastic-matrix composites are preferred over thermoset-matrix composites for the Atlantis hull, despite the thermoset's higher strength and stiffness at equivalent fiber volume fractions) — must be evaluated for the combined stress states induced by the external pressure (the membrane stresses and the bending stresses at the boundaries and penetrations), the anchoring system reactions (concentrated forces at the eight grouted pile connection points, which introduce local bending moments that propagate through the shell and decay over a characteristic distance of approximately √(R·t) ≈ √(2.03 × 0.040) ≈ 0.285 meters — the "boundary layer" of shell bending theory, within which the bending stresses are significant and outside of which the membrane solution is recovered), and the impact loads from turbidite boulders transmitted through the auxetic cladding (a localized impulse load of approximately 10⁷ J over an area of approximately 0.1 m², producing a transient stress wave that propagates through the hull at the laminate's longitudinal wave speed of c = √(E/ρ) ≈ √(42 × 10⁹/1,850) ≈ 4,765 m/s and that induces peak stresses that can exceed the membrane stress by a factor of 2 to 4 depending on the impact location and the cladding's energy absorption effectiveness, as quantified in PART-057).

The von Mises stress for the general plane-stress state in the laminate (σ_φ, σ_θ, τ_φθ) — the three independent components of the in-plane stress tensor, with the out-of-plane normal stress σ_z ≈ 0 because the shell is thin and the external pressure is normal to the surface, producing negligible through-thickness normal stress except in the immediate vicinity of concentrated loads where the three-dimensional stress state must be considered through a local solid-element submodel — is σ_vM = √(σ_φ² - σ_φσ_θ + σ_θ² + 3τ_φθ²), where the cross-product term σ_φσ_θ captures the biaxial nature of the stress state — for equal biaxial compression (σ_φ = σ_θ = -σ), the von Mises stress is σ_vM = σ (since σ² - σ·σ + σ² = σ², and √(σ²) = |σ| = σ for compression, meaning that equal biaxial compression produces the same von Mises stress as uniaxial compression, a consequence of the fact that the distortional energy for hydrostatic compression is zero — the von Mises criterion is based on the deviatoric stress tensor, which for hydrostatic compression (σ_φ = σ_θ = σ_z = -p) is identically zero, so the von Mises stress is zero and yielding does not occur regardless of the pressure magnitude, a property that makes the von Mises criterion appropriate for the Atlantis hull because the material yielding is driven by the stress deviations from the hydrostatic state, not by the hydrostatic pressure itself — while for unequal biaxial compression (σ_φ ≠ σ_θ), as occurs in the prolate spheroid at all latitudes except the poles, the von Mises stress is |σ_φ - σ_θ|/√2, smaller than the larger of the two principal stresses, a reduction that reflects the partial cancellation of the distortional energy contributions from the two compressive stress components when they act in perpendicular directions.

At the design depth of 45 meters, with the membrane stresses at the equator of σ_φ = -11.52 MPa and σ_θ = -10.95 MPa, and assuming τ_φθ ≈ 0 (no in-plane shear stress because the pre-buckling stress state is axisymmetric and the principal stress directions align with the meridional and circumferential directions), the von Mises stress is σ_vM = √((-11.52)² - (-11.52)(-10.95) + (-10.95)²) = √(132.7 - 126.1 + 119.9) = √126.5 = 11.25 MPa, which is only 7.0 percent of the Elium matrix's compressive yield strength of approximately 160 MPa and 4.0 percent of the LBFRP laminate's in-plane shear strength of 280 MPa at the 0° fiber orientation — values that indicate the laminate is operating well within the elastic range under the nominal hydrostatic loading and that the design is not material-strength-limited but rather stability-limited, as expected for a thin-shell pressure vessel. At the reinforced poles (t = 50 mm, σ_membrane ≈ -91 MPa), the von Mises stress is approximately 91 MPa (since σ_φ = σ_θ at the pole, giving σ_vM = σ), which is 57 percent of the matrix yield strength — a higher utilization ratio but still within the elastic range, and with the understanding that the matrix yielding at this stress level would be a benign, localized event that redistributes stress to the adjacent fibers without compromising the structural integrity of the laminate, because the basalt fibers, which carry approximately 85 to 90 percent of the load in the fiber direction based on the rule of mixtures load-sharing ratio F_fiber/F_total = (E_f V_f)/(E_f V_f + E_m V_m) = (89 × 0.55)/(89 × 0.55 + 3.5 × 0.45) = 48.95/(48.95 + 1.575) = 0.969, or approximately 97 percent in the 0° plies that are oriented in the meridional direction — the fibers bear nearly the entire load in their orientation direction, and the matrix yielding would not cause fiber failure because the fiber strain at the matrix yield point (ε_fiber = σ_fiber/E_f = (0.97 × 91 × 10⁶)/(89 × 10⁹) ≈ 0.001, or 0.1 percent) is far below the fiber failure strain of approximately 2.5 to 3.0 percent.

The von Mises stress concentrations at the penetrations — the viewport (300 mm diameter), the hatch (600 mm diameter), and the eight smaller electrical/hydraulic feedthrough penetrations (50 to 100 mm diameter) — are assessed through the finite element submodeling technique, in which a local model of the penetration region with a mesh resolution of approximately 2 mm (capable of resolving the stress gradient within the annular reinforcement doubler region, where the stress varies by a factor of 2 to 3 over a radial distance of approximately 50 mm) is loaded with the displacement boundary conditions extracted from the global shell model at the submodel boundaries (typically located at a distance of 3 to 4 times the penetration diameter from the hole center, where the stress state has recovered to within 10 percent of the nominal membrane stress and where the boundary-condition transfer from the global to the local model introduces minimal error). The submodel results show peak von Mises stresses of 38 to 52 MPa at the viewport opening edge (mitigated by a reinforcement doubler that increases the local thickness from 40 mm to 95 mm over a 150 mm wide annular ring, reducing the stress concentration factor from approximately 3.2 to approximately 1.4, such that the peak stress is only 40 percent above the nominal membrane stress rather than 220 percent), 31 to 44 MPa at the hatch opening (mitigated by a 65 mm thick reinforcement doubler), and 22 to 28 MPa at the feedthrough penetrations (mitigated by individual 15 mm thick reinforcement pads bonded to the inner hull surface and post-cured under vacuum at 80°C for 8 hours to achieve full consolidation of the pad-to-hull interface), with all peak stresses remaining below 35 percent of the matrix yield strength and below 20 percent of the fiber-direction strength, indicating that the reinforcement doubler designs are adequate and that no material yielding or fiber failure will occur at the penetrations under the maximum design pressure, including the additional 0.1 MPa dynamic pressure from wave passage at the 45-meter depth — a margin that the stress analysis confirms to be robust even when accounting for the ±10 percent uncertainty in the laminate elastic properties arising from the inherent variability of the AFP fabrication process (variations in fiber volume fraction of ±2 percent, tow alignment of ±1 degree, and void content of ±0.5 percent).

## 5. LONG-TERM DEGRADATION AND THE CREEP BUCKLING INTERACTION

The viscoelastic nature of the Elium thermoplastic matrix introduces a time-dependent component to the structural response that must be considered for a pressure vessel with a 50-year design life under continuous external pressure — a loading condition that, for a metallic pressure vessel, would be addressed through the time-independent yield and buckling criteria alone, but that for the polymer-matrix composite requires the additional consideration of creep deformation (the progressive increase in strain under constant load, driven by the molecular-scale relaxation processes in the polymer — chain segment rotation, disentanglement, and the breaking and re-forming of secondary bonds — that occur at rates determined by the temperature relative to the glass transition temperature T_g and by the stress level relative to the material's short-term strength), which can interact with the buckling behavior in two ways: first, creep increases the shell's radial displacement over time, effectively increasing the geometric imperfection amplitude and thereby reducing the buckling pressure according to the knockdown factor relationship (which is a function of Δ/t, so that a doubling of the imperfection amplitude from Δ/t = 0.05 to 0.10 would reduce the knockdown factor from 0.51 to approximately 0.42, reducing the design buckling pressure from 1.67 MPa to approximately 1.37 MPa, which would still provide a safety factor of approximately 2.5 at the design depth — marginally acceptable but motivating the conservative approach of maintaining the initial imperfection amplitude below the 5 percent threshold through quality control during fabrication, as described in PART-044, to ensure that even after 50 years of creep, the effective imperfection amplitude remains below approximately 0.08 to 0.10 times the thickness, which would correspond to a creep strain of approximately 0.5 to 1.0 percent in the matrix at the sustained stress level of 10 to 15 MPa, a strain level that is well within the creep strain capacity of the Elium resin at 10°C based on the creep compliance master curve generated through time-temperature superposition of DMA data at temperatures from 30 to 80°C and shifted to the 10°C reference temperature using the Williams-Landel-Ferry (WLF) equation with constants C₁ = 17.4 and C₂ = 51.6 K, which predicts that the creep compliance at 50 years (1.58 × 10⁹ seconds) at 10°C is approximately 1.5 to 2.0 times the 1-hour compliance at the same temperature, corresponding to a long-term creep modulus of approximately 2.3 to 2.8 GPa (reduced from the short-term matrix modulus of approximately 3.5 GPa at 10°C), which when propagated through the laminate's micromechanical model (the rule of mixtures for the fiber-direction modulus and the Halpin-Tsai equations for the transverse and shear moduli, with the reduced matrix modulus substituted into the equations) yields a long-term laminate modulus reduction of only approximately 3 to 5 percent (from 42 GPa to approximately 40 GPa), because the fiber-dominated moduli — which account for 85 to 90 percent of the laminate stiffness in the 0° direction and approximately 70 percent in the ±45° direction — are essentially unaffected by the matrix creep, the fibers themselves being elastic (basalt fibers exhibit negligible creep at stresses below 40 to 50 percent of their tensile strength, and the fiber stress under the Atlantis design conditions is approximately 10 to 15 percent of the fiber strength, well below the creep threshold). The second creep-buckling interaction is the possibility of "creep buckling" — a time-dependent instability in which the shell, although stable against instantaneous buckling at the applied load, gradually deforms under the sustained load until the increasing geometric nonlinearity reduces the tangent stiffness to zero, at which point the shell snaps through without any increase in load — a phenomenon that is well-documented for metallic shells at elevated temperatures (where thermal activation accelerates the creep rate) but that for the Atlantis hull at 10°C is not a concern because the matrix creep rate is extremely slow at this temperature (the WLF shift factor from the T_g = 120°C to 10°C is approximately log₁₀(a_T) = -17.4 × (10 - 120)/(51.6 + 10 - 120) = -17.4 × (-110)/(-58.4) ≈ -32.8, corresponding to a shift factor of approximately 10⁻³³, meaning that the effective time at 10°C is 10³³ times slower than at T_g — a staggering factor that renders creep buckling irrelevant on human timescales, with the creep strain accumulated over 50 years being equivalent to the strain accumulated in approximately 1.6 × 10⁻²⁴ seconds at T_g, i.e., effectively zero). The creep-buckling analysis therefore confirms that the 50-year design life is not compromised by time-dependent deformation, and the buckling safety factor of 3.0 to 3.7 at the design pressure is maintained for the full service life of the Atlantis pod.

The complete structural analysis of the Atlantis prolate spheroidal pressure hull demonstrates that the 3.15:1 aspect ratio, 40 mm nominal LBFRP-001 laminate thickness, and the localized 50 mm polar reinforcement and penetration doubler designs are adequate to resist the combined external pressure, dynamic wave loads, boulder impact loads, and anchoring system reaction forces for a 50-year design life at the 45-meter Monterey deployment depth with a buckling safety factor of 3.0 to 3.7 and von Mises stress utilization ratios below 60 percent at all locations under the maximum design loading conditions, confirming the structural viability of the Atlantis architecture as a manned subsea habitation module.
