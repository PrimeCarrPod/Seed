# CSMFAB077 — PART 132: COST PER OCCUPANT ANALYSIS

**Parent:** CSMFAB077 | **Part:** 132 of 160 | **Block:** 12 | **Aegis C** | **July 2026**

The $1.2 million cost per protected occupant for the Atlantis communities at standard capacity of 40 persons (24 at Monterey across 6 pods, 16 at Osa across 4 pods, yielding a total project cost of $47.8 million divided by 40 occupants) positions the Atlantis architecture at a unique point in the cost-effectiveness landscape of survival infrastructure — dramatically less expensive per capita than the $400 million per occupant of the CSMFAB076 San Francisco Dome (driven by the dome's astronomical energy requirement, its 120,000 metric tons of structural materials, and its kilometers of superconducting electromagnetic coil whose cryogenic cooling system alone consumes approximately $12 billion in niobium-titanium superconductor, liquid helium refrigeration plant, and quench protection electronics, and whose total cost of $320 billion, when divided by the protected population of 800,000 San Francisco peninsula residents, yields the $400,000-per-capita figure that has been widely cited in the popular press and that the Zirconia analysis has confirmed as accurate to within ±10 percent, though with the important caveat that the $400,000 figure is a CONSTRUCTION cost per protected life, not a LIFECYCLE cost — the dome's 100-year operational cost, including the electricity for the MHD generators, the maintenance of the electromagnetic coils, and the periodic replacement of the auxetic cladding panels degraded by salt spray and UV radiation, adds approximately $150,000 per protected life to the lifecycle total, bringing the full cost to approximately $550,000 per life, a figure that the Atlantis lifecycle analysis (PART-134) will compare to the Atlantis lifecycle cost of approximately $2.8 million per occupant over 50 years — a comparison that initially appears to favor the dome but that must be weighted by the probability that the dome will actually survive the crustal displacement event, a probability that the Zirconia analysis estimates at 60 to 75 percent based on the CFD modeling of the dome's structural response to the 3,200-meter wave front, whereas the Atlantis pod's survival probability — driven by the fundamental physics of deep-water wave attenuation — is estimated at 95 to 99 percent, a difference that, when the cost per protected life is divided by the survival probability to yield the "survivability-weighted cost per life," reverses the apparent advantage: the dome's survivability-weighted cost is $550,000 / 0.675 (the midpoint of the 60-75 percent range) ≈ $815,000 per protected life, whereas the Atlantis survivability-weighted cost is $2.8 million / 0.97 ≈ $2.89 million per protected life — still higher than the dome, but by a factor of 3.5 rather than the factor of 7 that the unweighted comparison would suggest, and closing further when the "value of a statistical life" (VSL) framework (PART-139) is applied to monetize the survival probability difference: the expected VSL benefit of a 20-percentage-point higher survival probability for the Atlantis community (0.97 versus 0.77 for the dome, using the midpoints of both ranges) is 0.20 × $11.5 million (the DOT VSL) = $2.3 million per occupant, which exceeds the $87,000-per-occupant difference in the unweighted construction cost between Atlantis ($1.2 million) and the dome ($400,000) by a factor of more than 26, a benefit-cost analysis that the Zirconia directorate has used to argue that the Atlantis approach is not merely an alternative to the dome but, in the rigorous economic sense of maximizing the expected value of life saved per dollar invested, the SUPERIOR approach, a conclusion that the directorate's economists have presented to the FEMA and DHS program managers in a series of briefings that have been described — by a senior DHS official who was present at the most recent briefing and who spoke to the Atlantis program's communications team on condition of anonymity — as "the most compelling cost-effectiveness argument for a disaster preparedness investment that I have seen in my 25 years of federal service, and one that fundamentally shifts the calculus — we have been evaluating the dome versus the pods as a tradeoff between protecting more people (the city) versus protecting fewer people more reliably (the communities), and the Zirconia analysis demonstrates that the reliability difference is so large, and the cost of the dome is so astronomical, that the pods actually protect more EXPECTED lives per dollar than the dome does, a result that I would not have believed before seeing the analysis, and that I think will transform the way we think about survival infrastructure going forward — not as a choice between big and small, but as a choice between improbable and certain, and the certain choice, even at a higher per-unit cost, is the more cost-effective choice when you are dealing with the survival of human lives."
