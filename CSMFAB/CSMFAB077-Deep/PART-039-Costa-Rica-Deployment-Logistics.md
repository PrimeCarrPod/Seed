# CSMFAB077-DEEP-039 — COSTA RICA DEPLOYMENT LOGISTICS: PORT INFRASTRUCTURE, CHARLEMAGNE FLEET ROUTING, CUSTOMS AND REGULATORY FRAMEWORK

**Document ID:** CSMFAB077-DEEP-039 | **Parent:** CSMFAB000000000077 | **Version:** 2.0 | **Part:** 039 of 160 — Block 4

The deployment of the Atlantis communities in Costa Rica depends on the logistical infrastructure that the Costa Rican government has agreed to provide, through the Memorandum of Understanding between the Atlantis Program and the Government of Costa Rica (MOU-ATL-CR-2026-001), signed in San José in January 2026 by the Minister of Environment and Energy and by the Aegis C Program Director, a MOU that stipulates the use of the port facilities at Caldera (on the Pacific coast, approximately 80 kilometers west of San José, the capital, and the primary commercial port of Costa Rica, handling approximately 12 million metric tons of cargo per year, primarily the containerized imports — the consumer goods, the machinery, the construction materials — that supply the Costa Rican economy, a port that the Atlantis logistics team has surveyed and has determined is adequate for the receiving and the staging of the Atlantis pod components, which will be shipped from the Mojave fabrication facility to Caldera via the Charlemagne fleet's heavy-lift vessel, a transit that, as described in PART-030, takes approximately 12 days and that passes through the Panama Canal, requiring the canal transit reservation that the Atlantis logistics team has secured, a reservation that is contingent on the payment of the canal transit fee — approximately $150,000 for the Charlemagne vessel, based on its gross tonnage and its cargo — and on the vessel's compliance with the canal's safety and environmental regulations, a compliance that the Charlemagne fleet's operations manual guarantees and that the Panama Canal Authority's inspection — which the vessel will undergo, at the canal's Cristobal anchorage, before the transit — will verify), and the use of the overland transport route from Caldera to the deployment staging areas — the route that follows the Inter-American Highway (Carretera Interamericana, Route 1) south from Caldera to the town of Palmar Norte, and then the Route 34 (the Costanera Sur) south from Palmar Norte to the Osa Peninsula and the Golfo Dulce, a distance of approximately 250 kilometers, much of which is a two-lane road that traverses the mountainous terrain of the Talamanca Range, a terrain that the Atlantis logistics team has surveyed, using the LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging) topographic data that the Costa Rican Instituto Geográfico Nacional has collected, and that has identified three bridges, two tunnels, and seven hairpin turns that are the critical points of the route — the points where the size and the weight of the Atlantis pod (15 meters long, 5 meters in diameter, approximately 22 metric tons) could exceed the road's capacity, a capacity that the Atlantis structural engineering team has assessed, through the finite element analysis of the bridges and the tunnels, using the structural drawings that the Costa Rican Ministry of Public Works and Transport (MOPT) has provided, an analysis that has confirmed that the route, with the exception of one bridge over the Río Térraba (a steel-truss bridge, built in 1975, with a design load of 30 metric tons — adequate for the pod's 22-metric-ton transport trailer, but with a factor of safety that is lower than the Atlantis standard of 2.0, a deficiency that the Atlantis logistics team has addressed by designing a temporary bridge reinforcement — a steel I-beam that is bolted to the underside of the bridge deck, increasing its load capacity to 45 metric tons, a reinforcement that the MOPT has approved and that the Atlantis deployment team will install, using a mobile crane that is part of the overland transport convoy, approximately 48 hours before the pod's passage, and that will be removed, restoring the bridge to its original condition, within 48 hours after the passage, a temporary intervention that the MOPT permit authorizes and that the Atlantis environmental team has assessed as having no permanent impact on the river or its ecosystem, a impact that the SETENA environmental monitoring — the water-quality sampling, the riverbed surveys, the fish-population assessments — will verify).

The regulatory framework for the Costa Rica deployment, as described in PART-021 and PART-028, is governed by the SETENA Viabilidad Ambiental (VA-2026-077), a comprehensive environmental permit that incorporates, by reference, the Atlantis Environmental Impact Study (EIA-OSA-2026), the Corcovado National Park Management Plan (the plan that governs all activities within the park's boundaries and that the Atlantis program has committed to follow, even though the deployment site is outside the park's boundary — it is in the Golfo Dulce Forest Reserve, a buffer zone around the park that permits the controlled use of the natural resources, a use that the Atlantis deployment represents — and the park's management plan requires that any activity in the buffer zone be compatible with the park's conservation objectives, a compatibility that the Atlantis environmental team has demonstrated, through the ecological analysis that is part of the EIA), and the Costa Rican Maritime Authority's (MOPT-Dirección de Navegación y Seguridad) navigation safety permit, a permit that establishes the exclusion zone around the Atlantis pod — a zone with a radius of 500 meters, within which the navigation of all vessels, except the Atlantis support vessels, is prohibited, a prohibition that the Coast Guard (Servicio Nacional de Guardacostas) will enforce, through the patrol boat that is stationed at the Golfito Coast Guard base and that will include the Atlantis exclusion zone in its regular patrol route, a enforcement that the Atlantis program has requested and that the MOPT has agreed to provide, as part of the Memorandum of Understanding, a collaboration between the Atlantis program and the Costa Rican authorities that this PART-039 documents and that is essential for the security and the success of the Costa Rica deployment.
