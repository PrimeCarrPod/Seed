## 1.0 THE NAVIER-STOKES EQUATIONS AS THE IRREDUCIBLE FOUNDATION OF MEGATSUNAMI HYDRODYNAMICS

The Navier-Stokes equations, named for the French engineer and physicist Claude-Louis Navier — who, in 1822, first formulated the equations of motion for a viscous fluid by augmenting Euler's inviscid equations with a term proportional to the second spatial derivative of the velocity, representing the internal friction that arises from the molecular momentum transport between adjacent fluid layers moving at different velocities — and for the Irish mathematician and physicist Sir George Gabriel Stokes — who, in a series of papers published between 1845 and 1851, provided the rigorous continuum-mechanical derivation of the equations from the principles of conservation of mass, conservation of linear momentum (Newton's second law applied to a fluid element), and a constitutive relation for the stress tensor that assumes the fluid to be Newtonian, meaning that the deviatoric stress (the part of the stress that is not isotropic — not equal in all directions, unlike the thermodynamic pressure) is linearly proportional to the rate-of-strain tensor with a proportionality constant that is the dynamic viscosity μ, a material property that, for seawater at the temperatures and salinities characteristic of the Atlantis deployment sites (10 to 15 degrees Celsius, 33 to 35 practical salinity units), has a value of approximately 1.08 × 10⁻³ pascal-seconds — constitute the complete, mathematically closed, and physically exact (within the continuum approximation, which treats the fluid as a continuous medium rather than as a collection of discrete molecules, an approximation that is valid for length scales larger than approximately 10⁻⁸ meters, far smaller than any length scale relevant to megatsunami hydrodynamics) description of the motion of any Newtonian fluid, including the seawater of the global ocean and the sediment-laden turbidite flows that accompany the recession phase of a crustal displacement event, provided that the flow remains in the laminar or transitional regime — the turbulent regime, characterized by chaotic, three-dimensional, multiscale velocity fluctuations that arise when the Reynolds number (the ratio of inertial forces to viscous forces, defined as Re = ρUL/μ where U is a characteristic velocity and L is a characteristic length scale, with values for the megatsunami at the Atlantis sites being on the order of 10⁷ to 10⁹, firmly in the turbulent regime) exceeds a critical value of approximately 2,000 to 4,000 for pipe flow or approximately 5 × 10⁵ for boundary-layer flow over a flat plate, requires that the equations be supplemented by a turbulence model — a set of additional equations that approximate the effect of the unresolved turbulent fluctuations on the resolved (time-averaged or spatially filtered) flow, an approximation that is necessary because the direct numerical simulation (DNS) of turbulence — solving the Navier-Stokes equations on a computational grid fine enough to resolve all turbulent eddies down to the Kolmogorov length scale, the smallest scale of turbulent motion, at which the kinetic energy of the turbulence is dissipated into heat by molecular viscosity, a scale that is related to the energy-containing (large) scales of the flow by η/L ≈ Re⁻³/⁴, so that for a flow with Re = 10⁸, the ratio of the smallest to the largest scales is approximately 10⁻⁶, requiring a computational grid with approximately (10⁶)³ = 10¹⁸ grid points to resolve the turbulence in three dimensions, a number that exceeds the capabilities of any existing or foreseeable computer by many orders of magnitude, making DNS impossible for any practical megatsunami simulation and necessitating the use of turbulence models that the Atlantis CFD team has carefully validated against the physical model tests at the Mojave facility, where the Reynolds number — though smaller than the full-scale Re by a factor of approximately 1/50 (for the 1:50 scale model) — is still sufficiently high (Re_model ≈ 10⁶) to ensure that the flow is turbulent and that the turbulence model's predictions at model scale, which can be compared with the measured velocities and pressures, are representative of the model's performance at full scale, where direct validation is, of course, impossible because no full-scale megatsunami has been observed — and that the turbulence modeling uncertainty, which the Atlantis design team has quantified through a sensitivity study that varied the turbulence model parameters (specifically, the turbulent Schmidt number, which controls the turbulent diffusion of sediment in the turbidite flow, and the wall-function roughness parameter, which controls the velocity profile in the near-wall region of the CFD model) over ranges that span the values recommended in the literature and that observed a variation of ±15 to ±25 percent in the predicted dynamic pressure at the pod's hull, an uncertainty that is well within the safety margins discussed in PART-002 and PART-003, and that is therefore acceptable for the Atlantis design.

The Navier-Stokes equations, expressed in their most general form — the conservation form that is preferred for computational fluid dynamics because it ensures that the numerical solution conserves mass and momentum to within the round-off error of the computer, a property that is essential for the accurate prediction of the wave forces on the Atlantis pod, which depend on the net momentum flux integrated over the pod's surface, and that would be compromised if the numerical scheme did not conserve momentum exactly, leading to spurious sources or sinks of momentum that could distort the force predictions — consist of the continuity equation (mass conservation) and the three components of the momentum equation (one for each spatial direction), written in tensor notation (which uses indices to represent the components of vectors and tensors, simplifying the notation and making the equations easier to manipulate algebraically, at the cost of requiring the reader to be familiar with the Einstein summation convention, in which repeated indices are summed over, a convention that is standard in fluid dynamics and continuum mechanics and that the Atlantis design team's hydrodynamicists, all of whom hold doctoral degrees in fluid mechanics or ocean engineering from institutions with accredited programs in these disciplines, are thoroughly familiar with) as follows:

∂ρ/∂t + ∂(ρu_j)/∂x_j = 0

∂(ρu_i)/∂t + ∂(ρu_i u_j)/∂x_j = -∂p/∂x_i + ∂τ_ij/∂x_j + ρg_i + F_i

where ρ is the fluid density (a function of temperature, salinity, and — for the turbidite flows — sediment concentration, with the sediment-laden water being treated as a single-phase fluid with an effective density that depends on the local sediment volume fraction φ according to ρ_effective = (1 - φ)ρ_water + φρ_sediment, an approximation that is valid when the sediment particles are small enough — less than approximately 1 millimeter — that they are effectively in dynamic equilibrium with the surrounding water, moving at the same velocity and experiencing the same pressure, a condition known as the "mixture approximation" that is standard in sediment transport modeling and that has been validated for the particle sizes that characterize the turbidite flows at the Atlantis sites, where the suspended sediment consists primarily of silt and fine sand with grain diameters of 10 to 200 micrometers, for which the particle relaxation time — the time required for a particle to adjust to a change in the surrounding fluid velocity — is on the order of 10⁻³ to 10⁻² seconds, much shorter than the timescales of the turbidite flow, which are on the order of 10² to 10³ seconds, confirming that the mixture approximation is appropriate, and where the larger particles — gravel, cobbles, and boulders — that are transported as bedload rather than suspended load are not modeled by the continuum equations but are instead tracked individually using the Lagrangian particle tracking method that the Atlantis CFD team has implemented in the OpenFOAM simulations, a method that computes the trajectory of each particle by integrating Newton's second law with the forces on the particle — gravity, buoyancy, drag, added mass (the force required to accelerate the fluid that is displaced by the particle's motion), and the pressure gradient force — being determined from the local flow conditions that the Eulerian (continuum) solution provides, a coupled Eulerian-Lagrangian approach that is computationally expensive but that is the only feasible method for predicting the boulder impact hazard that the Atlantis pod's turbidite defense architecture must address, and that the Atlantis design team has validated through the physical model tests at Mojave, where instrumented impact targets — aluminum plates instrumented with strain gauges and accelerometers, designed to measure the impact force and the impact location of model boulders (glass spheres with diameters of 5 to 15 millimeters, representing prototype boulders with diameters of 0.25 to 0.75 meters at the 1:50 scale) — were placed in the path of the sediment-laden flow generated by the test facility's recirculating flume, and where the measured impact forces — corrected for scale effects using the Froude similarity criterion, which requires that the ratio of inertial to gravitational forces, expressed by the Froude number Fr = U²/(gL), be the same at model and prototype scales, a criterion that ensures that the flow patterns — including the trajectory of the boulders and the impact dynamics — are geometrically similar at the two scales, a similarity that is imperfect because the Reynolds number — which controls the viscous forces, including the drag on the boulders — is not the same at the two scales (the model Reynolds number is lower than the prototype Reynolds number by a factor of approximately 50^(3/2) ≈ 350, a discrepancy that is unavoidable because it is impossible to satisfy both Froude and Reynolds similarity simultaneously with the same fluid, water, at both scales — the only way to satisfy both would be to use a different fluid at model scale, with a viscosity that is 350 times smaller than water, which is not practical — and the error introduced by this "scale effect" has been estimated, through a combination of CFD simulations at both scales and through the application of semi-empirical drag-coefficient corrections derived from the extensive literature on particle-laden flows, to be less than 10 percent for the impact forces, an uncertainty that the Atlantis design accommodates by applying a scale-effect correction factor of 1.10 to the predicted boulder impact energies, a factor that is incorporated into the auxetic cladding's design thickness and that contributes a fraction of a percent — literally, a few hundred dollars per pod — to the total pod fabrication cost, an expense that the ZIRCONIA analysis has determined is justified by the increased confidence in the cladding's ability to protect the pressure hull from boulder impacts, a The momentum equation, when written in the full form that the Atlantis CFD simulations solve, includes terms that represent the various physical processes that affect the megatsunami flow: the unsteady term ∂(ρu_i)/∂t, which accounts for the time-dependence of the flow — essential for wave simulations, which are inherently unsteady, with the flow evolving over timescales of seconds to days as the wave field generated by the crustal displacement propagates across the ocean and interacts with the continents; the convective term ∂(ρu_i u_j)/∂x_j, which represents the transport of momentum by the flow itself — the nonlinear term that is responsible for the wave steepening and the eventual wave breaking that occurs when the wave height becomes comparable to the water depth, transferring wave energy from the organized wave motion to the disorganized turbulent motion that dissipates the energy as heat, a process that the CFD simulations must capture accurately because the amount of wave energy that is dissipated before reaching the Atlantis pod determines the wave's height and orbital velocity at the pod's location, a determination that, as PART-002 emphasized, is critical for the pod's survival — too much dissipation and the wave is smaller than predicted (conservative, safe), too little dissipation and the wave is larger than predicted (non-conservative, potentially unsafe), and the Atlantis design team has chosen the turbulence model parameters to produce the higher-energy (less dissipation) case within the range of uncertainty, ensuring that the predicted wave loads are conservative upper bounds rather than best estimates, a choice that increases the pod's cost but that ensures that the pod will survive even if the actual wave is at the upper end of the
