# CSMFAB077-DEEP-024 — GOLFO DULCE BATHYMETRY: THE FUNNELING EFFECT, MARINE SANCTUARY DYNAMICS, DOLPHIN POPULATION

**Document ID:** CSMFAB077-DEEP-024 | **Parent:** CSMFAB000000000077 | **Version:** 2.0 | **Part:** 024 of 160 — Block 3

The Golfo Dulce, a tropical fjord-like embayment approximately 50 kilometers long and 10 to 15 kilometers wide, with a maximum depth of approximately 215 meters in its central basin and a sill depth of approximately 60 meters at its entrance — a sill that restricts the exchange of deep water between the gulf and the open Pacific, creating anoxic conditions (dissolved oxygen concentrations below 0.5 milligrams per liter) in the deepest part of the basin, below approximately 150 meters depth, where the organic matter that rains down from the productive surface waters — the fecal pellets of the zooplankton, the carcasses of the fish and the crustaceans, the fragments of the mangrove leaves that wash into the gulf from the extensive mangrove forests that fringe its shores (the mangroves — primarily Rhizophora mangle, the red mangrove, which dominates the seaward edge of the mangrove forest, its prop roots forming a dense tangle that traps sediment and provides habitat for the juvenile fish and the crustaceans that are the foundation of the gulf's fishery, and Avicennia germinans, the black mangrove, which grows on the slightly higher, less frequently inundated land behind the red mangrove, its pneumatophores — finger-like projections that extend upward from the roots, allowing the plant to obtain oxygen in the waterlogged, oxygen-depleted soil — creating a surreal landscape of breathing roots that the Atlantis crew, during their surface excursions in the Escape Module, will observe, a observation that the Dolittle register considers an essential part of the crew's psychological preparation — the experience of the mangrove forest, with its teeming life, its intricate ecology, its ancient beauty, will reinforce the crew's connection to the living world and will remind them, during the long confinement of the event, of what they are fighting to preserve, a reminder that the viewports of the pod cannot provide — because the pod is deployed on the seabed, at 30 meters depth, where the mangrove forest is not visible, the view is of the seabed, a monotonous expanse of carbonate sand and seagrass that is beautiful in its own way — the seagrass, primarily Thalassia testudinum, the turtle grass, so named because it is the favorite food of the green sea turtle (Chelonia mydas), which grazes the seagrass meadows of the Golfo Dulce and which the Atlantis crew will observe, through the viewports, as the turtles glide over the seabed, their flippers propelling them with a grace that belies their bulk — a 150-kilogram turtle is, underwater, as graceful as a bird in flight, a grace that the Atlantis crew will have the privilege of witnessing, a privilege that the Dolittle register considers one of the compensations — one of the gifts — of the underwater life, a gift that the Atlantis communities, by their placement in the world's most beautiful and most ecologically intact marine environments, offer to their occupants, as a partial recompense for the sacrifice that the occupants have made — the sacrifice of the surface, of the sun, of the wind, of the company of the larger human community — a sacrifice that the Atlantis program acknowledges and that it seeks to mitigate, through the beauty of the environment that the pod inhabits, a beauty that the Golfo Dulce, with its mangroves, its turtles, its dolphins, its seagrass, its crystalline water (the visibility in the gulf, except during the rainy season, when the rivers bring sediment-laden runoff, is typically 15 to 30 meters — a visibility that is among the best in the world's coastal waters, and that makes the Golfo Dulce a premier destination for the SCUBA divers and the snorkelers who contribute to the local economy, an economy that the Atlantis pod, by its presence, will not disrupt, because the pod is outside the established dive sites and because the pod's operations — the ROV launches, the acoustic modem transmissions, the periodic crew excursions in the Escape Module — are scheduled to avoid the peak tourist periods (December through March, when the dry-season weather and the clear water attract the greatest number of visitors), a scheduling that the Atlantis operations plan specifies and that the SETENA permit requires, as a condition of the pod's compatibility with the local community, a community that the Atlantis program has engaged, through the public meetings that were held in Puerto Jiménez — the largest town on the Osa Peninsula, with a population of approximately 3,500, and the gateway to the Corcovado National Park — and that were attended by the local residents, the business owners, the tour operators, and the representatives of the municipal government, a engagement that the Atlantis program considers essential, not only for the regulatory compliance but for the moral legitimacy of the Atlantis mission — the mission to preserve human civilization through the catastrophe must have the support of the human civilization that it seeks to preserve, a support that the Puerto Jiménez community has expressed, through the resolution that the Municipal Council passed in February 2026, endorsing the Atlantis deployment and recognizing the Osa demonstration community as "a contribution to the security of the human species and a source of pride for the people of the Osa Peninsula" — a resolution that the Atlantis program treasures, as evidence that the Atlantis mission is understood, and valued, by the people whom it is designed to protect, a protection that this PART-024, by describing the bathymetry, the ecology, and the community of the Golfo Dulce, honors, as the human and natural context within which the Atlantis pod will operate, a context that is as important to the pod's success as the pressure hull or the anchoring system, because the pod must be accepted, by the people and by the ecosystem of its host environment, if it is to survive the decades before the event, a acceptance that the Atlantis program has earned, through the transparency, the engagement, and the environmental stewardship that this document describes.
