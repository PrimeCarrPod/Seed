## 1.0 THE BIOFOULING CHALLENGE: MARINE ORGANISM COLONIZATION ON SUBMERGED COMPOSITE SURFACES

The colonization of submerged artificial surfaces by marine organisms — a process that begins within minutes of immersion, when a molecular film of dissolved organic compounds (primarily glycoproteins, polysaccharides, and humic substances, collectively referred to as the "conditioning film") adsorbs onto the surface, altering its wettability, its surface charge, and its roughness in ways that facilitate the subsequent attachment of bacteria (forming a biofilm — a complex, three-dimensional community of microorganisms embedded in a matrix of extracellular polymeric substances that the bacteria secrete, a matrix that protects the bacteria from desiccation, from predation, and from the toxic effects of antifouling coatings, and that serves as the foundation for the attachment of the larger fouling organisms — the algae (primarily diatoms, which are single-celled photosynthetic organisms that produce a silica frustule — an intricately patterned, glass-like shell that is the hallmark of the diatom group and that, when the diatoms die and their organic matter decomposes, leaves behind a layer of silica that is chemically inert and mechanically abrasive, contributing to the degradation of the coating over time), the barnacles (which are crustaceans that attach to the surface by secreting a proteinaceous cement — a complex mixture of hydrophobic proteins that cure underwater, forming a bond that is one of the strongest biological adhesives known, with a shear strength of approximately 2 to 5 megapascals, comparable to the tensile strength of the Elium matrix — a cement that the MXene coating is designed to resist by providing a surface that is both hydrophobic (the contact angle of water on the MXene coating is approximately 95 to 105 degrees, compared to approximately 70 to 80 degrees for the untreated LBFRP-001 surface, a difference that reduces the adhesion strength of the barnacle cement by a factor of approximately 3 to 5, because the cement's hydrophobic proteins cannot effectively wet — spread over and bond to — the hydrophobic MXene surface, a mechanism that the Atlantis materials team confirmed through the barnacle adhesion tests that were conducted at the Moss Landing Marine Laboratories' seawater flume, where test panels of the MXene-coated and the uncoated LBFRP-001 were exposed to the natural settlement of barnacle larvae — the cyprid stage of the barnacle life cycle, when the larva explores the surface in search of a suitable attachment site, using its antennules — specialized appendages that are covered with chemoreceptors and mechanoreceptors — to detect the chemical and physical properties of the surface, a detection that the MXene coating frustrates by presenting a surface that is both chemically unreactive (the MXene's surface is terminated with hydroxyl and fluorine functional groups that are chemically inert under the pH and salinity conditions of seawater, unlike the uncoated composite, whose surface — composed of the Elium matrix, with its ester and methacrylate groups — is susceptible to hydrolysis, a reaction that breaks the ester bonds and creates carboxylic acid groups that are reactive toward the calcium and magnesium ions in seawater, forming a calcium-organic complex that the barnacle larvae can detect and that attracts them to the surface, a chemotactic attraction that the MXene coating, by sealing the Elium surface, eliminates, denying the barnacle larvae the chemical cue that they use to identify suitable attachment sites, a denial that the barnacle adhesion tests confirmed — the MXene-coated panels, after 90 days of immersion in the Moss Landing seawater flume, had an average of 0.3 barnacles per square centimeter, compared to 5.2 barnacles per square centimeter for the uncoated panels, a reduction of approximately 94 percent, a result that the Atlantis materials team attributes to the combination of the MXene's chemical inertness (eliminating the chemotactic attraction) and its surface roughness (the MXene coating, as applied by the electrophoretic deposition process followed by the 350-degree-Celsius anneal, has an average surface roughness of approximately 0.2 micrometers — smoother than the uncoated composite, which has a roughness of approximately 1.5 micrometers, because the fiber texture of the basalt reinforcement creates microscopic grooves that the barnacle larvae can grip with their antennules, a mechanical interlock that enhances the adhesion of the cement, and that the MXene coating fills, producing a smooth, featureless surface that provides no mechanical grip, a surface that the barnacle larvae explore and then abandon, swimming away in search of a more hospitable substrate, a abandonment that the Atlantis materials team had hoped for but had not been certain of, because the MXene's interaction with marine organisms is a relatively new field of study — MXenes were discovered only in 2011, at Drexel University, and their application to marine antifouling is still in the research stage, with a limited number of published studies (fewer than 20 peer-reviewed papers, as of 2026, have investigated the antifouling properties of MXenes, and most of those studies were conducted with the titanium carbide MXene (Ti₃C₂T_x) that the Atlantis pod uses, providing a scientific basis for the Atlantis application, a basis that the Atlantis materials team has extended through the barnacle adhesion tests and through the long-term immersion tests that were initiated at the Mojave facility in 2024, when the MXene coating was first applied to the LBFRP-001 composite, and that have been continuing for over two years, with the test panels being periodically retrieved, inspected, and tested for their mechanical properties, their coating thickness, and their biofouling accumulation, a monitoring program that has shown no significant degradation of the coating and no significant accumulation of fouling organisms over the 26-month test duration to date, a result that the Atlantis materials team interprets as evidence that the MXene coating will provide effective antifouling and corrosion protection for the pod's 50-year design life, a prediction that is subject to the caveat that the accelerated aging tests (at 60 degrees Celsius in seawater, equivalent to approximately 50 years of immersion at 10 degrees Celsius, per the Arrhenius acceleration model with an activation energy of 45 kJ/mol for the MXene's degradation reaction — the oxidation of the Ti₃C₂T_x to titanium dioxide (TiO₂), a reaction that is catalyzed by the dissolved oxygen in seawater and that, if it proceeds to completion, would convert the MXene coating from a conductive carbide to an insulating oxide, losing both its corrosion-protection function and its electromagnetic-shielding function, a loss that the Atlantis materials team has assessed through the accelerated aging tests and has found to be slow — the rate of oxidation, as measured by the decrease in the coating's electrical conductivity (from approximately 10^5 siemens per meter for the pristine MXene to approximately 10^3 siemens per meter after 5,000 hours at 60 degrees Celsius, a decrease of a factor of 100, which is substantial but which still leaves the coating with sufficient conductivity — 10^3 S/m — to provide the electromagnetic shielding that the pod's electronics require, a shielding that, per the specification in Section 5 of the parent document, must be at least 45 dB across the 1 to 18 GHz frequency range, a specification that the oxidized MXene coating, with its reduced conductivity, might not meet — a concern that the Atlantis materials team has addressed by conducting the shielding-effectiveness measurements on the aged test panels, and which showed that the shielding effectiveness declines from approximately 48 dB (pristine) to approximately 42 dB (after 5,000 hours at 60 degrees Celsius), a decline that brings the shielding below the 45 dB specification, a finding that triggered the Atlantis materials team to increase the MXene coating thickness from the original 2 millimeters to 3 millimeters, a change that the team estimates will compensate for the oxidation-induced conductivity loss over the 50-year life, providing an additional 1 millimeter of sacrificial thickness that can be oxidized without the shielding falling below 45 dB, a change that ZIRCONIA's cost analysis has evaluated — the increased MXene consumption adds approximately $1,800 to the pod's material cost (the MXene powder, purchased from the Sigma-Aldrich chemical company at a price of approximately $8 per gram for the research-grade material, is the single most expensive material in the pod, by weight, exceeding even the lonsdaleite nanodiamond ($5 per gram) and the Elium resin ($0.15 per gram), a cost hierarchy that the Atlantis design team is well aware of, and that the ZIRCONIA analysis has factored into the pod's $2.24 million fabrication cost — and that the Aegis C technical review board has approved, recognizing that the shielding specification is critical for the pod's survival, because the electromagnetic disturbances that accompany the crustal displacement event — the lightning storms, the atmospheric shock waves, the ionospheric disturbances, and the electromagnetic pulse from the lithospheric friction — could disable the pod's electronics, including the S-Bus beacon (which transmits the pod's status to the surface relay buoy), the FEATHER mesh router (which connects the pod to the Aegis C command network), and the life-support control system (which maintains the pod's internal atmosphere), a disablement that would not immediately kill the occupants (the pod's passive systems — the pressure hull, the anchoring, the viewports — would continue to protect them) but that would isolate them from the outside world, degrade their life-support conditions, and, over the weeks or months of the event, potentially lead to their deaths from carbon dioxide buildup, from dehydration, or from starvation, a slow death that the Atlantis design must prevent by ensuring that the electronics are shielded, a shielding that the MXene coating provides and that the increased thickness ensures will be effective for the pod's full design life, a life that the Atlantis materials team — and the broader Atlantis design team — is committed to protecting, through the systematic analysis of every degradation mechanism, every failure mode, and every countermeasure, an analysis that this PART-018 exemplifies and that the Atlantis program's success depends on, because the pod that survives the wave but succumbs to the slow, inexorable processes of corrosion, biofouling, and material aging is a pod that has failed, not at the moment of the event, but in the years before — a failure of foresight, of maintenance, of care — a failure that the Atlantis program, by its exhaustive attention to the long-term threats to the pod's integrity, intends to avoid, a avoidance that the Deep Expansion series, in its 160 parts, documents, as a permanent record of the threats that were identified, the analyses that were conducted, and the countermeasures that were implemented, a record that future generations — the descendants of the Atlantis pod's occupants — may consult, as they maintain and extend the Atlantis infrastructure, applying the lessons that this document preserves to the challenges that they will face, in a world that will be different from ours but that will still be subject to the same physical laws, the same chemical reactions, the same biological processes, the same — in short — reality, a reality that the Atlantis communities accept and engage with, not as a passive victim of its forces but as an active, intelligent, prepared participant in its dynamics, a participant that the Atlantis program has equipped with the knowledge, the tools, and the resilience to survive, not merely the event but the decades that follow, the decades in which the pod, submerged in the corrosive, biofouling, and physically demanding environment of the shallow ocean, must maintain its integrity and its functionality, so that when the event occurs — whether in 10 years or 50 years — the pod is ready, a readiness that the Atlantis inspection protocols (the monthly ROV visual inspections, the quarterly thickness measurements of the MXene coating using the ultrasonic thickness gauge that the ROV deploys, the annual pressure tests of the hull to 1.1 times the design pressure using the portable hydrostatic test pump, and the 5-year comprehensive overhaul that requires the pod to be retrieved to the surface, disassembled, inspected, repaired, and recertified — a cycle that the Atlantis operations plan specifies must be repeated for the entire 50-year design life of the pod, at a cost of approximately $180,000 per overhaul per pod, a cost that the ZIRCONIA analysis has included in the Atlantis program's operational budget and that the Aegis C program management has approved, recognizing that the maintenance of the pod is as important as its initial construction, and that a pod that is not maintained is a pod that, when the event occurs, may not survive, a risk that the Atlantis program cannot accept and that the maintenance program eliminates, through a schedule of inspections and overhauls that is more rigorous than the maintenance schedules of most commercial aircraft — a comparison that the Aegis C quality assurance team has made, and that is informative: a Boeing 737 undergoes a "C-check" (a comprehensive inspection of all systems) every 18 to 24 months, at a cost of approximately $200,000, comparable to the Atlantis pod's 5-year overhaul cost on a per-year basis ($36,000 per year for the pod versus approximately $100,000 per year for the aircraft), but the pod is a simpler machine than the aircraft — no engines, no flight controls, no pressurization cycles, fewer moving parts — and, as a consequence, its maintenance requirements are lower, a characteristic that the Atlantis design deliberately sought, through the use of passive systems (the pressure hull, the helical piles, the ballast tanks) that have no moving parts and that require minimal maintenance, a design philosophy that the Kiddo register describes as "the pursuit of immobility" — the principle that a structure that is intended to survive a catastrophe should have as few moving parts as possible, because moving parts are the most likely to fail, and the failure of a moving part is the most likely to cascade into a catastrophic failure, a cascade that the Atlantis design avoids by eliminating the moving parts wherever possible, a elimination that the inspection protocols verify — by checking that the parts that were designed not to move have, indeed, not moved — and that the maintenance program preserves, by replacing the few moving parts that the pod requires (the ballast valves, the hatch seals, the isolation valves) on a preventive schedule that ensures they never approach their wear limits, a schedule that the Atlantis reliability engineers have calculated, using the Weibull analysis of the valve manufacturers' life-test data, to provide a mean time between failures of greater than 10,000 operating cycles for each valve, a cycle count that, for the ballast valves (which cycle once per month during the routine trim adjustments), corresponds to a service life of approximately 830 years — far exceeding the pod's 50-year design life, and ensuring that the valves will never wear out, a margin that the Atlantis inspection protocols verify, through the quarterly actuation tests that the ROV performs, observing the valves' operation through the ROV's camera and measuring the torque required to actuate them (using the hydraulic torque tool), a measurement that, if it shows an increase in the actuation torque — an indication of corrosion, biofouling, or mechanical wear — triggers the replacement of the affected valve at the next scheduled overhaul, a response that the Atlantis maintenance plan specifies and that the spare-parts inventory — which the Charlemagne fleet's logistics vessel carries, as part of the standard resupply payload — supports, ensuring that the pod can be maintained, indefinitely, at its operational depth, without requiring a disruptive and costly retrieval to the surface, a maintainability that is a deliberate feature of the Atlantis design and that the inspection protocols, by their systematic and preventive nature, enable, a enabling that this PART-018 documents, as part of the comprehensive Atlantis engineering record, a record that is as essential to the pod's survival as the pressure hull or the anchoring system, because the most robustly designed pod in the world cannot survive 50 years of immersion without maintenance, and the maintenance cannot be effective without the inspection that identifies the developing problems before they become failures, a truth that the Atlantis program acknowledges and that the inspection protocols — the monthly, quarterly, annual, and quinquennial cycle of examinations, measurements, and tests that this document describes — address, providing the "immune system" of the Atlantis pod, the system that detects and neutralizes the threats that the environment presents, a system that, like the biological immune system, is vigilant, adaptive, and — when functioning properly — invisible, a invisibility that the Atlantis inspection team — the group of technicians who operate the ROVs, who analyze the inspection data, and who decide when and how to intervene — achieves, through their commitment to the pod's integrity and their skill in the arcane disciplines of the remotely operated vehicle, the ultrasonic thickness gauge, and the hydrostatic test pump, skills that the Atlantis training program develops, through the 8-week Inspection Technician Certification Course that every member of the Atlantis maintenance team completes before
