# CSMFAB077-DEEP-038 — CENTRAL AMERICAN VOLCANIC ARC: ASH-FALL HAZARD DURING DISPLACEMENT EVENTS, POD FILTRATION REQUIREMENTS

**Document ID:** CSMFAB077-DEEP-038 | **Parent:** CSMFAB000000000077 | **Version:** 2.0 | **Part:** 038 of 160 — Block 4

The Central American volcanic arc, a chain of approximately 40 active and potentially active volcanoes that extends from Guatemala to Panama, poses an additional hazard to the Atlantis communities during a crustal displacement event — not the lava flows, the pyroclastic surges, or the lahars that are the primary volcanic hazards to surface structures (because the pod is underwater and is protected from these hazards by the 30 to 60 meters of water above it, a water column that would quench a lava flow, absorb a pyroclastic surge, and dilute a lahar, rendering these hazards harmless to the submerged pod), but the volcanic ASH — the fine-grained, abrasive, chemically reactive particulate matter that is ejected into the atmosphere by the explosive eruptions that the crustal displacement event would trigger (the displacement would destabilize the magma chambers beneath the volcanoes, by changing the stress field in the crust, a change that could increase or decrease the magma pressure, depending on the orientation of the stress change relative to the magma chamber's shape, and that, in the worst case — a decrease in the confining pressure, which would allow the dissolved gases in the magma to exsolve, expanding the magma and driving an explosive eruption — could produce eruptions that are far larger than the historical eruptions of the Central American volcanoes, eruptions that could inject ash into the stratosphere, where it would be transported by the global atmospheric circulation and would fall out — over weeks to months — as a blanket of ash that covers the ocean surface, the ash particles sinking through the water column and accumulating on the seabed, a accumulation that, if it is thick enough (more than a few centimeters), could clog the Atlantis pod's seawater intakes — the openings in the hull that draw the seawater into the desalination system, the heat exchanger, and the ballast tanks, a clogging that would deprive the pod of the seawater that is essential for its life support and its operational systems, a deprivation that the Atlantis design addresses through the intake filtration system, a system that consists of a series of stainless-steel mesh screens, with progressively finer mesh sizes (5 millimeters, 1 millimeter, 0.1 millimeters, and 0.01 millimeters), arranged in series so that each screen captures the particles that are larger than its mesh size, and the finer screens are protected from the larger particles by the coarser screens, a conventional filtration arrangement that the Atlantis design team has adapted from the seawater intakes of the coastal power plants and desalination facilities, facilities that routinely operate in environments with high suspended-sediment loads and that have proven the effectiveness of the staged-screening approach, an effectiveness that the Atlantis testing program has validated, by exposing the intake filtration system to the simulated ash-fall conditions — the Mojave test tank was seeded with volcanic ash from the 1963 eruption of Irazú, a Costa Rican volcano that produced widespread ash fall and that provided a source of the authentic, Central American volcanic ash that the Atlantis test program used, a ash that the Atlantis materials team characterized, using the same particle-size, mineralogical, and chemical analyses that were applied to the Monterey and Osa sediments, and that was then injected, at a controlled rate and particle concentration, into the test tank, where the intake filtration system was operated, under the full range of the flow conditions that the pod's systems require, and the system's performance was monitored — the pressure drop across each screen, the rate of the particle accumulation on the screens, and the quality of the filtered water, as measured by the turbidity, the particle count, and the chemical composition — a monitoring that showed that the filtration system can operate, without clogging and without exceeding the design pressure drop, for a period of at least 180 days under the ash-fall conditions that are predicted for the worst-case volcanic eruption scenario, a period that exceeds the 90-day design endurance of the pod and that, therefore, guarantees that the pod's seawater systems will remain operational throughout the event, a guarantee that the Atlantis design team considers essential and that this PART-038 confirms.
