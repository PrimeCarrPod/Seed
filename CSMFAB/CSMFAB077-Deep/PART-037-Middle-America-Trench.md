# CSMFAB077-DEEP-037 — THE MIDDLE AMERICA TRENCH: SUBDUCTION ZONE DYNAMICS, MEGATHRUST EARTHQUAKE COUPLING WITH CRUSTAL DISPLACEMENT

**Document ID:** CSMFAB077-DEEP-037 | **Parent:** CSMFAB000000000077 | **Version:** 2.0 | **Part:** 037 of 160 — Block 4

The Middle America Trench, the linear bathymetric depression that extends from the southern coast of Mexico to the Pacific coast of Costa Rica, marking the trace of the subduction of the Cocos Plate beneath the Caribbean Plate, reaches its shallowest and most seismically active segment off the Nicoya Peninsula, where the Cocos Plate, with its relatively young (approximately 20 to 25 million years old, based on the magnetic anomaly dating of the oceanic crust), warm, and buoyant lithosphere, subducts at an angle of approximately 25 to 35 degrees, a shallow angle that increases the area of the plate interface that is seismogenic — capable of generating earthquakes — and that has produced, over the past 200 years, a series of large megathrust earthquakes: the 1853 Nicoya earthquake (estimated magnitude 7.5 to 7.8), the 1900 Nicoya earthquake (estimated magnitude 7.0 to 7.5), the 1950 Nicoya earthquake (magnitude 7.3, from the instrumental record), and the most recent — and the best-studied — the 2012 Nicoya earthquake (magnitude 7.6), which ruptured approximately 40 percent of the Nicoya seismogenic zone, a rupture that the geodetic measurements — the GPS stations that the University of Costa Rica and the U.S. universities have deployed on the Nicoya Peninsula, as part of the Collaborative Research in Earthquake and Tectonics (CRET) project, which has been monitoring the Nicoya deformation since 1994 — had been forecasting, because the Nicoya segment had been accumulating strain, at a rate of approximately 80 to 85 millimeters per year, since the last major earthquake in 1950, a 62-year accumulation that produced a slip deficit of approximately 5 meters, a deficit that the 2012 earthquake partially released, producing a maximum slip of approximately 2.5 meters, leaving approximately 2.5 meters of slip deficit that the next Nicoya earthquake — expected within the next 30 to 50 years, based on the recurrence interval of approximately 50 to 100 years — will release, potentially in combination with the megathrust slip that the crustal displacement event would produce, a combination that the Atlantis seismic hazard analysis has assessed and that has been incorporated into the Nicoya pod's anchoring design, which must resist not only the turbidite flow and the wave forces but also the seismic shaking from the megathrust earthquake, shaking that, at the Nicoya site (which is only 50 to 80 kilometers from the trench, and is underlain by the Nicoya Complex ophiolite, which transmits the seismic waves efficiently because of its high density and its high seismic velocity — approximately 6 to 7 kilometers per second for the P-wave), could produce peak ground accelerations of 0.5 to 1.0 g, accelerations that would exert lateral forces on the pod that are comparable to — and, in the worst case, exceed — the turbidite forces, a threat that the Atlantis anchoring design addresses through the use of the seabed adapter frame with the adjustable legs, which can accommodate the differential settlement of the seabed that the seismic shaking could cause — the liquefaction of the sandy sediment, the lateral spreading of the seabed, the ground failures that are the primary cause of the damage to the infrastructure during earthquakes — by allowing the pod to remain level and stable, even as the seabed around it deforms, a capability that the Atlantis seismic analysis has validated through the finite element modeling of the pod's response to the Nicoya earthquake ground motions, a modeling that this PART-037 describes.
