# CSMFAB077-DEEP-023 — OCEANOGRAPHIC REGIME: COSTA RICA COASTAL CURRENT, THERMOCLINE DYNAMICS, PAPAGAYO WIND EFFECTS

**Document ID:** CSMFAB077-DEEP-023 | **Parent:** CSMFAB000000000077 | **Version:** 2.0 | **Part:** 023 of 160 — Block 3

The Costa Rica Coastal Current (CRCC), a seasonal eastern-boundary current that flows northwestward along the Pacific coast of Costa Rica from approximately December through April (the dry season, when the northeast trade winds, blowing across the Central American isthmus, drive the upwelling in the Gulf of Papagayo and the Gulf of Nicoya, two semienclosed embayments on the northern Pacific coast that are the primary sources of the cold, nutrient-rich water that the CRCC transports southward and then westward, feeding the Costa Rica Dome — a region of persistent upwelling centered at approximately 9°N, 90°W, where the thermocline — the layer of rapid temperature change that separates the warm, well-mixed surface layer (approximately 25 to 28 degrees Celsius, 0 to 50 meters depth) from the cold, nutrient-rich deep water (approximately 10 to 15 degrees Celsius, below 100 meters depth) — shoals to within 20 to 30 meters of the surface, a shoaling that brings the deep water into the euphotic zone (the depth range, approximately 0 to 80 meters, where sufficient sunlight penetrates to support photosynthesis) and that triggers the massive phytoplankton blooms that make the Costa Rica Dome one of the most biologically productive regions of the tropical eastern Pacific, a productivity that supports the rich fisheries — the tuna, the mahi-mahi, the sailfish, the marlin — that are the basis of the Costa Rican sportfishing industry and that, in the context of the Atlantis community, will provide a post-event food source for the Osa pod's occupants, through the aquaponic system's fish-farming component (the tilapia that the pod cultivates is a freshwater species, but the Atlantis design team has investigated the possibility of supplementing the tilapia with a marine fish — the Pacific thread herring (Opisthonema libertate), a small, fast-growing, schooling fish that is abundant in the Golfo Dulce and that could be captured, using the pod's deployable fish trap, a collapsible net that the ROV deploys from the pod's exterior storage compartment and that is baited with the waste from the tilapia processing, a sustainable fishing practice that the Dolittle register has approved — provided that the capture rate does not exceed the reproduction rate, a criterion that the Atlantis marine biology team has assessed by modeling the population dynamics of the thread herring in the Golfo Dulce, using the data from the CIMAR fisheries surveys that have been conducted annually since 1995, and that indicate a stable population of approximately 10^7 to 10^8 individuals, a population that can sustain a harvest of approximately 100 to 200 individuals per day — more than sufficient to feed the 16 to 24 occupants of the Osa pods — without any detectable impact on the population, a harvest that the Atlantis operations plan authorizes, subject to the environmental monitoring that verifies the population stability, a verification that the CIMAR acoustic survey — which uses a scientific echosounder, operating at 38 and 120 kHz, to estimate the fish biomass in the Golfo Dulce — provides, on a quarterly basis, a data stream that the Atlantis environmental team analyzes and that SETENA reviews, as part of the adaptive management of the Atlantis pod's ecological impact, a management that the Atlantis program is committed to and that this PART-023, by describing the oceanographic regime that sustains the Golfo Dulce ecosystem, contextualizes, as the physical foundation for the biological productivity that the Atlantis pod depends on, a dependency that the Atlantis design acknowledges and that the environmental monitoring program validates, ensuring that the Atlantis pod's presence in the Golfo Dulce enhances, rather than diminishes, the ecological health of the gulf, a enhancement that the Atlantis program considers essential — the pod must be a net positive for the environment it inhabits, a principle that the Dolittle register articulated as "the Atlantis pod is not a parasite on the gulf but a symbiont, an organism that benefits from the gulf's productivity and that, in return, contributes to the gulf's protection, through the scientific data it collects, the environmental awareness it promotes, and the physical presence that deters the illegal fishing and the uncontrolled tourism that are the greatest threats to the gulf's ecological integrity" — a symbiosis that the Atlantis design embodies and that the Costa Rican environmental authorities, by issuing the Viabilidad Ambiental, have recognized, a recognition that validates the Atlantis approach and that paves the way for the long-term deployment of underwater communities throughout Costa Rica's protected marine areas, a expansion that PART-040, the final part of this Deep Expansion, will describe, as the culmination of the Atlantis program's Costa Rica strategy, a strategy that this PART-023, by establishing the oceanographic context, supports.
