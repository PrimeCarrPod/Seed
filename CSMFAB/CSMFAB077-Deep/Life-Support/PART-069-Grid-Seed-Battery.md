# PART-069 — GRID-SEED BATTERY: LITHIUM IRON PHOSPHATE ENERGY STORAGE FOR SUBSEA POWER BUFFERING

## CSMFAB077-Deep | Life-Support Subsystem | Aegis C Material Architecture

---

The Grid-Seed battery is the secondary energy storage subsystem within the Atlantis-class underwater habitation module's triple-redundant power architecture, positioned between the primary ocean-current turbine and the tertiary hydrogen PEM fuel cell, and serving the dual functions of buffering the intermittent turbine output to provide a continuous, regulated 48 V DC supply to the pod's electrical loads during the four daily slack-water periods when the turbine falls below its 0.8 m/s cut-in speed — periods that collectively total approximately 4 to 6 hours per day at the Monterey site, during which the pod's 3.0 kW continuous load draws approximately 12 to 18 kWh from the battery, representing 40 to 60 percent of the battery's 30 kWh usable capacity (the capacity between the 20 percent and 80 percent state-of-charge limits that define the operating window, as detailed below), a depth of discharge that is comfortably within the battery's design envelope and that allows the battery to be fully recharged during the subsequent high-current periods when the turbine output exceeds the pod's load, the excess power — typically 1.0 to 2.5 kW for 12 to 14 hours per day, delivering 12 to 35 kWh of charge energy, sufficient to fully recharge the battery from 20 percent SoC to 80 percent SoC in approximately 12 hours at the average excess power of 2.5 kW — being directed to the battery through the bidirectional DC-DC converter that manages the charge-discharge cycle, a converter that operates in buck mode during charging (stepping the 48 V DC bus voltage down to the battery's terminal voltage, which ranges from approximately 44.8 V at 20 percent SoC to approximately 58.4 V at 100 percent SoC, with the nominal voltage being 51.2 V at 50 percent SoC, determined by the 16-series LiFePO₄ cell configuration, each cell having a nominal voltage of 3.2 V and an operating range of 2.5 V at 0 percent SoC to 3.65 V at 100 percent SoC, with the 20-80 percent SoC window corresponding to cell voltages of approximately 3.0 V to 3.4 V, a range over which the LiFePO₄ open-circuit voltage (OCV) curve is relatively flat — the OCV changes by only approximately 0.05 V per 10 percent SoC in the 20-80 percent region, compared to approximately 0.15 V per 10 percent SoC for lithium nickel manganese cobalt oxide (NMC) chemistries in the same SoC range, a flatness that complicates the SoC estimation (because the OCV is a weak function of SoC, small voltage measurement errors translate to large SoC estimation errors, requiring the extended Kalman filter described below to fuse the voltage measurement with the coulomb-counting current integration to achieve an accurate SoC estimate) but that simplifies the power conversion design because the battery voltage variation over the usable SoC range is only approximately 51.2 V ±2.4 V, meaning that the DC-DC converter's voltage regulation range is modest, reducing the converter's size, cost, because the battery must seamlessly transition between charging and discharging as the turbine output fluctuates, with the transition being managed by the converter's digital controller, which monitors the bus voltage and the battery current at a 10 kHz sampling rate and adjusts the converter's operating mode within a single switching cycle — 50 microseconds at the 20 kHz switching frequency — ensuring that the bus voltage never deviates from the 48 V setpoint by more than 0.5 V during the transition, a transient response that is achieved through a Type III compensation network in the converter's voltage control loop, providing a phase margin of 60 degrees and a gain crossover frequency of 2 kHz, values that ensure stability across the entire range of battery SoC and load current while providing the fast transient response required for the turbine-to-battery and battery-to-turbine transitions that occur at the tidal cycle timescale.

The selection of lithium iron phosphate (LiFePO₄) as the Grid-Seed battery chemistry, rather than the higher-energy-density lithium nickel manganese cobalt oxide (NMC), lithium nickel cobalt aluminum oxide (NCA), or lithium cobalt oxide (LCO) chemistries that dominate the consumer electronics and electric vehicle markets, is driven by the unique combination of safety, longevity, and thermal stability requirements that characterize the Atlantis underwater habitation environment: a sealed, pressure-tight enclosure at 45 meters depth, with a crew of four persons living, working, and sleeping within approximately 3 meters of the battery compartment, and with no possibility of evacuating the pod to a safe distance in the event of a battery fire — a thermal runaway event in an NMC battery, initiated by an internal short-circuit (the most common failure mode for lithium-ion cells, typically caused by a manufacturing defect that leaves a metallic particle — copper, aluminum, or iron, from the electrode cutting or welding processes — inside the cell, which under the influence of vibration, thermal expansion, or electrochemical cycling migrates to a position where it penetrates the 20-25 micrometer polyethylene or polypropylene separator that physically isolates the positive and negative electrodes, creating a localized short-circuit that generates intense Joule heating (I²R, where the short-circuit current can exceed 500 A in a high-power 18650 or 21700 format cell, generating localized heating rates of thousands of watts per cubic centimeter), rapidly raising the local temperature above the 80-100°C threshold at which the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) — the passivation layer that forms on the graphite anode during the first charge cycle, composed of lithium carbonate (Li₂CO₃), lithium fluoride (LiF), lithium alkyl carbonates, and other organic and inorganic decomposition products of the electrolyte solvent (typically ethylene carbonate, dimethyl carbonate, and ethyl methyl carbonate) and the lithium salt (LiPF₆) — begins to decompose exothermically, releasing heat that raises the cell temperature further, triggering the decomposition of the cathode material at approximately 180-200°C for NMC, which releases oxygen (the layered oxide structure of NMC — LiNi_xMn_yCo_zO₂ — contains labile oxygen that is released when the crystal structure collapses at high temperature, a critical difference from the olivine structure of LiFePO₄, in which the oxygen atoms are covalently bonded to phosphorus in the PO₄³⁻ tetrahedral units, with a P-O bond energy of approximately 500 kJ/mol, compared to the approximately 300 kJ/mol metal-oxygen bond energy in the layered NMC structure, meaning that LiFePO₄ does not release oxygen until temperatures exceed approximately 400°C — well above the temperatures reached in even the most severe internal short-circuit scenario — whereas NMC releases oxygen at approximately 200°C, fueling the thermal runaway reaction in a positive feedback cycle: the oxygen released from the cathode reacts exothermically with the organic electrolyte solvents, generating more heat, which accelerates the cathode decomposition, releasing more oxygen, and the cycle escalates until the cell reaches temperatures of 600-900°C, at which point the aluminum current collector melts, the copper current collector (with a melting point of 1,085°C) may not melt but can become exposed as the electrode structure disintegrates, and the cell vents its electrolyte vapor and decomposition gases — hydrogen, methane, ethane, carbon monoxide, and various fluorinated organic compounds from the decomposition of LiPF₆ and the fluorinated binders (PVDF) — through the safety vent (a scored metal disk at the positive terminal that ruptures at a pressure of approximately 1.0-1.5 MPa, designed to prevent the cell case from exploding), and if the vented gases encounter an ignition source — a spark from the short-circuit itself, or a hot surface — they ignite, producing a jet flame that can reach temperatures of 1,000°C and that can propagate to adjacent cells through the hot gas impingement and the radiant heat transfer, triggering a cascading thermal runaway that can involve the entire battery pack within seconds to minutes, a catastrophic failure that in the confined, oxygen-enriched atmosphere of the Atlantis pod (where the O₂ partial pressure is maintained at 21 kPa, but the total pressure is 1 atm, so the O₂ concentration is 21 percent — identical to the Earth's surface atmosphere, and thus not an additional fire risk beyond the normal terrestrial fire hazard, but the confined volume and the impossibility of egress make any fire, regardless of the ambient O₂ concentration, an existential threat to the crew) would be unsurvivable.

The passive balancing circuit, which equalizes the state of charge of the 16 series-connected super-cells by dissipating the excess charge of the higher-SoC cells through a 100 mA balancing current, operates during the charging phase when the pack voltage exceeds approximately 54.4 V (3.4 V per cell), at which point the BMS enables the balancing MOSFET for any super-cell whose voltage exceeds the average super-cell voltage by more than 10 mV, shunting 100 mA of the charge current around that super-cell through a 33 Ω power resistor, which dissipates 0.33 W (I²R = 0.1² × 33 = 0.33 W) of heat that must be removed by the pack's liquid cooling system, a modest thermal load that the cooling system handles without any detectable increase in the coolant temperature — the 100 mA balancing current is deliberately low to limit the balancing power dissipation and to avoid the need for active cooling of the BMS circuit board, which is passively cooled by conduction to the aluminum pack enclosure, but the low balancing current means that the balancing process is slow — a 1 Ah imbalance between two super-cells (corresponding to approximately 0.1 percent of the super-cell's 1,200 Ah capacity, at the 12-parallel configuration) would take 10 hours to correct at 100 mA, and a 10 Ah imbalance (approximately 0.8 percent) would take 100 hours, approximately 4 days, which is acceptable for the Atlantis application because the imbalance accumulates slowly — the primary cause of imbalance in the Atlantis battery is the temperature gradient across the pack, with the warmer cells at the center experiencing slightly higher self-discharge rates (the self-discharge rate of LiFePO₄ approximately doubles for every 10°C increase in temperature, following the Arrhenius equation with an activation energy of approximately 50 kJ/mol, so a 5°C temperature difference between the center and the periphery of the pack corresponds to a self-discharge rate ratio of exp((50,000/8.314) × (1/298 - 1/303)) = exp(6,013 × (0.003356 - 0.003300)) = exp(6,013 × 0.000056) = exp(0.337) = 1.40, meaning the center cells self-discharge approximately 40 percent faster than the periphery cells, accumulating an imbalance of approximately 0.01 percent SoC per day, or approximately 0.12 Ah per day for the 1,200 Ah super-cell, which the 100 mA balancing current can correct in approximately 1.2 hours of balancing operation, so the 100 mA balancing current is more than sufficient for the Atlantis application's imbalance rate, and the balancing circuit is a cost-effective and reliable solution that the BMS industry has converged on for stationary LFP battery systems, with the alternative — active balancing, in which charge is transferred from the higher-SoC cells to the lower-SoC cells through a switched-capacitor or DC-DC converter circuit with efficiencies of 85-95 percent — being more complex and more expensive (approximately $8 per channel for active balancing vs. $0.50 per channel for passive balancing, and with 16 channels, the active balancing adds approximately $120 to the BMS cost) but offering higher balancing currents (1-5 A typical for active balancing ICs), a capability that is valuable for large-format NMC battery packs with high self-discharge rates and significant cell-to-cell variation, but that is unnecessary for the well-matched, low-self-discharge CATL LiFePO₄ cells in the Atlantis pack, a design decision that the Torvalds register validated through a trade study comparing the passive and active balancing architectures against the Atlantis requirements, concluding that passive balancing meets all requirements with margin and that the active balancing's additional cost and complexity would not be justified by any measurable improvement in pack performance or life.

The extended Kalman filter (EKF) for SoC estimation is a model-based state estimation algorithm that combines the BMS's voltage and current measurements with an equivalent-circuit model (ECM) of the LiFePO₄ cell to produce an optimal (in the minimum-variance sense) estimate of the cell's state of charge and internal resistance, updating the estimate at each measurement time step (1 second) through a two-step predict-correct cycle. In the prediction step, the state vector x_k = [SoC_k, R₀_k]ᵀ (where R₀ is the cell's ohmic internal resistance, the dominant source of voltage drop under load and a key indicator of cell degradation — R₀ increases approximately linearly with cycle life, from approximately 0.8 mΩ at beginning of life to approximately 1.5 mΩ at end of life, and this increase is the primary mechanism for the capacity fade observed in LiFePO₄ cells, as the increased internal resistance causes a larger voltage drop during discharge, causing the cell to reach the 2.8 V cutoff earlier and thus deliver less usable capacity, even though the cell's actual chemical capacity — the total lithium inventory available for intercalation — may not have decreased proportionally) is propagated forward in time using the state transition model: SoC_{k+1} = SoC_k - (η_c × I_k × Δt)/(C_n × 3,600), where η_c is the coulombic efficiency (approximately 0.998 for LiFePO₄, very close to unity, reflecting the absence of significant side reactions at the electrodes under normal operating conditions — the dominant side reaction is the continuous growth of the SEI on the anode, which consumes lithium at a rate of approximately 0.002 percent per cycle, hence the 99.8 percent coulombic efficiency), I_k is the measured battery current (positive for discharge, negative for charge), Δt is the time step in seconds, and C_n is the nominal cell capacity in ampere-hours, with the factor of 3,600 converting ampere-hours to ampere-seconds, and R₀_{k+1} = R₀_k (the internal resistance is modeled as a slowly varying parameter, with its dynamics being driven by the cell's degradation, which occurs on a timescale of hundreds to thousands of cycles, far slower than the 1-second EKF update rate, so the random-walk model — R₀_{k+1} = R₀_k + w_R, where w_R is a small process noise term with a variance that represents the expected rate of resistance increase, approximately 10⁻⁶ Ω² per hour, based on the long-term degradation data — is appropriate). In the correction step, the predicted state is updated using the measurement of the cell voltage V_k and the measurement model: V_k = OCV(SoC_k) - I_k × R₀_k + v_k, where OCV(SoC) is the open-circuit voltage as a function of SoC (a nonlinear function that is implemented as a lookup table, with 100 points covering the 0-100 percent SoC range, interpolated using cubic splines, and derived from the cell manufacturer's OCV-SoC data, measured at 25°C with a 0.05C discharge rate to minimize the internal resistance's effect on the voltage and thus to obtain the true OCV, and then corrected for the cell's temperature using the entropic coefficient dU/dT, which for LiFePO₄ is approximately -0.05 mV/K in the 20-80 percent SoC range, meaning that the OCV decreases by approximately 0.5 mV per 10°C temperature increase, a small correction that the EKF includes in the measurement model through a temperature-dependent offset term OCV(SoC, T) = OCV(SoC, 25°C) + dU/dT × (T - 25), where T is the cell temperature measured by the thermistor — a NTC 10KΩ, Beta 3,435 K thermistor adhered to the cell surface with thermally conductive epoxy, providing a temperature measurement accuracy of ±0.5°C) and v_k is the measurement noise, assumed to be zero-mean Gaussian with a variance of approximately 4 mV² (corresponding to the LTC6811's ±1.2 mV TUE plus the thermal noise in the cell voltage — the Johnson-Nyquist noise of the cell's internal resistance, approximately 0.5 nV/√Hz × √(10 Hz bandwidth) ≈ 1.6 nV, negligible — plus the modeling error in the OCV-SoC lookup table, which is the dominant source of measurement model uncertainty, estimated at ±2 mV based on the OCV hysteresis of LiFePO₄ — the OCV at a given SoC depends on whether the SoC was reached from a higher SoC (discharge) or a lower SoC (charge), a hysteresis of approximately 5-10 mV that is not captured by the simple OCV-SoC model and that contributes to the measurement model uncertainty, which is why the measurement noise covariance R in the EKF is set to approximately 4 mV² to account for this unmodeled effect, ensuring that the EKF does not over-trust the voltage measurement and instead relies on the coulomb-counting prediction for short-term SoC estimation while using the voltage measurement to correct the long-term drift of the coulomb counter). The EKF's gain matrix K_k — the Kalman gain, which determines how much the prediction is corrected based on the new measurement — is computed from the predicted state covariance matrix P_k⁻ and the measurement Jacobian H_k = [dOCV/dSoC, -I_k], where dOCV/dSoC is the slope of the OCV-SoC curve at the current SoC estimate (approximately 3.3 mV per percent SoC in the 20-80 percent region, as discussed), and the Kalman gain adapts dynamically: when the current is high (I_k large), the measurement Jacobian's second element is large, meaning that the voltage measurement is more sensitive to R₀ than to SoC, so the EKF uses the voltage measurement primarily to update the R₀ estimate rather than the SoC estimate; when the battery is at rest (I_k = 0), the measurement model simplifies to V_k = OCV(SoC_k) + v_k, and the Kalman gain directs the correction almost entirely to the SoC estimate, effectively resetting the coulomb counter to the true SoC as determined by the OCV, a reset that occurs during the four daily slack-water periods when the turbine is not generating and the battery is neither charging nor discharging heavily, providing the EKF with the zero-current voltage measurements that are most informative for SoC estimation — the voltage measured at I = 0 is the OCV, and the OCV-SoC relationship provides the SoC directly (subject to the hysteresis uncertainty), so the EKF's correction step during these periods is essentially a direct SoC measurement, which is why the EKF, despite the complexity described above, reduces to a relatively simple algorithm in practice: coulomb-count during high-current periods, correct the SoC with OCV measurements during zero-current periods, and use the R₀ estimate for degradation tracking and cell health monitoring, a realization that led the BMS software team to implement a simplified "poor man's EKF" that uses a fixed Kalman gain (pre-computed from the steady-state Riccati equation solution for the Atlantis battery's typical operating profile) rather than the full online covariance propagation and Kalman gain computation, reducing the computational load by approximately 80 percent while achieving the same SoC estimation accuracy (±0.5 percent) as the full EKF, a simplification that the Torvalds register reviewed, validated through a simulation comparing the simplified and full EKF performance on the 12-month ADCP current dataset, and approved for the deployed BMS software, noting that the simplified algorithm's slight degradation in R₀ estimation accuracy (from approximately ±5 μΩ for the full EKF to approximately ±8 μΩ for the simplified version) does not affect the ability to detect the cell degradation trend over the 12-year replacement cycle.

The liquid cooling system that maintains the battery cells at 25°C ±2°C is a critical subsystem whose design is driven by the thermal characteristics of the LiFePO₄ cells and the heat transfer constraints of the underwater environment. The cells generate heat according to the equation Q_gen = I² × R₀ + I × T × dU/dT, where the first term is the irreversible Joule heating (ohmic loss) and the second term is the reversible entropic heating/cooling — the term T × dU/dT is approximately 25°C × (-0.05 mV/K) = -1.25 mV, and at a discharge current of 62.5 A (the pod's 3.0 kW load at 48 V), the reversible term contributes approximately 62.5 × (-0.00125) = -0.078 W (negative, meaning that the entropic effect cools the cell slightly during discharge, a small effect compared to the Joule heating of 62.5² × 0.001 = 3.9 W per cell at the pack level — with 192 cells, the total Joule heating at the 3.0 kW load is 192 × 3.9 = 749 W, and the total entropic cooling is 192 × (-0.078) = -15 W, so the net heat generation is approximately 734 W, which the liquid cooling system must remove to maintain the 25°C cell temperature. The cooling system circulates a 50/50 mixture of propylene glycol and deionized water — propylene glycol is selected over ethylene glycol for its lower toxicity (important for the enclosed habitation environment, where a coolant leak into the pod's atmosphere is a potential hazard, though the coolant loop operates at atmospheric pressure in a double-walled configuration, with the inner tube carrying the coolant and the outer tube serving as a containment barrier with a leak-detection sensor — a conductivity probe that detects the presence of the glycol-water mixture, which has a conductivity of approximately 50 μS/cm compared to < 1 μS/cm for the dry, clean outer tube, triggering an alarm if a leak is detected, a safety feature that the Kiddo register required after reviewing the coolant leak incident on the International Space Station in 2023, where an internal coolant loop leak released ammonia coolant into the cabin atmosphere, requiring an emergency response and a spacewalk to repair) — through a cold plate that is bonded to the bottom surface of the aluminum pack enclosure, with the cold plate being a machined aluminum plate (6061-T6, 10 mm thick) with internal serpentine coolant channels (6 mm × 6 mm square cross-section, 3 meters total length) that are sealed by a brazed-on aluminum cover plate, and the thermal interface between the battery cells and the cold plate is a 2 mm thick layer of thermally conductive gap filler (Bergquist Gap Pad 3000S30, with a thermal conductivity of 3.0 W/m·K and a Shore 00 hardness of 30, allowing it to conform to the slight surface irregularities of the cell bottoms and the cold plate surface, minimizing the contact thermal resistance to approximately 0.5 cm²·K/W, which for the 192 cells with a combined footprint area of approximately 1.0 m² corresponds to a temperature drop of ΔT = Q × R_th_contact/A = 734 × 0.5 × 10⁻⁴/1.0 = 0.037°C, negligible compared to the thermal resistance of the cells themselves, which is the dominant temperature drop in the cooling path — the thermal resistance from the cell interior (where the heat is generated) to the cell surface is approximately 1-2°C/W for the prismatic 100 Ah cells, and with 192 cells sharing the 734 W heat load, the temperature drop from the cell interior to the cell surface is approximately 1.5 × 734/192 = 5.7°C, meaning that the cell interior temperature is approximately 30.7°C when the cold plate is at 25°C, which is within the acceptable operating temperature range (maximum 45°C at the cell interior) and confirms that the cold plate cooling is adequate, with the glycol coolant entering the cold plate at 15°C (the temperature maintained by the pod's central glycol loop, which rejects heat to the seawater through the TPMS heat exchanger described in Part 072) and exiting at approximately 18°C after absorbing the 734 W of battery heat, a temperature rise of 3°C that the central glycol loop accommodates by adjusting the seawater flow rate through the heat exchanger to maintain the 15°C supply temperature.

The aerogel thermal barriers installed between adjacent cells and between the cell stack and the pack enclosure serve a dual function: during normal operation, they reduce the thermal gradient across the pack by limiting the heat transfer from the warmer center cells to the cooler periphery cells and the enclosure — the aerogel's thermal conductivity of 0.015 W/m·K is among the lowest of any solid material, approximately 2.5 times lower than still air (0.026 W/m·K) and approximately 15 times lower than the thermal conductivity of the aluminum pack enclosure (approximately 200 W/m·K for 6061-T6 aluminum), so the thermal resistance of a 3 mm thick aerogel sheet is R_th = L/(k × A) = 0.003/(0.015 × A) = 0.2/A m²·K/W, and for the approximately 3 m² of surface area of the cell stack, the total thermal resistance from the cells to the enclosure through the aerogel is approximately 0.2/3 = 0.067 K/W, meaning that only approximately (T_cell_center - T_enclosure)/0.067 = (30 - 25)/0.067 ≈ 75 W of heat is transferred through the aerogel from the cell stack to the enclosure, with the remaining 734 - 75 = 659 W being removed by the liquid cooling cold plate, confirming that the cold plate is the dominant heat removal path, as intended by the thermal design — and during a thermal runaway event in a single cell, the aerogel barriers serve as a firebreak, withstanding temperatures up to 650°C (the maximum rated temperature of the silica aerogel blanket, specifically Aspen Aerogels Pyrogel XTE, which is rated for continuous use at 650°C and which has been tested to prevent thermal runaway propagation in NMC battery packs at the UL 9540A test standard's cell-to-cell propagation test, a test that the Atlantis battery pack was also subjected to, using a deliberately overcharged LiFePO₄ cell as the thermal runaway initiator — the overcharged cell was heated to 270°C by an internal heater element, triggering the exothermic decomposition, but the aerogel barriers limited the peak temperature of the adjacent cells to 75°C, well below the 270°C thermal runaway onset temperature, confirming that the aerogel barriers prevent thermal runaway propagation in the LiFePO₄ pack, as expected from the chemistry's intrinsic safety, and providing the UL 9540A certification that the Atlantis battery's marine insurance underwriter — Lloyd's of London, which insures the Atlantis demonstration project under a bespoke subsea habitation policy that was developed in collaboration with Carrington Storm Motors' risk management team and that the Kiddo register reviewed for compliance with the Aegis C safety philosophy — required as a condition of coverage.

The CO₂ fire suppression system, activated at an enclosure internal temperature of 65°C — a temperature threshold that is well below the 270°C LiFePO₄ thermal runaway onset but above any plausible normal operating temperature (the enclosure interior typically operates at 25-30°C, and the 65°C threshold provides a 35°C margin against false activations caused by, for example, a temporary cooling system failure that allows the cell temperature to rise toward the 45°C maximum before the BMS reduces the charge/discharge current to limit the heat generation, a protective action that the BMS's thermal management algorithm implements when any cell temperature exceeds 40°C, well before the 65°C fire suppression threshold, so the fire suppression activation implies a failure of both the cooling system and the BMS thermal protection, a double-fault scenario that the Atlantis safety analysis rates as having a probability of less than 10⁻⁶ per mission, based on the failure rates of the cooling pump (approximately 5 × 10⁻⁶ per hour, or 0.04 per year, for the high-reliability brushless DC pump selected — a Micropump GB-P25 with a mean time between failures of 25,000 hours at the 15°C, 2 bar operating point, which is the standard for NASA-grade liquid cooling pumps and which the Micropump model has demonstrated in the ISS external thermal control system, where the same pump design has accumulated over 150,000 hours of continuous operation without a failure) and the BMS's thermal protection software (a SIL 2 certified safety function, per IEC 61508, with a probability of failure on demand of less than 10⁻³, meaning that the combined probability of both failures is approximately 0.04/year × (2,160 hours mission/8,760 hours/year) × 10⁻³ = 9 × 10⁻⁶, and when the additional probability of a fire initiator — an internal short-circuit that eludes the cell-level fusing, a probability estimated at 10⁻⁷ per cell-year by the cell manufacturer's safety analysis, and with 192 cells and a 0.25-year (90-day) mission, the probability is 192 × 0.25 × 10⁻⁷ = 4.8 × 10⁻⁶ — is multiplied by the probability that the thermal management and BMS fail (10⁻⁵, rounding up), the total probability of a fire requiring suppression is approximately 5 × 10⁻¹¹ per mission, or essentially zero, making the CO₂ fire suppression system a "belt-and-suspenders" precaution that the Aegis C safety philosophy requires despite its vanishingly low probability of being needed, because the consequence of an uncontained battery fire in a sealed underwater habitation module is the loss of all four crew members, a consequence whose severity outweighs the $800 cost of the CO₂ suppression system (a 2 kg CO₂ cylinder, a solenoid valve, a distribution nozzle, and a smoke detector — the smoke detector being the primary fire detection sensor, with the 65°C temperature threshold providing redundancy). Upon activation, the CO₂ cylinder discharges its contents into the sealed battery enclosure (which is hermetically sealed from the pod's interior atmosphere by a bolted access cover with a Viton O-ring seal, rated for a pressure differential of 0.5 bar, sufficient to contain the CO₂ discharge's pressure transient of approximately 0.2 bar, based on the ideal gas law: 2 kg of CO₂ at 25°C, with a molecular weight of 44 g/mol, corresponds to 45.5 moles, occupying a volume of V = nRT/P = 45.5 × 8.314 × 298/101,325 = 1.11 m³ at atmospheric pressure, while the battery enclosure's internal volume is approximately 0.4 m³, so the CO₂ partial pressure after discharge is approximately 101,325 × (1.11/0.4) = 281,000 Pa ≈ 2.8 bar, which exceeds the 1.5 bar absolute (0.5 bar gauge) enclosure pressure bar absolute (0.3 bar gauge), requiring a CO₂ charge of only 0.6 kg, which is sufficient to achieve a CO₂ concentration of approximately 50 percent in the enclosure (0.6 kg/44 g/mol = 13.6 moles, review identified as part of the iterative design process, and which increased the CO₂ cylinder cost from $65 to $85 (for the smaller, lighter cylinder with a reduced CO₂ charge) but eliminated the damage to adjacent equipment — for a risk score of 0.01 × 500 = 5, above the Aegis C risk acceptance threshold of 2) and that the design change reduced to a probability of 0.0001 (with the pressure
