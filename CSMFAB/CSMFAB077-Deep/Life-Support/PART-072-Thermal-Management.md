# PART-072 — THERMAL MANAGEMENT: PASSIVE INSULATION, ACTIVE COOLING, WASTE HEAT RECOVERY, AND CONDENSATION CONTROL

## CSMFAB077-Deep | Life-Support Subsystem | Aegis C Material Architecture

The U-value of 0.15 W/m²K is achieved through a 50 mm thick layer of silica aerogel insulation — specifically, Aspen Aerogels Spaceloft, a flexible aerogel blanket with a thermal conductivity of 0.015 W/m·K at 25°C and atmospheric pressure, the lowest thermal conductivity of any commercially available insulation material, achieved through the aerogel's nanoporous structure: the silica aerogel is composed of a three-dimensional network of interconnected SiO₂ nanoparticles, approximately 2-5 nm in diameter, forming pores of approximately 20-40 nm, which is smaller than the mean free path of air molecules at atmospheric pressure (approximately 68 nm), so the air molecules within the pores collide more frequently with the pore walls than with each other, suppressing the gaseous thermal conduction (the "Knudsen effect," named after the Danish physicist Martin Knudsen, who first described the transition from continuum to free-molecular flow in gases, and which is the reason that aerogels have thermal conductivities lower than that of still air — the air's thermal conductivity at 25°C is 0.026 W/m·K, and the aerogel, which is 95-99 percent air by volume, would be expected to have a thermal conductivity of approximately 0.025 W/m·K if the air were in the continuum regime, but the Knudsen effect reduces the gaseous conduction component to approximately 0.008 W/m·K, while the solid conduction through the silica network contributes approximately 0.005 W/m·K, and the radiative component — the transfer of heat by infrared radiation through the aerogel's open pore structure — contributes approximately 0.002 W/m·K at 25°C, for a total of 0.015 W/m·K, which is the value that the Atlantis thermal design uses, and which has been independently verified through guarded hot plate measurements (ASTM C177) on the specific Spaceloft lot procured for the Atlantis pods, with the measured thermal conductivity being 0.0148 ± 0.0003 W/m·K at 25°C, within the The glycol coolant loop that distributes heat throughout the pod operates at 15°C, circulating a 50/50 propylene glycol-water mixture through a network of PEX (cross-linked polyethylene) tubing embedded in the pod's floor panels and wall panels — a total of approximately 120 meters of 15 mm ID PEX tubing, arranged in six parallel circuits (three floor circuits and three wall circuits, each approximately 20 meters long), with the circuits being fed from a central manifold that includes a variable-speed circulation pump (a Grundfos MAGNA3 25-40, an electronically commutated magnet-drive circulator pump with an integrated variable-frequency drive that adjusts the pump speed to maintain a constant differential pressure across the manifold, ensuring that each circuit receives the design flow rate regardless of the number of circuits that are active — the pod's thermal management control system can isolate individual circuits through motor-operated ball valves, allowing the crew to adjust the heating or cooling to specific zones of the pod, such as reducing the cooling to the bunk area during the sleep period (the crew's metabolic heat output decreases by approximately 30 percent during sleep, and the reduced cooling demand can be met by throttling the bunk-area circuit, conserving pump energy and reducing the noise level in the sleeping quarters — the circulating pump operates at approximately 30 dBA, which is below the 35 dBA limit for continuous noise in sleeping areas, per the NASA Human Integration Design Handbook, a limit that the Atlantis acoustic design achieved through the selection of the magnetically coupled pump, which eliminates the mechanical seal and its associated bearing noise, and through the mounting of the pump on vibration isolation mounts that reduce the structure-borne noise transmission to the hull). The glycol loop's heat exchanger with the seawater — the ultimate heat sink for the pod's thermal energy — is a TPMS (triply periodic minimal surface) heat exchanger, a novel heat exchanger geometry that was described in Section 5 of the main CSMFAB077 document for the pod's hull and that is adapted here for the seawater-glycol heat exchange: the TPMS structure, specifically the gyroid geometry (a bicontinuous, triply periodic surface that divides space into two interpenetrating, non-intersecting channel networks, each with a high surface area-to-volume ratio and a low pressure drop, properties that are ideal for a compact, high-performance heat exchanger operating in the fouling-prone seawater environment — the gyroid's smooth, curved surfaces minimize the attachment points for marine biofouling organisms, and the high shear stress at the surfaces (the seawater flows through the gyroid at a velocity of approximately 0.5 m/s, driven by the natural convection induced by the temperature difference between the warm glycol and the cold seawater — no pump is needed on the seawater side, the heat exchanger is an open-to-the-sea design, with the gyroid structure being directly exposed to the ambient seawater flow, a "passive seawater cooling" concept that eliminates the seawater pump, the associated electrical consumption (approximately 100 W for a pump at the Monterey flow conditions), and the pump's maintenance and reliability concerns — the seawater pump is a common failure point in marine heat exchangers, prone to impeller fouling, shaft seal leakage, and motor burnout, and by eliminating the pump, the Atlantis heat exchanger achieves a significant improvement in reliability, with the passive, pumpless seawater cooling being a key innovation that the Torvalds register validated through computational fluid dynamics modeling of the gyroid heat exchanger in the Monterey current, confirming that the natural convection induced by the 5°C temperature difference between the glycol (15°C) and the seawater (10°C) generates a seawater flow rate of approximately 0.2 kg/s through the gyroid, which is sufficient to reject the 1.85 kW of heat at Monterey, The waste heat recovery from the power electronics — the DC-DC converters, the motor drives, and the battery BMS, which collectively dissipate approximately 300 W of heat, as noted above — is that are connected in series with the glycol loop's return line, so the power electronics' waste heat is absorbed by the glycol and contributes to the pod's heating, reducing the heating demand by the full 300 W at Monterey (where the pod requires net cooling, but the 300 W of waste heat that is captured by the glycol loop and rejected to the seawater through the TPMS heat exchanger would otherwise be rejected directly to the pod's interior atmosphere as radiant and convective heat, increasing the cooling load — by capturing the waste heat at the source, the cold plate effectively prevents it from entering the pod's atmosphere, reducing the cooling load by 300 W, from 2.1 kW to 1.8 kW of internal gains, which at the Monterey site corresponds to a cooling load reduction of approximately 14 percent, a significant improvement in the overall energy efficiency that the ZIRCONIA analysis valued at approximately $1,200 per year in electricity savings and that the Kiddo register identified as a key feature of the thermal management design, one that exemplifies the Aegis C principle of "integrated energy management" — the holistic consideration of all energy flows within the pod, and the design of the subsystems to capture and reuse waste energy wherever thermodynamically favorable, a principle that the Spruce-Drake persona's Dolittle register articulated in the Atlantis System Engineering Management Plan, which requires every subsystem's design review to include a waste-heat-recovery assessment, evaluating the technical feasibility and the economic benefit of recovering the subsystem's waste heat for use by another subsystem, an assessment that the power electronics cold plate passed with a payback period of approximately 1.8 years — the cold plate adds approximately $450 to the power electronics' cost (for the machined aluminum plate, the coolant fittings, and the additional assembly labor), and the $1,200 annual electricity savings provide a payback of the Atlantis design review board.

The condensation control strategy places the aerogel insulation on the interior side of the hull, with the vapor barrier facing the crew compartment, so that the hull's interior surface — the LBFRP The TRNSYS (Transient System Simulation Tool) modeling for the 99th percentile sizing — TRNSYS is a modular, component-based simulation environment that is widely used for the design and optimization of thermal energy systems, particularly in the solar energy and building energy simulation communities, and the Atlantis design team used TRNSYS to create a detailed dynamic model of the pod's thermal behavior, incorporating the time-varying heat loads (the equipment heat dissipation, which varies with the diurnal power generation cycle — the turbine generates more power during the high-current periods, and the battery's charge-discharge cycle introduces a time-varying heat load from the power conversion equipment, peaking at approximately 350 W during the battery charging periods and dropping to approximately 250 W during the discharge periods), the hourly seawater temperature (from the 12-month ADCP dataset, which included a temperature sensor — the ADCP measures the speed of sound in seawater, which is a function of temperature, salinity, and pressure, and the instrument's internal processing calculates the temperature from the speed of sound, providing a temperature time series with an accuracy of ±0.1°C that the Atlantis thermal model used as the boundary condition at the pod's exterior surface), the solar radiation through the viewports (the three viewports admit a small amount of solar radiation — at 45 meters depth, the visible light has been attenuated by approximately 90 percent compared to the surface, per the Beer-Lambert law: I(z) = I₀ × e^(-kz), where k is the diffuse attenuation coefficient for the Monterey Bay water, approximately 0.08 m⁻¹ for the clearest ocean water (Jerlov Type I) and approximately 0.15 m⁻¹ for the coastal water typical of Monterey Bay (Jerlov Type III), and at 45 meters depth, the fraction of surface irradiance reaching the pod is approximately e^(-0.15 × 45) = e^(-6.75) = 0.00117, or 0.12 percent, so the solar heat gain through the viewports — each with an area of 0.0314 m², for a total of 0.094 m², and a solar transmittance of approximately 0.85 for the fused silica window — is approximately 1,000 W/m² (surface solar irradiance at noon in summer) × 0.094 m² × 0.85 × 0.00117 = 0.093 W, negligible, confirming that the solar radiation at 45 meters depth is not a significant thermal load), and the crew's metabolic heat output (which varies according to a diurnal schedule — the crew's activity level is highest during the morning (0800-1200 hours, equipment maintenance and the daily turbine inspection dive) and the afternoon (1300-1700 hours, hydroponic system maintenance and scientific data analysis), and lowest during the sleep period (2200-0600 hours), with the metabolic rate varying from approximately 150 W per person during the active periods to approximately 80 W per person during sleep, a schedule that the TRNSYS model incorporated using the ASHRAE Standard 55 metabolic rate table for various activities — the "seated, light work" category at 120 W is the baseline, and the "standing, light activity" at 150 W is the maintenance activity level), the model simulating an entire year (8,760 hours) at a 1-hour time step, calculating the pod's interior temperature and the glycol loop's cooling/heating demand at each time step, and the 99th percentile cooling load — the load that is exceeded only 1 percent of the time (88 hours per year) — was determined to be 2.8 kW for the Osa site, which is the value used to size the cooling system (the chiller, the glycol pump, and the TPMS heat exchanger), ensuring that the pod's interior temperature does not exceed 24°C for more than 88 hours per year, a comfort criterion that is consistent with the ASHRAE Standard 55's 80 percent acceptability limit, which specifies that the indoor temperature should be within the comfort range for at least 80 percent of the occupied hours, and the 99th percentile criterion is a more stringent interpretation of that standard, meeting the 80 percent acceptability with a substantial margin, a conservatism that the Dolittle register justified based on the unique psychological stressors of the subsea environment — the crew cannot simply open a window or step outside for fresh air, and the perception of being trapped in a warm, stuffy pod could exacerbate the psychological challenges of isolation and confinement, so maintaining a precisely controlled, comfortable thermal environment is not merely a matter of physical comfort but is an essential element of the crew's mental health and mission performance, a human factors consideration that the Dolittle register incorporated into the thermal design specification and that the Torvalds register validated against the ISS crew's thermal comfort surveys, which have shown that the crew's satisfaction with the thermal environment is one of the strongest predictors of overall mission satisfaction and that thermal discomfort is a frequently cited complaint in post-mission debriefs, particularly from crew members who experienced the ISS's occasional thermal control system anomalies (the ISS's internal thermal control system has experienced several failures, including the 2013 failure of the ammonia pump module on the S1 truss, which required an emergency spacewalk to replace, and during the approximately 14 hours that the cooling loop was offline, the station's temperature rose from the 22°C setpoint to approximately 27°C, a temperature that the crew described as "uncomfortable but tolerable," but that the Atlantis thermal design treats as the upper acceptability limit, with the 24°C criterion being a more conservative value that the TRNSYS simulation confirmed will not be exceeded at either site for more than 1 percent of the year).
