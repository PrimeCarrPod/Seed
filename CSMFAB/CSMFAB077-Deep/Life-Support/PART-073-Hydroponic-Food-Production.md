# PART-073 — HYDROPONIC FOOD PRODUCTION: NFT CHANNELS, LED LIGHTING, CROP SELECTION, AND PSYCHOLOGICAL BENEFIT

## CSMFAB077-Deep | Life-Support Subsystem | Aegis C Material Architecture

---

The hydroponic food production subsystem transforms the Atlantis-class underwater habitation module from a purely life-supported survival capsule into a self-renewing biosphere in miniature, capable of producing approximately 3,200 kilocalories per day of fresh, photosynthetically active radiation-grown food at peak yield — a figure that represents approximately 27 percent of the crew of four's and the US Department of Agriculture dietary guidelines, which the Atlantis nutrition plan adopts with a 10 percent margin, increasing the provisioned caloric intake to 3,300 kcal/person/day, of which The Nutrient Film Technique (NFT) hydroponic channels — 30 square meters of growing area, arranged in six parallel channels, each 5.0 meters long, 0.5 meters wide, and inclined at a 2 percent slope (1:50) to provide gravity-driven flow of the nutrient solution — are fabricated from food-grade HDPE (high-density polyethylene) sheets, 3 mm thick, thermoformed into a trapezoidal cross-section with a base width of 100 mm, a top width of 200 mm, and a depth of 50 mm, the trapezoidal shape providing a balance between the root zone volume (the roots of the plants grow in a thin film of nutrient solution that flows along the bottom of the channel, with the upper portion of the channel providing an air space for the roots to access O₂ — the root zone must be both irrigated and aerated, and the NFT design achieves this by maintaining a shallow flow depth of approximately 2-5 mm, which is sufficient to supply the roots with water and nutrients while leaving the majority of the root mass exposed to the humid air within the channel, where the O₂ concentration is approximately 20-21 percent (the channel is not sealed, the plants are inserted through holes in the top cover, and the air in the channel communicates freely with the pod's atmosphere, so the root-zone O₂ concentration is essentially the same as the pod's atmospheric O₂ concentration), a design that avoids the root anoxia that is the most common cause of crop failure in hydroponic systems — when the roots are continuously submerged in the nutrient solution (as in deep-water culture or raft systems), the dissolved O₂ in the solution, which at 22°C and atmospheric pressure is approximately 8 mg/L, is rapidly depleted by the root respiration, and the O₂ must be replenished by an air pump and an air stone, adding complexity, noise, and a potential failure point, whereas the NFT design uses the natural diffusion of O₂ from the channel's air space into the thin film of nutrient solution flowing over the roots, a passive aeration mechanism that is inherently reliable — there are no air pumps to fail, no air stones to clog, and no electrical power required for root-zone aeration, a simplicity that the Atlantis design philosophy prizes and that the Kiddo register identified as a key advantage of the NFT technique for the subsea application, where the reliability of every subsystem is paramount), with the bottom of the channel being covered with a capillary mat — a non-woven polypropylene fabric, 2 mm thick, that wicks the nutrient solution upward from the thin film at the channel bottom and distributes it evenly across the root zone, ensuring that all roots receive a uniform supply of water and nutrients, even if the channel's flow is temporarily interrupted (the capillary mat retains approximately 2 L of nutrient solution per square meter, providing a buffer of approximately 2-3 hours of water supply for the plants in the event of a pump failure, sufficient time for the crew to diagnose and repair the pump — the nutrient solution circulation pump, a 12 V DC diaphragm pump with a flow rate of 5 L/min and a power consumption of 25 W, the same Micropump model used for the battery cooling loop in Part 069, selected for its high reliability (MTBF of 50,000 hours) and its chemical compatibility with the nutrient solution — the pump's wetted parts are EPDM (ethylene propylene diene monomer) rubber and polypropylene, both of which are inert to the dissolved salts in the nutrient solution (the solution's pH of 5.8-6.2 and electrical conductivity of 1.5-3.0 mS/cm, corresponding to a total dissolved solids concentration of approximately 1,000-2,000 mg/L, are non-corrosive to these materials, and the pump's long-term exposure to the nutrient solution has been validated through a 5,000-hour continuous test in which the pump circulated the solution with no measurable degradation in the flow rate or the materials), and the crew can swap the pump with a spare in approximately 10 minutes — the pump is mounted on a quick-release bracket and connects to the nutrient solution manifold through push-to-connect fittings, a standardized interface that is used throughout the Atlantis pod's fluid systems and that allows any fluid component to be replaced without tools, a design feature that the crew training program emphasizes and that the crew practices during the pre-deployment simulation at Mojave, where they must replace a failed nutrient pump within 15 minutes while wearing the pod's standard-issue work gloves, a time that includes the isolation of the failed pump (closing two manual ball valves, one on the suction side and one on the discharge side, to prevent the nutrient solution from draining out of the manifold), the disconnection of the quick-connect fittings, the removal of the failed pump, the installation of the spare, the reconnection of the fittings, the opening of the isolation valves, and the restart of the pump, with the capillary mat providing the root-zone moisture buffer during the approximately 10-minute pump-off period, after which the flow is restored and the plants experience no detectable stress.

The LED lighting system that provides the photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) for the hydroponic crop growth is a custom-designed array of 300 light-emitting diodes per channel — 1,800 LEDs total across the six channels — arranged in two linear arrays (one on each side of the channel, angled inward at 30 degrees from the vertical to direct the light onto the plant canopy and to minimize the light that misses the plants and strikes the channel walls, where it is absorbed and converted to heat without contributing to photosynthesis), with each LED being a 3-watt surface-mount device (the Cree XLamp XP-G3 Photo Red S Line, with a peak wavelength of 660 nm and a full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) of 20 nm, and the Cree XLamp XP-E2 Royal Blue, with a peak wavelength of 450 nm and a FWHM of 20 nm), the ratio of red to blue LEDs being 4:1 for the vegetative growth phase — the 4:1 ratio is based on the extensive photobiology research that has been conducted since the 1990s on the effects of light spectrum on plant growth and development, initially driven by the NASA Advanced Life Support program's need for efficient, compact plant lighting for space missions, and subsequently commercialized by the indoor agriculture industry, with the 4:1 red-to-blue ratio being found to maximize the photosynthetic rate (measured by the net CO₂ assimilation rate, A_max, in μmol CO₂/m²/s) for a wide range of leafy green crops — lettuce (Lactuca sativa), kale (Brassica oleracea var. sabellica), spinach (Spinacia oleracea), and basil (Ocimum basilicum) — while minimizing the stem elongation and the leaf area expansion that occur under predominantly red light due to the shade-avoidance response (the phytochrome photoreceptor system, which senses the ratio of red (660 nm) to far-red (730 nm) light, triggers the shade-avoidance response when the R:FR ratio is low — which is the condition under a plant canopy, where the leaves above absorb the red light and transmit the far-red light, so a low R:FR ratio is interpreted by the plant as "I am being shaded by other plants," and the response is to allocate resources to stem elongation (to grow above the competitors) and to reduce the investment in leaf area (since the leaves would be shaded and thus less productive), a response that is undesirable in a hydroponic system where the goal is to maximize the harvestable leaf biomass, not the stem height, and the 4:1 red-to-blue ratio provides sufficient blue light to activate the cryptochrome photoreceptors, which suppress the shade-avoidance response and produce the compact, highly productive plant morphology that the Atlantis hydroponic system requires, with the additional benefit that the blue light stimulates the production of the secondary metabolites — anthocyanins, carotenoids, and flavonoids — that contribute to the color, flavor, and nutritional quality of the produce (the red coloration of red-leaf lettuce and the purple coloration of kale are produced by anthocyanins, whose synthesis is regulated by the cryptochrome and UV-A photoreceptors, and the blue light's activation of these photoreceptors increases the anthocyanin content by approximately 30-50 percent compared to plants grown under predominantly red light, per the studies by Stutte et al. (2009) at the Kennedy Space Center and by Li and Kubota (2009) at the University of Arizona's Controlled Environment Agriculture Center, both of which informed the Atlantis LED spectrum selection and confirmed the 4:1 ratio for the vegetative phase and the 7:1 ratio for the flowering phase — the higher red fraction during flowering promotes the transition from vegetative growth to reproductive development, which is regulated by the phytochrome system's perception of the photoperiod (the duration of the light period) and the R:FR ratio, and the 7:1 ratio provides a strong flowering signal for the fruiting crops (tomatoes and strawberries), accelerating the onset of flowering by approximately 5-7 days compared to the 4:1 vegetative ratio, and increasing the fruit set by approximately 15-20 percent, a yield improvement that the Atlantis crop model predicted and that the Mojave hydroponic laboratory — a 10 m² prototype of the Atlantis system, with the same LED arrays, the same NFT channels, and the same environmental control — validated through three successive 90-day crop cycles, with the measured yield of the tomato cultivar 'Red Robin' (a dwarf variety selected for its compact growth habit, which is essential in the Atlantis pod's limited vertical space — the LED arrays are mounted 300 mm above the channel surface, and the plant canopy is limited to 250 mm height, so the selected crops are all dwarf or compact varieties that can complete their growth cycle within this vertical constraint) being 15.3 ± 0.8 kg/m² for the 90-day cycle, within 2 percent of the crop model's predicted 15.0 kg/m², validating both the LED spectrum and the crop model for the Atlantis application.

The photosynthetically active photon flux density (PPFD) of 400 μmol/m²/s at the plant canopy — measured with a quantum sensor (Apogee MQ-500, which measures the PPFD in the 400-700 nm waveband with an accuracy of ±5 percent, traceable to the NIST standard for spectral irradiance) at 15 points across each channel's growing area, with the coefficient of variation across the 15 points being less than 10 percent (the LED array's spacing and the 30-degree inward angle are designed to provide uniform illumination, and the Mojave prototype measurements confirmed a uniformity of 8.2 percent, meeting the less-than-10-percent specification) — provides a daily light integral (DLI) of DLI = PPFD × photoperiod × 3,600/1,000,000 = 400 × 16 × 0.0036 = 23.0 mol/m²/day, where the photoperiod is 16 hours — the 16-hour photoperiod being selected as the standard for long-day plants (which require more than approximately 12 hours of light per day to remain in the vegetative growth phase) and for day-neutral plants (which are insensitive to the photoperiod), with the majority of the Atlantis crops — lettuce, kale, basil, and microgreens — being long-day or day-neutral, while the fruiting crops — tomatoes and strawberries — are day-neutral and will flower and fruit under the 16-hour photoperiod without requiring a specific short-day or long-day regime, a simplification that avoids the need for multiple lighting schedules and the associated control complexity. The DLI of 23 mol/m²/day is at the high end of the recommended range for leafy green production in controlled environments — the optimal DLI for lettuce is approximately 14-17 mol/m²/day, and for kale, approximately 20-25 mol/m²/day, per the Cornell University Controlled Environment Agriculture guidelines, so the 23 mol/m²/day is optimized for the kale (the primary calorie crop, due to its high yield of 3.0 kg/m² and its nutritional density — kale is rich in vitamins A, C, and K, as well as calcium, iron, and fiber, and its dark green leaves are a psychologically appealing addition to the crew's diet, providing visual variety and the satisfying crunch that the ISS crew has identified as the most-missed sensory quality of fresh food — the "crunch factor," a term that the Dolittle register encountered in the ISS dietary survey data and that informed the selection of crops with textural variety, including the crisp lettuce, the chewy kale, the juicy tomatoes, the sweet strawberries, and the spicy microgreens (a mix of radish, mustard, and arugula microgreens, harvested at 10 days after seeding, when the cotyledons are fully expanded and the first true leaves are emerging, providing a concentrated source of flavor and phytonutrients at a yield of 1.5 kg/m² — the microgreens are grown in a dedicated channel, with a 10-day cycle (seeds are sown every 5 days, and each channel half — 2.5 m² — is harvested every 5 days, ensuring a continuous supply of approximately 0.75 kg of microgreens per day, which the crew uses to add flavor and visual appeal to the otherwise monotonous dry-food meals, a culinary improvement that the crew's food satisfaction survey — administered weekly as part of the THALIA ROOK Cascade psychological monitoring protocol — consistently rates as one of the top three factors contributing to the crew's overall satisfaction with the pod's food system, alongside the fresh strawberries (the strawberry cultivar 'Seascape,' a treat that the crew savors and that the Dolittle register identified as the single most psychologically valuable crop in the Atlantis system, based on the ISS Veggie experience, where the crew's reaction to the first zinnia flower that bloomed on the station in 2016 — after a challenging growth period in which the plants suffered from fungal infection and water stress, and the crew member embraces, with the strawberries being the "zinnia moment" of the Atlantis food system, the crop that the crew most looks forward to and that provides the strongest positive emotional response, a benefit that the THALIA ROOK Cascade data quantifies as a 28 percent reduction in the crew's stress score on the POMS tension-anxiety subscale during the weeks when the strawberries are in production, compared to the weeks when only leafy greens are available.

The Hoagland's modified nutrient solution — the standard hydroponic nutrient formulation, originally developed by Dennis Hoagland and Daniel Arnon at the University of California, Berkeley, in 1938, and modified by subsequent researchers to optimize the micronutrient concentrations for specific crops and growing conditions — is prepared in a 200-liter polyethylene reservoir, with the macronutrients (N, P, K, Ca, Mg, and S) supplied as the following salts, dissolved in deionized water from the forward osmosis system (Part 066): calcium nitrate tetrahydrate, Ca(NO₃)₂·4H₂O, at a concentration of 0.95 g/L, providing 130 mg/L of nitrate-N and 185 mg/L of Ca (the nitrate-N is the primary nitrogen source for the plants, and the Atlantis system uses exclusively nitrate-N, rather than the ammonium-N (NH₄⁺) that is sometimes included in hydroponic formulations to reduce the pH drift (nitrate uptake by the plant roots releases hydroxide ions (OH⁻), which raises the solution pH, while ammonium uptake releases protons (H⁺), which lowers the pH, and a balanced supply of both forms can stabilize the pH without the need for external pH adjustment, but the Atlantis system opts for the simpler nitrate-only formulation, relying on the automatic pH dosing system to correct the pH drift, rather than introducing the risk of ammonium toxicity — ammonium can be toxic to plants at concentrations above approximately 10 mg/L, causing root damage and reduced growth, and the closed-loop recirculation of the nutrient solution in the Atlantis NFT system would cause the ammonium concentration to fluctuate as the plants selectively absorbed the different nitrogen forms, a dynamic that the simple nitrate-only system avoids entirely, providing a stable, predictable nutrient environment for the plants, a design decision that the Atlantis horticulturist made based on the extensive controlled-environment agriculture literature that documents the superior crop quality and the reduced management complexity of the nitrate-only nutrient solutions for recirculating hydroponic systems); potassium nitrate, KNO₃, at 0.45 g/L, providing 60 mg/L of nitrate-N and 175 mg/L of K (potassium is essential for the plant's stomatal regulation — the guard cells that control the opening and closing of the stomata, the pores on the leaf surface through which CO₂ enters and water vapor exits, respond to the potassium ion concentration, and a potassium deficiency causes the stomata to remain partially closed, reducing the photosynthetic rate and the crop yield, a nutrient deficiency that is among the most common in hydroponic systems and that the Atlantis nutrient management protocol addresses by monitoring the potassium concentration weekly using a handheld ion-selective electrode — the Horiba LAQUAtwin K-11, which measures the potassium concentration with an accuracy of ±10 percent, sufficient for the Atlantis application, where the target K concentration is 150-200 mg/L and the monitoring frequency of once per week ensures that any deviation from the target is detected and corrected before it affects the crop yield — and by replenishing the potassium through the addition of potassium sulfate, K₂SO₄, to the nutrient reservoir, a salt that provides potassium without adding nitrogen (which is already supplied by the calcium nitrate and the potassium nitrate) and without adding chloride (which can accumulate to toxic levels in recirculating hydroponic systems if a potassium chloride salt is used — the Atlantis system uses exclusively sulfate-based micronutrient salts to avoid the chloride accumulation, a detail that the Atlantis horticulturist specified based on the experience of the commercial hydroponic industry, where chloride toxicity is a known problem in closed-loop systems with high evapotranspiration rates, as the chloride is not absorbed by the plants in proportion to its concentration in the solution and gradually accumulates, reaching toxic levels of 200-400 mg/L after several months of operation, at which point the crop exhibits the characteristic chloride toxicity symptoms — leaf margin burn, interveinal chlorosis, and reduced growth — which the Atlantis system avoids by using only sulfate salts, a design feature that the Mojave prototype validated through a 270-day (three 90-day crop cycles) continuous operation without a complete nutrient solution change — only a 10 percent weekly replenishment of the consumed nutrients and a 10 percent weekly top-up of the water lost to evapotranspiration — and the chloride concentration, measured monthly by ion chromatography, remained below 50 mg/L throughout the test, confirming the effectiveness of the sulfate-only micronutrient strategy); monopotassium phosphate, KH₂PO₄, at 0.25 g/L, providing 50 mg/L of P and 65 mg/L of K (phosphorus is the energy currency of the plant cell, a component of ATP (adenosine triphosphate), the molecule that stores and transfers the chemical energy from photosynthesis to the biosynthetic reactions that produce the plant's biomass, and a phosphorus deficiency causes the characteristic purple coloration of the leaves — the anthocyanins that are normally masked by the green chlorophyll become visible when the chlorophyll synthesis is impaired by the phosphorus deficiency — a visual symptom that the Atlantis crew is trained to recognize and that triggers an immediate nutrient solution analysis and, if confirmed, the addition of phosphoric acid, H₃PO₄, to the nutrient reservoir, which also lowers the pH (the phosphoric acid contributes protons), a dual-purpose corrective action that the Atlantis nutrient management protocol specifies for phosphorus deficiency); and magnesium sulfate heptahydrate, MgSO₄·7H₂O, at 0.50 g/L, providing 50 mg/L of Mg and 65 mg/L of S (magnesium is the central atom of the chlorophyll molecule, and a magnesium deficiency causes the interveinal chlorosis — the yellowing of the leaf tissue between the veins, while the veins themselves remain green — that is among the most recognizable nutrient deficiency symptoms and that the crew monitors visually during the daily plant inspection, a task that takes approximately 10 minutes and that involves examining the leaves of each plant for discoloration, spotting, curling, or insect damage — the Atlantis hydroponic system maintains a strict biological isolation, the seeds are surface-sterilized with a 1 percent sodium hypochlorite solution before sowing, the nutrient solution is passed through a 0.2 μm filter before recirculation (the same polysulfone hollow-fiber membrane used for the condensate recycling, to prevent the spread of any waterborne pathogens — Pythium spp., Fusarium spp., and Phytophthora spp. are the most common root pathogens in hydroponic systems, and their introduction into the Atlantis pod would be catastrophic for the crop yield, potentially causing a complete crop failure that would eliminate 27 percent of the crew's caloric intake and, more importantly, the psychological benefit of the fresh food, a scenario that the Atlantis biosecurity protocol is designed to prevent through the seed sterilization, the nutrient solution filtration, the positive-pressure ventilation of the hydroponic area (the air is drawn from the hydroponic area and exhausted to the pod's main ventilation system, so any airborne pathogens that might enter the pod through the airlock or the hatch are less likely to reach the plants, as the airflow is from the "clean" areas (the crew quarters and the galley) toward the "dirty" areas (the equipment bay and the airlock), a directional airflow that the Atlantis ventilation system maintains through the careful sizing of the supply and return air ducts and the adjustment of the damper positions, a design that the Kiddo register reviewed and that the Torvalds register verified through a computational fluid dynamics simulation of the pod's ventilation patterns, confirming that the airflow direction is maintained under all operating conditions, including during the airlock cycling, when the pressure transients temporarily disrupt the airflow — but the disruption lasts only approximately 60 seconds, the duration of the airlock venting sequence, and the HEPA filtration in the ventilation system removes any airborne particles that might be drawn into the pod during this brief period, maintaining the biological isolation of the hydroponic area and protecting the crops from pathogen introduction.

The electrical conductivity (EC) of the nutrient solution is maintained at 1.5-3.0 mS/cm, depending on the crop — the lower end of the range (1.5-2.0 mS/cm) for the leafy greens (lettuce, kale, and microgreens), which are sensitive to high salt concentrations (the osmotic pressure of the nutrient solution affects the plant's ability to absorb water — the water potential of the root zone must be lower (more negative) than the water potential of the soil solution for water to flow from the soil into the roots, and if the nutrient solution's osmotic pressure is too high, the water flow is reduced or reversed, causing the plant to wilt even though the roots are submerged in water, a condition known as "physiological drought," which is a common problem in hydroponic systems with excessive nutrient concentrations, and which the Atlantis system avoids by maintaining the EC within the crop-specific ranges and by measuring the EC continuously using a conductivity probe (the Atlas Scientific EZO-EC, with an accuracy of ±2 percent and a response time of less than 1 second, connected to the pod's control system through an I²C interface, and the control system adjusts the nutrient dosing pumps — peristaltic pumps that inject concentrated nutrient stock solutions into the recirculating flow — to maintain the EC setpoint, a closed-loop control that operates at a 10-minute interval, sufficient to compensate for the plants' nutrient uptake, which depletes the solution's nutrient concentration by approximately 5-10 percent per day, depending on the crop growth stage and the environmental conditions, a depletion rate that the control system tracks and that the nutrient stock solutions — each at 100 times the target concentration, stored in 5-liter collapsible bags that are replaced every 30 days, the bags being made from a multi-layer laminate of polyethylene and aluminum foil that provides an oxygen and light barrier, preventing the degradation of the nutrient salts and the growth of algae or bacteria in the concentrated solutions, a packaging technology that was developed for the medical intravenous fluid industry and that the Atlantis horticulturist adapted for the nutrient delivery system, a cross-industry technology transfer that the Kiddo register identified as a valuable innovation and that the ZIRCONIA analysis estimated would save approximately $2,500 per year in nutrient solution costs compared to the alternative of mixing the nutrient solution from dry salts on-site, a task that would require the crew to spend approximately 2 hours per week measuring and mixing the salts, time that is better spent on the mission's scientific objectives, a cost-benefit that the Torvalds register validated and that the pre-packaged nutrient stock solution approach was approved for the Atlantis system.

The pH of the nutrient solution is maintained at 5.8-6.2 — the optimal range for the availability of the essential plant nutrients, as described by the classic chart of nutrient availability versus pH (the "nutrient availability diagram" that appears in every hydroponics textbook, showing that at pH 5.8-6.2, all of the essential nutrients — N, P, K, Ca, Mg, S, Fe, Mn, B, Zn, Cu, and Mo — are at their maximum availability, with the exception of molybdenum, which is more available at higher pH, but molybdenum is required in such small quantities (0.01 mg/L) that its reduced availability at pH 6.0 does not limit the plant growth, and the other micronutrients — iron, manganese, boron, zinc, and copper — become increasingly unavailable as the pH rises above 6.5, due to the precipitation of the metal hydroxides and the formation of insoluble phosphate complexes, while the macronutrients — phosphorus and calcium — begin to precipitate as calcium phosphate (Ca₃(PO₄)₂) at pH values above 6.5, removing both the calcium and the phosphorus from the solution and causing the deficiency symptoms described above, a precipitation that the Atlantis system prevents by the automatic pH control system, which uses a pH sensor (the Atlas Scientific EZO-pH, with the same I²C interface and the same accuracy as the EC sensor) and two peristaltic dosing pumps — one for the pH-down solution (0.1 M phosphoric acid, H₃PO₄) and one for the pH-up solution (0.1 M potassium hydroxide, KOH), the two solutions being selected because their counterions — phosphate and potassium — are essential plant nutrients, so the pH adjustment simultaneously supplies the plants with additional phosphorus or potassium, a dual-purpose dosing strategy that reduces the total salt load on the nutrient solution (compared to using nitric acid for pH down and potassium silicate for pH up, which would add nitrogen and silicon — the silicon is beneficial but not essential, and the additional nitrogen could unbalance the nitrate-to-ammonium ratio, with the potential for ammonium toxicity as discussed above, so the phosphoric acid and the potassium hydroxide are the optimal pH adjustment agents for the nitrate-only Atlantis formulation) and that the Mojave prototype testing validated through the 270-day continuous operation, during which the pH was maintained within the 5.8-6.2 range for 99.2 percent of the time, with the remaining 0.8 percent being brief excursions to 5.6 or 6.4 that the control system corrected within one dosing cycle (10 minutes), and that had no measurable effect on the crop yield or quality.

The CO₂ enrichment to 1,000 ppm — approximately 2.5 times the ambient atmospheric CO₂ concentration — is achieved by diverting a portion of the crew's exhaled CO₂, which the atmospheric management system (Part 071) would otherwise remove through the molecular sieve scrubber, into the hydroponic area's ventilation duct, where it is mixed with the recirculating air and delivered to the plant canopy through a perforated distribution tube, a 25 mm diameter PVC pipe with 3 mm diameter holes spaced every 100 mm along the length of each channel, providing a uniform CO₂ concentration of 1,000 ± 100 ppm across the entire growing area, as verified by a CO₂ sensor (the Senseair K30, a non-dispersive infrared (NDIR) sensor with an accuracy of ±30 ppm and a response time of 20 seconds, mounted at the canopy level in the center of each channel and connected to the pod's control system, which modulates the CO₂ diversion valve to maintain the 1,000 ppm setpoint, a control loop that operates at a 1-minute interval — the CO₂ concentration in the hydroponic area changes relatively slowly, as the plants' photosynthetic CO₂ uptake at the 400 μmol/m²/s PPFD and the 30 m² growing area is
