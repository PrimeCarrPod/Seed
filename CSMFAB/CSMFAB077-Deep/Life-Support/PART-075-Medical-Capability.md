## 1. HYPERBARIC CHAMBER AND DECOMPRESSION SICKNESS TREATMENT

The medical capability of the Atlantis-class subsea habitation module is anchored — in both the physical and operational senses — by the twin-lock hyperbaric chamber, a cylindrical pressure vessel of 2.5 meters internal length and 1.8 meters internal diameter, fabricated from the same LBFRP-001 lonsdaleite-reinforced basalt fiber composite that forms the pod's primary pressure hull, and rated for an internal pressure of 6 atmospheres absolute (approximately 608 kilopascals gauge pressure, corresponding to a seawater depth of approximately 50 meters, or a total absolute pressure of approximately 7 atmospheres when accounting for the 1-atmosphere ambient pressure at which the chamber is operated when the pod's interior is at its nominal 1.0 atmosphere), a pressure rating that exceeds the maximum treatment pressure specified by the United States Navy Diving Manual (Revision 7, NAVSEA 0910-LP-115-1922) for the treatment of severe decompression sickness and arterial gas embolism — the two most life-threatening manifestations of decompression illness, collectively referred to as DCI — which is 2.8 atmospheres absolute (18 meters of seawater equivalent) for the administration of Treatment Table 6, the standard hyperbaric oxygen protocol for severe DCI, and which also accommodates the U.S. Navy Treatment Table 6A (maximum pressure 6.0 atmospheres, 50 meters equivalent) for cases of arterial gas embolism that do not respond to Treatment Table 6 at 2.8 atmospheres, a protocol that involves an initial pressurization to 6.0 atmospheres on air for a period not exceeding 30 minutes (extended beyond 30 minutes only in cases of life-threatening neurological symptoms that fail to improve at 2.8 atmospheres), followed by staged decompression with oxygen breathing periods at 2.8 atmospheres, 1.9 atmospheres, and surface pressure over a total treatment duration of up to 8 hours, during which the patient breathes 100 percent oxygen through a built-in breathing system (BIBS) mask — a demand regulator mask that delivers oxygen on inhalation and exhausts the exhaled gas outside the chamber to prevent oxygen enrichment of the chamber atmosphere, which, at elevated partial pressures, creates a fire hazard that has been responsible for multiple fatal hyperbaric chamber fires in the history of diving medicine, most notably the 1980 accident at the German Aerospace Center (DLR) in which an oxygen-enriched atmosphere at 5.0 atmospheres ignited, killing two occupants, an incident that directly informs the Atlantis hyperbaric chamber's design, which incorporates the mandatory fire safety features specified by NFPA 99 (Health Care Facilities Code) Chapter 14 (Hyperbaric Facilities), including the prohibition of all ignition sources within the chamber, the use of fire-retardant materials for all interior furnishings (ASTM E84 Class A flame spread rating, smoke developed index below 50), the continuous monitoring of chamber oxygen concentration with an alarm setpoint of 23.5 percent (the maximum safe oxygen concentration specified by NFPA 99, providing a 2.5 percent margin below the 25 percent threshold at which materials that are normally flame-retardant at 21 percent oxygen begin to exhibit significantly increased flammability, per the limiting oxygen index test methodology of ASTM D2863), and the installation of a deluge fire suppression system that can flood the chamber interior with water from the pod's fire main within 15 seconds of activation.

The twin-lock configuration of the chamber provides two interconnected compartments — a main treatment lock of 1.5 meters length and a smaller entry lock of 1.0 meter length, separated by an internal pressure-rated door — that enables medical personnel to enter and exit the chamber during a treatment without interrupting the patient's pressure exposure, a capability that is essential for the management of severe DCI cases in which the patient's condition may deteriorate during treatment and require the administration of intravenous fluids, emergency medications through the chamber's medical lock — a small pass-through port of 150 millimeters diameter that can transfer medical supplies, medication ampules, syringes, and diagnostic equipment between the ambient-pressure exterior and the pressurized interior without altering the chamber's pressure — or advanced airway management including endotracheal intubation, which, at hyperbaric pressures, involves specific considerations related to the expansion of the endotracheal tube cuff, which must be filled with sterile water or saline rather than air to prevent cuff overinflation and potential tracheal mucosal injury during the decompression phase, when the air in a conventional air-filled cuff would expand according to Boyle's Law (P₁V₁ = P₂V₂), potentially increasing the cuff volume by a factor of 2.8 when ascending from 2.8 atmospheres to surface pressure, a volume increase that could exceed the elastic compliance limit of the tracheal wall and cause pressure necrosis of the tracheal mucosa, a well-documented complication of hyperbaric intubation that the Atlantis medical protocols explicitly address through a checklist-based procedure for hyperbaric airway management adapted from the Undersea and Hyperbaric Medical Society's Hyperbaric Nursing Guidelines. The entry lock also serves as the staging area for saturation decompression in the event that one or more pod occupants have been exposed to elevated ambient pressure — for example, during an emergency escape scenario in which the pod's internal pressure is equalized with the external seawater pressure at 45 meters (5.5 atmospheres absolute) to permit the crew to exit through the moon pool into the water column for transfer to a rescue submersible or surface vessel — and require a gradual decompression to surface pressure over a period of 2 to 3 days, during which the crew members would "live" in the hyperbaric chamber, breathing the chamber atmosphere (which is maintained at the appropriate decompression pressure, with oxygen partial pressure controlled to 0.3 to 0.5 atmospheres to prevent both hypoxia and oxygen toxicity) and receiving food, water, and medical care through the entry lock's pass-through ports, a capability that transforms the twin-lock chamber from a treatment device into a survival habitat, the same dual-function role that saturation diving chambers serve on offshore oil and gas platforms, where divers live at the working depth's pressure for weeks at a time to avoid the inefficiency of daily compression and decompression cycles.

The hyperbaric chamber's life support system is independent of the pod's main environmental control system — a deliberate design choice that ensures the chamber remains fully functional even if the pod's primary life support is compromised by a fire, flood, or atmospheric contamination event, the very scenarios that might necessitate the chamber's use — and consists of a dedicated carbon dioxide scrubber (soda lime, 5-kilogram canister with a CO₂ absorption capacity of approximately 150 liters of CO₂ per kilogram of soda lime, providing approximately 12 hours of operational duration for a single occupant at a metabolic CO₂ production rate of approximately 0.5 liters per minute, or approximately 6 hours for two occupants, with the canister being replaceable via the medical lock without depressurizing the chamber), a dedicated oxygen supply (two J-size medical oxygen cylinders of 6,800 liters capacity each, stored external to the chamber and connected via stainless steel tubing with check valves at the chamber penetration to prevent backflow, providing approximately 48 hours of oxygen for two occupants at the Therapeutic Oxygen Administration rate of 15 liters per minute per person, as specified by the U.S. Navy Treatment Table 6 oxygen breathing requirements), a temperature control system (a hydronic heating and cooling loop circulating temperature-controlled water through a heat exchanger within the chamber, capable of maintaining the chamber temperature between 18°C and 30°C despite the adiabatic heating that accompanies pressurization — compression of the chamber atmosphere from 1.0 to 6.0 atmospheres would, absent active cooling, raise the temperature by approximately 70°C per the ideal gas law adiabatic compression relationship T₂/T₁ = (P₂/P₁)^((γ-1)/γ), where γ for air is approximately 1.4, meaning that the compression from 1 to 6 atmospheres would theoretically increase the absolute temperature by a factor of 6^(0.4/1.4) ≈ 1.7, corresponding to a temperature increase from 20°C (293 K) to approximately 225°C (498 K), a temperature that would be lethal within minutes if not actively controlled — a theoretical maximum that in practice is substantially mitigated by the thermal mass of the chamber structure and the heat capacity of the pressurization gas but that nonetheless requires the active cooling system to maintain a comfortable and safe internal temperature during all phases of treatment), and communication equipment including a two-way intercom and a closed-circuit video camera that enables the crew medical officer — who may not be inside the chamber with the patient — to visually monitor the patient's condition, observe the readings of the patient-worn pulse oximeter and electrocardiogram electrodes that are passed through the medical lock to external monitoring equipment, and communicate with the patient to assess neurological status, pain level, and subjective symptoms, all of which are documented in the patient's treatment record using the standardized U.S. Navy Diving Casualty Report form (NAVMED 6470/1), which the Atlantis program has adopted as its medical documentation standard for all diving-related injuries.

## 2. DIAGNOSTIC IMAGING, LABORATORY, AND MONITORING EQUIPMENT

The diagnostic imaging capability aboard the Atlantis pod centers on a portable ultrasound system with a phased-array transducer probe operating at a frequency range of 2 to 5 megahertz, a configuration that provides a penetration depth of up to 25 centimeters — sufficient to image the liver, kidneys, spleen, gallbladder, bladder, uterus, and the major blood vessels of the abdomen and thorax in an average adult, with the 2 megahertz frequency providing the deepest penetration at the cost of reduced spatial resolution (approximately 1-2 millimeters axial resolution) and the 5 megahertz frequency providing improved resolution (approximately 0.5-1 millimeter) at a reduced penetration depth of approximately 10 centimeters, suitable for superficial structures such as the thyroid gland, testicles, and peripheral blood vessels — and that supports the Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma (FAST) examination protocol, a standardized four-view ultrasound examination (subxiphoid cardiac view to assess for pericardial effusion, right upper quadrant to assess for intraperitoneal fluid around the liver and right kidney, left upper quadrant to assess for fluid around the spleen and left kidney, and suprapubic view to assess for fluid in the pelvis) that can be performed by a trained operator in under five minutes to identify life-threatening internal hemorrhage, pneumothorax, and pericardial tamponade — three conditions that account for the majority of preventable trauma deaths in the pre-hospital and remote environments that are most analogous to the Atlantis pod's medical context, where evacuation to definitive surgical care is impossible during a crustal displacement event and the crew medical officer must manage traumatic injuries with the resources available within the pod. The ultrasound system includes a linear-array transducer (7 to 12 megahertz) for high-resolution imaging of superficial structures including the musculoskeletal system (tendons, ligaments, muscles, and joints — relevant for the diagnosis of strains, sprains, and tears that are common in the physically demanding environment of a subsea habitat, where crew members perform maintenance tasks in awkward postures within confined spaces), the vascular system (carotid arteries, jugular veins, and peripheral vessels — relevant for the guidance of intravenous line placement in patients with difficult venous access, a common challenge in dehydrated or hypotensive trauma patients), and the eyes (assessment of retinal detachment, vitreous hemorrhage, and elevated intracranial pressure via optic nerve sheath diameter measurement, the latter being a non-invasive surrogate for intracranial pressure monitoring that is particularly valuable in the absence of a CT scanner or invasive intracranial pressure monitor, as an optic nerve sheath diameter exceeding 5 millimeters, measured 3 millimeters posterior to the globe, correlates with intracranial pressures exceeding 20 mmHg with a sensitivity of approximately 90 percent and a specificity of approximately 85 percent based on the meta-analysis of optic nerve sheath diameter sonography studies published in the Annals of Emergency Medicine).

The electrocardiographic monitoring capability is provided by a 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) system that records the cardiac electrical activity from ten electrodes placed on the patient's limbs and chest — the standard 12-lead configuration that provides the six limb leads (I, II, III, aVR, aVL, aVF) and the six precordial leads (V1 through V6), collectively providing a comprehensive view of the heart's electrical axis, rhythm, conduction intervals, and the presence or absence of myocardial ischemia, injury, or infarction as indicated by ST-segment elevation or depression, T-wave inversion, and the development of pathological Q waves — and that transmits the ECG data to the pod's medical display station, a ruggedized 24-inch monitor mounted in the medical bay that runs the U.S. Food and Drug Administration-cleared ECG interpretation software developed by the University of Glasgow, which provides automated rhythm analysis and interval measurements (PR interval, QRS duration, QT interval corrected for heart rate using the Bazett formula QTc = QT/√RR) with a sensitivity for acute myocardial infarction detection exceeding 95 percent and a specificity of approximately 85 percent, based on the validation study conducted with the European Society of Cardiology's ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction database, and that flags abnormalities for review by the crew medical officer and, if the telemedicine link is active, by the on-call cardiologist at the Aegis C Medical Operations Center. An automated external defibrillator (AED) is co-located with the ECG system in the medical bay, programmed with the American Heart Association's 2020 Advanced Cardiac Life Support (ACLS) algorithms for the management of ventricular fibrillation and pulseless ventricular tachycardia — the two "shockable" rhythms that account for approximately 25 percent of out-of-hospital cardiac arrests and for which early defibrillation (within 3 to 5 minutes of collapse) improves survival to hospital discharge from less than 5 percent to approximately 50 to 70 percent, a statistic that is particularly relevant to the Atlantis pod, where the crew medical officer can initiate defibrillation within 1 to 2 minutes of collapse given the pod's compact dimensions (the maximum distance from any crew location to the medical bay is approximately 10 meters) — and equipped with adhesive defibrillation pads that are pre-connected to the AED to minimize the time from rhythm analysis to shock delivery, a human-factors design feature that the U.S. military's Tactical Combat Casualty Care (TCCC) guidelines identify as a key determinant of defibrillation success in high-stress environments.

The point-of-care laboratory capability is anchored by the i-STAT handheld blood analyzer, a device developed by Abbott Laboratories and widely deployed across the U.S. military, NASA spaceflight missions (where it has been used on the International Space Station since 2006 for crew health monitoring), and remote clinical environments, which accepts disposable cartridges — each containing microfabricated biosensors for a specific panel of analytes — and delivers quantitative results within 2 to 10 minutes from a sample volume of 65 to 95 microliters of whole blood, a volume that can be obtained from a fingerstick capillary sample without the need for venipuncture, reducing the skill level required for sample collection and the risk of complications (hematoma, infection, nerve injury) associated with venous blood draws. The Atlantis pod's i-STAT cartridge inventory includes the Chem8+ panel (sodium, potassium, chloride, ionized calcium, glucose, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, hematocrit, hemoglobin — the essential chemistry parameters for the assessment of renal function, electrolyte balance, and glycemic status, which are particularly relevant in the context of dehydration, heat stress from the pod's environmental control system malfunction, and acute illness including gastroenteritis, the most common infectious disease in confined environments per the World Health Organization's Guide to Ship Sanitation), the CG4+ panel (pH, partial pressure of carbon dioxide, partial pressure of oxygen, bicarbonate, base excess, oxygen saturation — the arterial blood gas parameters that are essential for the assessment of respiratory function and acid-base balance, particularly relevant for the management of decompression sickness, in which arterial gas embolism and venous gas bubbles can impair pulmonary gas exchange and produce hypoxemia, and for the titration of oxygen therapy during hyperbaric treatment, where excessive oxygen partial pressures can produce central nervous system oxygen toxicity manifested as seizures), the Troponin I cartridge (cardiac troponin I, a protein released into the bloodstream following myocardial cell death, with elevated levels indicating myocardial infarction, myocarditis, or cardiac contusion — the latter being a potential consequence of blunt chest trauma from debris impact during a turbidite event), and the coagulation cartridge (prothrombin time/international normalized ratio, activated partial thromboplastin time — for the monitoring of coagulation status in trauma patients and in patients receiving anticoagulant medications from the pod's pharmacy), with a minimum inventory of 10 cartridges of each type for the 90-day mission plus an additional 10 cartridges of the Chem8+ and CG4+ panels as backup, reflecting the higher utilization rate expected for these panels, which would be performed weekly on all crew members as part of the routine health monitoring protocol and daily on any crew member diagnosed with decompression sickness, electrolyte imbalance, or acute illness.

The surgical capability of the Atlantis medical bay is designed around the "damage control surgery" philosophy developed by the U.S. military during the conflicts in Iraq and Afghanistan, which recognizes that in austere environments where evacuation to definitive surgical care is delayed by hours to days — or, in the Atlantis pod's crustal displacement scenario, by days to weeks — the surgical objective is not the definitive repair of all injuries but rather the control of hemorrhage, the prevention of contamination (through the closure or diversion of perforated hollow viscera), and the temporary stabilization of fractures and soft tissue injuries, with definitive repair deferred until the patient can be evacuated to a properly equipped surgical facility. The surgical instrument set — procured from the U.S. Army's Medical Materiel Development Activity (USAMMDA) Forward Surgical Team equipment list, adapted for the Atlantis pod's specific medical threat assessment — includes the instruments required for the following procedures, which were identified through a Delphi consensus process involving three board-certified emergency physicians, two trauma surgeons, and the Aegis C Medical Director as the surgical interventions most likely to be necessary and feasible in the Atlantis pod environment: open appendectomy (for acute appendicitis, which has an annual incidence of approximately 1 per 1,000 person-years in the general adult population and which, without surgical intervention, progresses to perforation in approximately 20 to 30 percent of cases, with a mortality rate of approximately 5 to 10 percent for perforated appendicitis managed non-operatively with antibiotics alone versus less than 0.1 percent for uncomplicated appendicitis treated with timely appendectomy, making appendectomy the highest-priority surgical capability for any isolated, medically self-sufficient community), tube thoracostomy (chest tube insertion, for the management of pneumothorax — either spontaneous, which has an annual incidence of approximately 10 per 100,000 person-years, or traumatic, which is a common consequence of blunt chest trauma — and hemothorax, with the procedure involving the insertion of a 28 to 36 French thoracostomy tube through the fourth or fifth intercostal space in the anterior axillary line, connected to an underwater seal drainage system that permits lung re-expansion while preventing the re-accumulation of air or blood in the pleural space, a procedure that the crew medical officer can perform using local anesthesia with 1 percent lidocaine and that, when performed correctly, is life-saving in cases of tension pneumothorax, where the progressive accumulation of air in the pleural space under pressure causes mediastinal shift, compression of the contralateral lung and great vessels, and cardiovascular collapse within minutes to hours), wound exploration and debridement with layered closure (for the management of traumatic lacerations, which are the most common injury in physically demanding occupational environments and which, in the marine environment, carry an elevated risk of infection from Vibrio species — particularly Vibrio vulnificus, which is endemic in warm coastal waters and which can cause rapidly progressive wound infections with a mortality rate exceeding 50 percent in immunocompromised individuals, a risk that is mitigated by the Atlantis pod's location in the cold temperate waters of Monterey Bay, where water temperatures of 10-15°C suppress Vibrio growth, but that remains relevant for the Osa Peninsula site in tropical waters of 25-28°C), and escharotomy or fasciotomy (for the management of compartment syndrome, which can develop following prolonged limb compression from trapped debris during a turbidite impact event, as elevated intra-compartmental pressures exceeding 30 mmHg impair capillary perfusion and lead to irreversible muscle and nerve ischemia within 6 to 8 hours, a timeframe that may be exceeded before the Charlemagne rescue fleet can reach the pod, making the crew medical officer's ability to perform a fasciotomy a potentially limb-saving — if not life-saving, given the risk of rhabdomyolysis-induced acute kidney injury from the release of myoglobin from necrotic muscle tissue into the systemic circulation — surgical procedure).

The surgical instrument set is complemented by a portable X-ray system with a digital flat-panel detector — a technology that replaces the chemical film processing of traditional radiography with a reusable detector panel that converts X-ray photons directly into electrical charge via an amorphous selenium photoconductor layer and transmits the resulting digital image to the medical display station within 5 seconds of exposure, eliminating the need for a darkroom, chemical developers, and film storage, all of which are impractical in the confined, humidity-controlled environment of a subsea habitat — that provides diagnostic-quality images of the chest (for the confirmation of endotracheal tube placement, central venous catheter tip position, and the assessment of pneumothorax, hemothorax, and pulmonary edema), abdomen (for the detection of free intraperitoneal air indicating hollow viscus perforation, a surgical emergency that requires immediate laparotomy), and extremities (for the diagnosis and characterization of fractures and dislocations, which account for approximately 40 percent of all injuries in military and industrial settings and for which the pod's supply of splinting materials — SAM splints, fiberglass casting tape, and prefabricated wrist and ankle orthoses — provides the capability for closed reduction and immobilization, with open reduction and internal fixation being deferred to definitive surgical care following evacuation), all while exposing the patient and the operator to radiation doses that are well below the annual occupational limits established by the International Commission on Radiological Protection (20 millisieverts per year averaged over five years, with no single year exceeding 50 millisieverts), with the typical chest radiograph delivering an effective dose of approximately 0.1 millisievert — equivalent to approximately 10 days of natural background radiation at sea level — and the typical extremity radiograph delivering approximately 0.001 millisievert.

## 3. PHARMACEUTICAL FORMULARY AND TELEMEDICINE ARCHITECTURE

The Atlantis pod's pharmaceutical formulary comprises 120 distinct medications organized into nine therapeutic categories — antibiotics (including broad-spectrum agents: ceftriaxone, a third-generation cephalosporin with activity against gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria including the marine Vibrio species; doxycycline, a tetracycline-class antibiotic with activity against atypical bacteria including Rickettsia and the malaria prophylaxis indication relevant for the Osa Peninsula site where Plasmodium vivax malaria is endemic; metronidazole, for anaerobic coverage including Clostridium species that may be present in contaminated traumatic wounds; ciprofloxacin, a fluoroquinolone for the treatment of gram-negative infections including Pseudomonas aeruginosa, which is ubiquitous in freshwater and marine environments and which can cause severe infections in immunocompromised individuals; and amoxicillin-clavulanate, for the management of upper respiratory tract infections, dental infections, and animal bites — the latter being a consideration for the Osa Peninsula site, where encounters with terrestrial wildlife, including the venomous fer-de-lance snake (Bothrops asper) and the American crocodile (Crocodylus acutus), are possible during shore excursions), analgesics (including morphine sulfate for severe pain — administered intravenously at a dose of 2 to 5 milligrams every 10 to 15 minutes, titrated to effect, with a maximum dose of 10 milligrams per hour, and with naloxone 0.4 to 2 milligrams available as a reversal agent in the event of opioid-induced respiratory depression, defined as a respiratory rate below 8 breaths per minute or oxygen saturation below 90 percent despite supplemental oxygen — and ketorolac tromethamine, a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug for moderate pain that avoids the respiratory depression, sedation, and constipation associated with opioid analgesics, making it particularly suitable for the management of musculoskeletal pain from the physically demanding tasks of underwater habitat maintenance, where crew alertness and physical capability must not be compromised by sedating medications), local anesthetics (1 percent lidocaine with and without epinephrine 1:100,000 — the addition of epinephrine, a vasoconstrictor, reduces the rate of systemic absorption of lidocaine from the injection site, prolonging the duration of anesthesia from approximately 1 hour to approximately 3 to 4 hours, and reduces bleeding at the surgical site, which is advantageous during laceration repair and other minor surgical procedures — and 0.5 percent bupivacaine, a longer-acting local anesthetic with a duration of 6 to 12 hours, for the management of rib fractures and other painful injuries where prolonged pain relief without systemic sedation is beneficial), antiemetics (ondansetron 4 milligrams orally disintegrating tablet, a 5-HT₃ receptor antagonist that is effective for the prevention and treatment of nausea and vomiting associated with acute gastroenteritis, motion sickness — a significant consideration given the pod's potential for low-frequency swaying motions from deep-ocean swell, even at 45 meters depth — and postoperative nausea), cardiovascular medications (including aspirin 325 milligrams for the acute management of suspected myocardial infarction, nitroglycerin 0.4 milligrams sublingual for suspected angina, epinephrine 1:10,000 for intravenous administration during cardiac arrest per ACLS algorithms, and atropine 0.5 milligrams for symptomatic bradycardia), psychiatric medications (including diazepam 5 milligrams for acute anxiety and panic attacks, which have an anticipated incidence based on the psychological stress of confined, isolated, and extreme environment habitation of approximately 5 to 10 percent of crew members over the 90-day mission, per the International Space Station behavioral health data, and quetiapine 25 milligrams for the management of acute insomnia and agitation), emergency medications (including the components of the Advanced Cardiac Life Support drug algorithm — epinephrine, amiodarone, and sodium bicarbonate — and the medications required for the management of anaphylaxis — epinephrine 0.3 milligrams intramuscular auto-injector, diphenhydramine 50 milligrams, and methylprednisolone 125 milligrams intravenous — as well as the specific antidotes for the pod's potential toxicological hazards, including hydroxocobalamin 5 grams for cyanide poisoning from the combustion of synthetic materials during a fire event, atropine and pralidoxime for organophosphate poisoning from the potential off-gassing of hydraulic fluids into the cabin atmosphere, and calcium gluconate for hydrofluoric acid burns from the electrolyte components of the Grid-Seed lithium iron phosphate battery packs), and a dermatological formulary (topical antibiotics including mupirocin and silver sulfadiazine for wound care, topical corticosteroids including hydrocortisone 1 percent and triamcinolone 0.1 percent for inflammatory skin conditions, and topical antifungals including clotrimazole for dermatophyte infections), with each medication stocked in a quantity sufficient to provide a 90-day supply for four occupants — calculated using the U.S. Navy medical allowance list methodology, which multiplies the expected daily utilization rate per person, derived from the medical literature and the International Space Station medical operations data, by the crew size and mission duration, and adds a 100 percent backup supply for all life-saving and mission-critical medications (antibiotics, epinephrine, morphine, naloxone, and the ACLS drugs, defined as those medications for which the unavailability of even a single dose could result in preventable death or permanent disability) to account for the impossibility of pharmaceutical resupply during a crustal displacement event.

The pharmaceutical storage system comprises two refrigerated compartments — one maintained at 4°C ± 2°C for the cold-chain medications that degrade at ambient temperature, including the ceftriaxone (which must be reconstituted with sterile water or lidocaine immediately before intramuscular injection, as the reconstituted solution is stable for only 24 hours at room temperature), the insulin (regular human insulin 100 units per milliliter, for the management of new-onset diabetes mellitus or diabetic ketoacidosis, which has an estimated probability of approximately 0.1 percent per person-year in the Atlantis crew demographic), and certain biological drugs that are susceptible to thermal degradation at ambient temperatures exceeding 25°C, and the other maintained at 25°C ± 2°C — a temperature that is approximately 10°C below the pod's maximum cabin temperature of 35°C during an environmental control system partial failure scenario and that is therefore compatible with the storage requirements of the majority of pharmaceutical products, which specify storage at "controlled room temperature" defined by the United States Pharmacopeia (USP <659>) as 20-25°C with excursions permitted to 15-30°C — with both compartments monitored by temperature data loggers that record the internal temperature at 15-minute intervals and that trigger an audible and visual alarm at the pod's central monitoring station if the temperature deviates from the specified range for more than 30 minutes, at which point the crew medical officer must transfer the affected medications to the backup storage compartment — a passive-insulated container with phase-change material packs that maintain the appropriate temperature for 24 hours without electrical power — until the primary refrigeration system is restored.

The telemedicine capability — which effectively extends the diagnostic and therapeutic capacity of the crew medical officer far beyond the limits of his or her 80-hour Wilderness First Responder training plus 40-hour hyperbaric medicine and 20-hour surgical skills training — is provided by the Iridium Certus satellite communication system operating at a data rate of 704 kilobits per second, sufficient to support high-definition video (1080p resolution at 30 frames per second, encoded using the H.265/HEVC video compression standard to minimize bandwidth consumption) for real-time video consultation with the Aegis C Medical Operations Center, where a team of physicians — including an emergency physician, a trauma surgeon, a hyperbaric medicine specialist, and a radiologist — is on call 24 hours per day, 365 days per year, with a response time commitment of 5 minutes for emergency consultations and 30 minutes for routine consultations, a service level agreement that the Aegis C program has contracted through a partnership with the University of California, San Francisco Department of Emergency Medicine and the Duke University Center for Hyperbaric Medicine and Environmental Physiology, both of which have faculty with expertise in austere and diving medicine. The telemedicine system supports store-and-forward imaging: the crew medical officer can acquire digital photographs of wounds, rashes, and physical examination findings using the pod's medical camera (a 20-megapixel digital camera with a 100-millimeter macro lens for close-up dermatological imaging and a ring-flash for shadow-free illumination of body cavities and surgical fields), ultrasound images and video loops from the portable ultrasound system, 12-lead ECG traces, and digital radiographs from the portable X-ray system, and transmit them asynchronously via the Iridium Certus link to the Medical Operations Center, where the relevant specialists can review the images, consult with one another if necessary, and return a written consultation report with diagnostic impressions, treatment recommendations, and — critically — step-by-step procedural instructions for any surgical or medical intervention that exceeds the crew medical officer's training, accompanied by annotated diagrams, relevant pages from the Carr-Library decision-tree algorithm, and, when the bandwidth permits, a short instructional video demonstrating the procedure on a simulator, a capability that the U.S. military's Virtual Health program has demonstrated to reduce the rate of medical evacuation from forward operating bases by up to 30 percent, by enabling the management of conditions that would otherwise exceed the capability of the on-site medical personnel.

The Carr-Library — named for the Aegis C program's founding medical director, Dr. Eleanor Carr, who developed the decision-tree methodology that the library employs — is a digital repository of clinical decision-making algorithms covering approximately 85 percent of the medical and surgical scenarios that the Atlantis program's Delphi consensus panel identified as having a non-negligible probability of occurrence during a 90-day mission at a depth of 30 to 45 meters with a crew of four healthy adults aged 25 to 55, a probability threshold of 0.1 percent (1 in 1,000) per mission, determined through a systematic review of the medical literature on the incidence of illness and injury in analogous environments — saturation diving, submarine deployments, Antarctic research stations, spaceflight missions, and offshore oil and gas platforms — and adjusted for the specific demographic, environmental, and operational characteristics of the Atlantis pod. Each algorithm in the Carr-Library follows a standardized format: a decision tree commencing with the patient's chief complaint (e.g., "chest pain," "abdominal pain," "shortness of breath," "altered mental status," "extremity trauma"), branching through progressively specific questions regarding the history of present illness (onset, duration, character, aggravating and alleviating factors, associated symptoms), the physical examination (vital signs, focused examination of the relevant organ system), and the available diagnostic test results (i-STAT laboratory values, ECG interpretation, ultrasound findings, X-ray images), with each branch leading either to a definitive diagnosis and treatment plan or to a recommendation for telemedicine consultation when the algorithm's discrimination is insufficient to distinguish between the remaining diagnostic possibilities, a deliberate design feature that acknowledges the inherent limitation of protocolized medicine — namely, that protocols cannot account for every possible permutation of a patient's presentation, and that complex or atypical cases require the judgment of an experienced physician who can integrate subtle clinical cues that are not captured by the algorithm's discrete branching logic. The Carr-Library is stored on a ruggedized tablet computer that is mounted in the medical bay and that is also accessible from any of the pod's display terminals via the internal FEATHER mesh network, ensuring that the crew medical officer can access the relevant algorithm from the patient's location — whether that is the medical bay, a crew bunk, or the floor of the galley where a crew member has collapsed — without leaving the patient unattended to consult a reference, a human-factors design consideration that the Wilderness Medical Society's Practice Guidelines for Emergency Care in the Wilderness emphasize as essential for the management of time-critical emergencies in remote environments.

## 4. CREW MEDICAL OFFICER TRAINING AND RECERTIFICATION

The crew medical officer — one of the four occupants of each Atlantis pod, selected during the crew recruitment process based on a combination of medical aptitude (as assessed by a written examination covering basic anatomy, physiology, and first aid), psychological suitability (as assessed by the comprehensive psychological screening protocol described in the Atlantis Psychological Design document), and willingness to undertake the training curriculum — completes a structured training program totaling 140 hours of initial instruction plus 40 hours of annual recertification training, a training burden that is substantial but necessary, given that the Atlantis pod's medical bay and pharmaceutical formulary provide a diagnostic and therapeutic capability that far exceeds that of a typical wilderness or remote industrial medical facility and that is equivalent, in many respects, to the capability of a Level III trauma center — a hospital that can provide prompt assessment, resuscitation, surgery, and intensive care for trauma patients, albeit without the subspecialty surgical expertise (neurosurgery, orthopedics, vascular surgery) that a Level I trauma center offers.

The initial training comprises three components. The first component — Wilderness First Responder (WFR) certification, 80 hours — is the most widely recognized standard for medical training in remote and austere environments, developed by the Wilderness Medical Society and taught by accredited providers including the National Outdoor Leadership School (NOLS) Wilderness Medicine Institute and Wilderness Medical Associates International, and covers the assessment and management of the most common medical and traumatic emergencies encountered in environments where evacuation to definitive care is delayed by hours to days, including patient assessment (the systematic primary and secondary survey methodology of the American College of Surgeons' Advanced Trauma Life Support course, adapted for the wilderness context where spine-injured patients must be log-rolled onto an insulating pad rather than onto a rigid backboard, which would cause pressure injuries and hypothermia during prolonged immobilization), wound management (irrigation with potable water — or, in the Atlantis pod, with sterile saline from the medical supply — at a pressure of 5 to 8 pounds per square inch delivered through a 35-milliliter syringe with an 18-gauge angiocatheter, a technique that reduces bacterial contamination by 80 to 90 percent compared to low-pressure irrigation with a bulb syringe, per the evidence-based wound management guidelines of the Wilderness Medical Society), musculoskeletal injury management (reduction of dislocations — particularly anterior shoulder dislocation, which accounts for approximately 95 percent of all shoulder dislocations and which can be reduced in the field using the Stimson technique, in which the patient lies prone with the affected arm hanging dependently and a 5 to 10 kilogram weight attached to the wrist, allowing the fatigued shoulder muscles to relax over 20 to 30 minutes until spontaneous reduction occurs — and splinting of fractures), environmental emergencies (hypothermia, hyperthermia, and drowning, all of which are directly relevant to the Atlantis pod's underwater environment, where a failure of the environmental control system could rapidly lead to hypothermia at the Monterey site's ambient water temperature of 10-15°C or, conversely, to hyperthermia if the pod's cooling system fails while the external seawater temperature is elevated during a summer upwelling reversal event), and medical emergencies (including anaphylaxis, asthma exacerbation, diabetic emergencies, seizures, and the acute management of psychiatric emergencies — particularly acute psychotic episodes and suicidal ideation, which the psychological monitoring protocol described in the Atlantis Psychological Design document is designed to detect before they reach crisis levels but which may nonetheless occur, given the unpredictability of individual psychological responses to the extreme stressors of confined, isolated, and dangerous environments).

The second component — hyperbaric medicine training, 40 hours — is delivered by a hyperbaric medicine physician certified by the Undersea and Hyperbaric Medical Society (UHMS) and covers the pathophysiology, diagnosis, and treatment of decompression sickness and arterial gas embolism; the operation of the twin-lock hyperbaric chamber, including pressurization and depressurization procedures, BIBS mask fitting and oxygen delivery, chamber fire safety, and emergency decompression protocols; the recognition and management of oxygen toxicity, including the CONdensation VEnTilation (CONVENT) index for predicting central nervous system oxygen toxicity risk based on the partial pressure of oxygen, the duration of exposure, and the individual's susceptibility modifiers (exercise, immersion, carbon dioxide retention), with a safety threshold defined as a unit pulmonary toxic dose (UPTD) of 615, which corresponds to a 2 percent decrease in vital capacity as a marker of pulmonary oxygen toxicity, and which the Atlantis hyperbaric protocols ensure is not exceeded during any single hyperbaric treatment; and the conduct of a neurological examination specifically focused on the detection of decompression sickness, which most commonly manifests as paresthesias, weakness, and gait disturbance from spinal cord involvement — particularly in the thoracic and lumbar segments, which are most susceptible to venous gas bubble embolization because of the spinal cord's segmental blood supply via the artery of Adamkiewicz and the relatively sluggish blood flow in the epidural venous plexus, where bubbles tend to accumulate — and as joint pain ("the bends") from bubble formation within the synovial fluid and periarticular tissues, most commonly affecting the shoulders and knees, which are the joints subjected to the greatest range of motion and the greatest mechanical stress during physical activity at depth.

The third component — surgical skills training, 20 hours — is delivered by a general surgeon or trauma surgeon and covers the surgical procedures listed in the Atlantis pod's surgical capability specification (appendectomy, tube thoracostomy, wound exploration and debridement, fasciotomy), using high-fidelity cadaveric and synthetic tissue simulators that replicate the tactile feedback and anatomical relationships of live human tissue, a training modality that the American College of Surgeons' Advanced Surgical Skills for Exposure in Trauma (ASSET) course has demonstrated to improve surgical performance in subsequent live-tissue procedures by a factor of approximately 2 to 3 relative to didactic instruction alone, as measured by the Objective Structured Assessment of Technical Skills (OSATS) scoring system, and that enables the crew medical officer to practice each procedure a minimum of five times — the number of repetitions that the surgical education literature identifies as the threshold for achieving initial procedural competence, defined as the ability to complete the procedure independently without critical errors — under the direct supervision of the instructor, who provides real-time feedback on instrument handling, tissue manipulation, hemostasis, and wound closure, the four domains of the OSATS global rating scale that correlate most strongly with surgical outcomes in clinical practice.

Annual recertification — 40 hours, conducted during the one-month crew rotation interval between the 90-day missions — reviews and updates the crew medical officer's skills through a combination of didactic instruction on any updates to the Carr-Library algorithms, the pharmaceutical formulary, or the telemedicine protocols that have been issued since the previous mission; hands-on skills practice on the surgical simulators and the hyperbaric chamber; and a capstone simulation exercise — a 4-hour, high-fidelity medical scenario conducted at the Aegis C Medical Simulation Center, using a full-scale mockup of the Atlantis medical bay and a computerized patient mannequin (the CAE Healthcare Apollo mannequin, which simulates vital signs including heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, and end-tidal carbon dioxide; responds to interventions including oxygen administration, intravenous fluid infusion, and medication administration with appropriate physiological changes; and can be programmed to simulate a wide range of medical and traumatic conditions, from anaphylaxis and myocardial infarction to tension pneumothorax and cardiac tamponade) — that tests the crew medical officer's ability to integrate all aspects of the medical capability, from initial assessment and resuscitation through telemedicine consultation and definitive treatment, under the time pressure and psychological stress of a simulated life-threatening emergency, with the simulation being video-recorded for subsequent debriefing by the Aegis C medical faculty, who provide a written performance evaluation that identifies areas for improvement and determines whether the crew medical officer is recertified for the upcoming mission or requires additional training, a quality assurance mechanism that the Aegis C program's Medical Director has implemented to ensure that the crew medical officers maintain the skills and judgment necessary to function as the sole healthcare provider for their pod-mates in an environment where the nearest hospital is, quite literally, 45 vertical meters of seawater and an unknowable distance of surface travel away.
