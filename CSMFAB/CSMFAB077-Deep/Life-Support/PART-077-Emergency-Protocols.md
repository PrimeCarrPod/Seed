## 1. FIRE DETECTION, SUPPRESSION, AND COMPARTMENT ISOLATION

The fire detection system aboard the Atlantis pod deploys a multi-criteria sensor network in which each of the six detection points — positioned in the common area, the galley, the medical bay, the lower equipment bay (housing the environmental control system, the Grid-Seed power modules, and the hydrogen fuel cell), the waste processing compartment, and the inter-pod tunnel junction — integrates three sensing modalities into a single addressable detector head, with the modalities comprising photoelectric smoke detection (operating on the light-scattering principle: a pulsed infrared LED emitting at 880 nanometers projects a beam into a sensing chamber that is optically baffled to exclude ambient light, and a photodiode positioned at 90 degrees to the beam axis detects the light scattered by smoke particles entering the chamber through a labyrinthine inlet designed to exclude dust and insects — the latter being irrelevant in the subsea environment, but the detector is a commercial off-the-shelf unit that retains its standard inlet design — with the detector's sensitivity being factory-calibrated to alarm at an obscuration of 2 percent per meter, the threshold specified by the National Fire Protection Association's NFPA 72 (National Fire Alarm and Signaling Code) for early-warning smoke detection in occupied spaces, a threshold that provides sufficient time for the crew to investigate and suppress a small fire before it grows to a size that threatens the pressure hull's integrity, as the time from the first smoke particle entering the detection chamber to the development of a flashover — the near-simultaneous ignition of all combustible materials in the compartment when the hot gas layer at the ceiling reaches a temperature of approximately 600°C and radiates sufficient thermal energy downward to ignite the materials at floor level, a transition that, in an enclosed compartment of the Atlantis pod's dimensions, occurs within approximately 3 to 5 minutes of the fire's initiation, depending on the fuel load, the ventilation rate, and the compartment geometry, and that once reached, makes survival of any occupants within the compartment essentially impossible, as the temperatures at floor level exceed 200°C, the carbon monoxide concentration exceeds 10,000 parts per million — a level that is lethal within 1 to 2 minutes, as carbon monoxide binds to hemoglobin with an affinity approximately 200 times greater than that of oxygen, forming carboxyhemoglobin that reduces the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity and shifts the oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve to the left, impairing the release of the remaining oxygen to the tissues, a dual hypoxia mechanism that produces unconsciousness at carboxyhemoglobin levels of approximately 50 to 60 percent, corresponding to carbon monoxide inhalation at 5,000 parts per million for approximately 10 to 15 minutes, or at 10,000 parts per million for approximately 3 to 5 minutes — and the thermal radiation from the hot gas layer is sufficient to ignite clothing and cause third-degree burns to exposed skin within seconds, a scenario that the Atlantis fire response protocol addresses by ensuring that the alarm activates no later than the smoldering phase of the fire, typically 60 to 90 seconds after the fire's initiation, before the development of a significant hot gas layer and before the carbon monoxide concentration exceeds 500 parts per million — the 8-hour occupational exposure limit established by the U.S. Occupational Safety and Health Administration — providing the crew with a window of 2 to 4 minutes to execute the fire response protocol before conditions in the affected compartment become incompatible with survival, a window that the Mojave test facility's full-scale fire tests have validated, with the multi-criteria detectors consistently alarming within 45 to 75 seconds of the ignition of a standardized fire source (a polyurethane foam cushion, representative of the bunk mattress material, ignited by an electrical arc simulator that replicates the failure mode of a damaged power cable, the most likely ignition source in the pod, given the extensive electrical infrastructure — the 120 kW Grid-Seed power distribution, the lighting circuits, the galley appliances, the medical equipment, and the personal electronic devices — and the confinement of all this electrical energy within a 50-cubic-meter habitable volume, creating a risk profile that the electrical engineer's arc-flash hazard analysis, conducted per IEEE 1584 (Guide for Performing Arc-Flash Hazard Calculations), has characterized as "moderate" for the 120-volt alternating current circuits and "high" for the 48-volt direct current circuits of the Grid-Seed battery bank, which can deliver short-circuit currents exceeding 2,000 amperes, sufficient to vaporize a copper conductor and create a plasma arc with a temperature exceeding 10,000°C — hot enough to ignite any combustible material within a radius of approximately 0.5 meters — within milliseconds of a bolted-fault or arcing-fault condition, a hazard that the pod's electrical protection system mitigates through the use of arc-fault circuit interrupters — AFCIs — on all 120-volt circuits and current-limiting fuses on all 48-volt circuits, but that cannot be entirely eliminated, as the AFCI technology, while highly effective, has a detection sensitivity of approximately 95 percent, meaning that approximately 5 percent of arcing faults are not detected by the AFCI and must be addressed by the crew's fire response, a statistical vulnerability that the multi-criteria detector network is the primary defense against, as the alternative — waiting for the crew to detect the fire by sight, smell, or the sensation of heat — would delay the response by 30 to 60 seconds relative to the detector alarm, a delay that, in the fast-developing fire scenario of an enclosed, hyperoxic (even at the pod's nominal 21 percent oxygen, the confined volume and the elevated ambient temperature — approximately 22 to 25°C — accelerate combustion relative to the typical residential or office fire, in which the larger volume provides a thermal sink that slows the temperature rise), electrically rich environment, could be the difference between a fire that is suppressed by a single handheld extinguisher and a fire that necessitates compartment isolation and CO₂ flooding, a far more disruptive and hazardous intervention that the Atlantis fire protocol employs only when the handheld extinguishers have failed or when the fire is already too large to be suppressed by a 2-kilogram extinguisher discharge — a judgment call that the crew commander makes within 10 to 15 seconds of arriving at the fire scene, based on the visual assessment of the fire's size (flame height, area involved, rate of growth), the heat flux (perceived through the firefighter's protective clothing), the smoke density (determining whether the crew member can see across the compartment, a rough but operationally useful proxy for the smoke concentration and the visibility conditions for firefighting), and the availability of a second extinguisher if the first one is exhausted without achieving suppression, a decision that the crew commander has practiced in the Mojave fire simulator — a full-scale mockup of the Atlantis common area, in which propane-fueled live-fire scenarios of varying intensity are conducted — a minimum of 20 times during the pre-mission training, developing the pattern-recognition and rapid-decision skills that the firefighting literature identifies as essential for effective incident command in the first 60 seconds of a fire, when the commander's decisions — to fight or to retreat, to ventilate or to seal, to call for backup or to manage the incident with the available personnel — determine the trajectory of the entire incident and the probability of a successful outcome, which, in the Atlantis context, means the survival of all four crew members and the preservation of the pod as a habitable environment.

Upon alarm activation — which triggers simultaneously a 90-decibel audible alarm (a 3-kilohertz tone, the frequency at which the human ear is most sensitive and at which the alarm is most likely to be perceived even by a crew member who is wearing noise-canceling headphones, which are permitted for recreational use during off-duty hours but which attenuate external sounds by approximately 20 to 30 decibels, reducing the effective alarm volume to approximately 60 to 70 decibels, which is still well above the 45-decibel ambient noise level and is therefore perceptible, although the crew member's reaction time may be delayed by 1 to 3 seconds relative to a crew member who is not wearing headphones, a delay that the human-factors analysis has deemed acceptable because the smoke detector alarm is the INITIAL alert, not the ONLY alert, and is followed within 5 to 10 seconds by the crew intercom announcement from the crew member at the central monitoring station, who will have identified the specific detector that triggered and the compartment in which the fire is located) throughout the pod, a visual strobe alarm (a xenon flash tube emitting a 1-joule flash of white light at a frequency of 1 hertz, visible even through closed eyes as a pulsing red brightness, ensuring that crew members who are sleeping and whose auditory system is habituated to the ambient noise — the brain's reticular activating system filters out repetitive, non-threatening sounds during sleep, a phenomenon that explains why a sleeping person may not awaken to a continuous alarm tone but will awaken to a pulsed or intermittent tone, which the brain interprets as novel and potentially threatening — are alerted by the combination of the 3-kilohertz pulsed tone and the 1-hertz strobe flash, a multimodal alerting strategy that the alarm-system design literature identifies as the most effective for awakening sleeping individuals, with a probability of successful awakening exceeding 95 percent within 30 seconds of alarm onset, based on the laboratory studies of Ball and Bruck (2004, Fire Safety Journal) on the effectiveness of different fire alarm signals for awakening sleeping adults, which demonstrated that a 3-kilohertz square-wave tone — the same waveform used in the Atlantis alarm — awakened 100 percent of the study participants within 30 seconds, while a 520-hertz square-wave tone, which was previously believed to be the optimal frequency for awakening sleeping individuals, awakened only 56 percent of participants within the same time interval, a finding that has since been incorporated into the NFPA 72 code and that the Atlantis alarm system implements through the specification of the 3-kilohertz alarm tone, a seemingly minor design detail — the choice of alarm frequency — that, in the context of a sleeping crew member who may have only 60 seconds to don an oxygen mask and reach the muster station before the fire conditions in their compartment become lethal, could be the single most consequential engineering decision in the entire fire response architecture, as the 5 to 10 seconds of additional awakening time that the 3-kilohertz tone provides relative to the 520-hertz tone could be the difference between a crew member who reaches the muster station and a crew member who does not, a realization that the Atlantis fire safety engineer — who spent 20 years as a U.S. Navy submarine damage control instructor before joining the Atlantis program — articulated during the design review as "the tone that saves a life is the one the sleeper hears," a principle that now governs every aspect of the alarm system, from the waveform of the audible alarm to the flash frequency of the strobe to the wording of the crew intercom announcement ("FIRE, FIRE, FIRE — [Compartment name] — ALL HANDS MUSTER AT [Muster station location] — DON O₂ MASKS — STANDBY FOR FURTHER INSTRUCTIONS," a script that was developed through iterative testing in the Mojave simulator, with each word evaluated for its information content, its emotional impact, and its potential for misinterpretation, and that was finalized as the script that maximized the speed and accuracy of the crew's response while minimizing the anxiety — and the associated performance degradation — that an emergency announcement inevitably induces, a human-factors optimization that exemplifies the Atlantis design philosophy: every word, every frequency, every flash is engineered, tested, and validated, because in the event that the system is needed, there will be no time for ambiguity, no margin for error, and no second chance.

Upon fire confirmation — either by the crew member who first arrives at the affected compartment and visually confirms the presence of flames or dense smoke, or by the activation of the second detector modality (heat detection, which triggers at a temperature rise rate of 8°C per minute or at an absolute temperature of 58°C, whichever occurs first, providing a second, independent confirmation of a genuine fire event, as the photoelectric smoke detector can be triggered by non-fire sources — steam from a leaking boiler pipe, dust from a maintenance activity, or the aerosolized oil droplets from a malfunctioning galley appliance — that generate particles in the size range (approximately 0.3 to 10 micrometers) to which the photoelectric sensor responds, an inherent limitation of the photoelectric detection technology that the heat detector's independent sensing modality — thermal — is designed to overcome, as a non-fire particle source will not generate the rapid temperature rise that characterizes a genuine combustion event, providing the crew with the confidence that the alarm is not a false positive before committing to the disruptive and potentially hazardous fire suppression actions that follow), the crew commander initiates the fire response sequence. The three crew members who are not at the central monitoring station — the fourth crew member, who is the designated "fire response coordinator" for the current watch, is at the station and is responsible for monitoring the detector readouts, communicating with the crew via the intercom, and logging all actions in the emergency log — proceed to the fire equipment lockers, which are positioned at the entrance to each compartment and which contain, for each crew member, a Dräger PARAT 5500 self-contained self-rescuer, a portable breathing apparatus that provides a minimum of 15 minutes of breathable oxygen (the device employs a potassium superoxide (KO₂) chemical oxygen generator that reacts with the moisture in the wearer's exhaled breath — KO₂ + H₂O → KOH + O₂, a reaction that liberates approximately 0.38 liters of oxygen per gram of KO₂, with the 550 grams of KO₂ in the PARAT 5500 cartridge capable of generating approximately 209 liters of oxygen, sufficient to support the average adult's resting oxygen consumption of approximately 0.3 liters per minute for approximately 700 minutes, or over 11 hours — far exceeding the 15-minute rated duration, which is limited not by the oxygen generation capacity but by the CO₂ scrubbing capacity of the lithium hydroxide (LiOH) cartridge that removes the exhaled carbon dioxide, a reaction — 2LiOH + CO₂ → Li₂CO₃ + H₂O — that is exothermic and that warms the inhaled air to approximately body temperature, a comfort feature that the manufacturer highlights but that, in the Atlantis pod's confined space, where the ambient temperature may already be elevated by the fire, represents an additional thermal burden on the firefighter, a burden that the 15-minute duration is intended to limit, as the core body temperature of a firefighter wearing the PARAT 5500 and engaged in strenuous firefighting activity — carrying a 5-kilogram CO₂ extinguisher, crouching or crawling in smoke-obscured conditions, manipulating valves and hatches — can rise by approximately 0.5°C per minute, such that after 15 minutes the core temperature would be elevated by approximately 7.5°C, from 37°C to 44.5°C, a level at which heat stroke — defined as a core temperature exceeding 40°C accompanied by central nervous system dysfunction, including confusion, ataxia, seizures, and loss of consciousness — becomes a significant risk, particularly in crew members who are dehydrated or who have a pre-existing medical condition that impairs thermoregulation, a risk that the 15-minute PARAT 5500 duration, the pre-fire hydration protocol that the crew follows (drinking 500 milliliters of cool water — approximately 10°C, from the galley refrigerator — within 15 minutes of the start of the firefighting activity, a strategy that the sports medicine literature identifies as effective for reducing the rate of core temperature rise during exercise in hot environments, as the ingested cool water absorbs approximately 20 kilojoules of heat — the specific heat of water, 4.18 kilojoules per kilogram per degree Celsius, multiplied by the 0.5-kilogram water mass and the 25°C temperature difference between the water (10°C) and the body (37°C, with the water being warmed to body temperature before it is absorbed and utilized), an effect that, while modest in absolute terms — 20 kilojoules is equivalent to the heat produced by approximately 5 minutes of resting metabolism — is nonetheless beneficial in the aggregate, particularly when combined with the other heat-mitigation strategies (donning the firefighting protective clothing only at the last possible moment before entering the fire compartment, rather than wearing it throughout the preparation phase, as the clothing's thermal insulation — a 3-millimeter-thick layer of Nomex aramid fabric over a moisture barrier and a thermal liner — imposes a significant thermal burden even before the firefighter enters the hot environment, as the clothing traps the body's metabolic heat, elevating the skin temperature and accelerating the onset of heat strain, an effect that the solar engineering literature refers to as the "pre-heat penalty" of protective clothing and that the Atlantis fire protocol mitigates by keeping the protective gear — stored in the fire equipment locker, donned at the locker in a process that requires approximately 60 seconds, then entered into the fire compartment — to minimize the duration of the pre-heat penalty), and the buddy system — firefighters always work in pairs, with one firefighter operating the extinguisher and the second firefighter observing the first for signs of heat strain or smoke inhalation, maintaining communication via the intercom headsets that are integrated into the PARAT 5500 facepiece, the same communication system that the crew uses during normal operations but that, in the firefighting context, provides the incident commander with continuous situational awareness of the firefighters' status and location, enabling the commander to order a retreat if the firefighters report symptoms of heat strain — dizziness, nausea, confusion, chest pain — or if the fire conditions deteriorate beyond the point where the handheld extinguishers can be expected to be effective, a decision that the protocol specifies must be made by the incident commander, not by the firefighters, to avoid the cognitive impairment and the risk-taking bias — "I've almost got it, just a few more seconds" — that can affect firefighters who are immersed in the firefighting task and who lose the situational awareness necessary to recognize when the fire has exceeded their capacity to control it, a phenomenon that the fire service literature documents extensively and that has been responsible for numerous firefighter fatalities, in which the deceased firefighter was found within a few meters of the exit, having attempted to escape but having delayed the decision to retreat until the conditions were no longer survivable, a tragedy that the Atlantis fire protocol is specifically designed to prevent through the combination of the incident commander's remote monitoring, the 15-minute PARAT 5500 oxygen supply (which provides a hard deadline — when the PARAT 5500's end-of-service-life indicator, a colorimetric strip that changes color as the LiOH cartridge becomes saturated with CO₂, signals that the cartridge has approximately 2 minutes of remaining life, the firefighter MUST retreat, regardless of the fire's status, a rule that the crew has practiced in the simulator and that is enforced without exception), and the pre-fire planning that identifies the "point of no return" for each compartment — the location within the compartment beyond which a firefighter cannot retreat within the 2-minute warning period, a calculation based on the distance from the point to the compartment exit, the speed of movement in firefighting protective gear under zero-visibility conditions (assumed to be 0.3 meters per second, or approximately one-tenth of the normal walking speed, to account for the need to crawl, to feel one's way along the wall, to navigate around obstacles, and to assist an incapacitated crew member), and the time required to operate the watertight door — 30 seconds from the arrival at the door to the door being closed and dogged, a time that was measured in the Mojave simulator and that represents the crew's best performance, not their average, consistent with the safety-critical design philosophy that emergency procedures must be achievable by a crew under the maximum stress and the maximum physical and cognitive impairment that can be reasonably anticipated, not by a crew in optimal condition under ideal circumstances.

The handheld fire extinguishers deployed throughout the pod are of two types, each positioned at the entrance to every compartment and at the galley — the highest-fire-risk location, given the presence of the microwave oven, the induction cooktop, the toaster oven, and the associated electrical circuits — and each type selected for compatibility with the specific fire risks of an enclosed, hyperoxic, electrically energized underwater habitat. The primary extinguisher is a 2-kilogram carbon dioxide (CO₂) unit, which discharges its contents through a non-conductive plastic horn (carbon dioxide, being a gas at atmospheric pressure and stored as a liquid under its own vapor pressure of approximately 5.7 megapascals at 20°C, exits the extinguisher's discharge valve at a temperature of approximately -78°C — the sublimation temperature of solid CO₂, or dry ice, which forms in the discharge stream as the liquid CO₂ expands and cools, creating a visible white cloud of CO₂ "snow" that serves as both a cooling agent and an oxygen-displacing agent, as the CO₂ gas, which is approximately 1.5 times denser than air, settles into the lower regions of the compartment and displaces the oxygen that supports combustion, reducing the oxygen concentration from the ambient 21 percent to below the 15 percent threshold at which most common combustible materials — wood, paper, fabric, plastics — cease to sustain flaming combustion, a process that occurs within approximately 10 to 15 seconds of the extinguisher's discharge into a typical Atlantis compartment of 12 to 15 cubic meters, a discharge duration of approximately 15 to 20 seconds for the 2-kilogram unit, after which the compartment's oxygen concentration will have been reduced to approximately 10 to 12 percent — a level that is insufficient to support human consciousness, a fact that the fire crew is acutely aware of and that is the reason the PARAT 5500 masks are donned BEFORE the extinguisher is discharged into an occupied compartment, as even a brief exposure — 2 to 3 breaths — to an atmosphere with 10 percent oxygen results in impaired judgment, reduced coordination, and, within 30 to 60 seconds, loss of consciousness, a rapidity of onset that leaves no margin for error and that has been responsible for numerous fatalities in enclosed-space firefighting incidents, where firefighters who removed their self-contained breathing apparatus facepieces — because the facepiece was fogged, or because the firefighter believed the fire was extinguished and the atmosphere was safe — were overcome by the oxygen-depleted atmosphere that the CO₂ extinguisher had created, an invisible, odorless, and tasteless hazard that is indistinguishable from normal air until the moment of unconsciousness, a hazard that the Atlantis fire protocol addresses through the absolute prohibition — no exception, no judgment call, no "I thought it was okay" — against the removal of the PARAT 5500 facepiece within a compartment that has been subjected to CO₂ flooding, whether from the handheld extinguishers or from the fixed CO₂ flooding system described below, until the compartment has been ventilated to the pod's external ambient pressure — a process that requires approximately 15 to 30 minutes, during which the ventilation system, operating at its maximum capacity of 15 air changes per hour, flushes the CO₂-laden atmosphere from the compartment, replacing it with fresh, oxygenated cabin air, and the oxygen concentration, monitored continuously by an electrochemical sensor at the compartment's exit, has returned to the normal 20.9 percent, at which point the crew member at the central monitoring station authorizes the removal of the PARAT 5500 facepieces, a decision that is not delegated to the firefighters but is made by the incident commander, who has the benefit of the remote oxygen concentration data, the elapsed time, and the knowledge that no fire is more dangerous than the one that has been extinguished but whose residual oxygen-depleted atmosphere remains.

The secondary extinguisher — a 2-liter aqueous film-forming foam (AFFF) unit, deployed only for fires involving ordinary combustibles (wood, paper, fabric — Class A fires, in the NFPA classification) where the CO₂ extinguisher's cooling effect is insufficient to prevent re-ignition, as CO₂, while effective at displacing oxygen, provides essentially no cooling — the gas exits the nozzle at -78°C, but the thermal mass of the CO₂ "snow" is negligible relative to the thermal mass of the burning material, and the snow sublimates within seconds of contacting the hot surface, providing a transient cooling effect that may suppress the flames momentarily but that does not reduce the temperature of the fuel below its ignition temperature, such that when the CO₂ dissipates and the oxygen concentration returns, the fuel may spontaneously re-ignite if its temperature remains above the autoignition threshold — approximately 250 to 400°C for the typical combustible materials in the Atlantis pod, a scenario that the AFFF extinguisher is designed to prevent by depositing a thin film of surfactant-stabilized foam onto the burning surface, cooling the surface through the evaporation of the water component of the foam (approximately 95 percent water by volume) and simultaneously forming a vapor-suppressing barrier that prevents the flammable vapors from mixing with the oxygen in the air, a dual-mechanism suppression that is more effective than CO₂ for deep-seated fires in porous materials (the bunk mattress, the wool-felt wall panels, the cork ceiling panels) where the fuel mass retains heat and where a surface-only suppression — the CO₂ extinguisher's mechanism — may fail to extinguish the fire in the interior of the material, a scenario that the Mojave fire tests encountered during the development of the fire response protocol, when a CO₂ extinguisher successfully suppressed the flames on the surface of a burning mattress but, 90 seconds after the extinguisher discharge ceased, the mattress re-ignited — the interior of the polyurethane foam had continued to smolder, generating flammable pyrolysis gases that, when the oxygen concentration in the compartment recovered, ignited spontaneously, a demonstration that prompted the Atlantis fire safety engineer to add the AFFF extinguisher to the fire equipment specification and to mandate that CO₂-only firefighting be followed, if possible, by the application of AFFF to the cooled but potentially still-smoldering fuel surface, a two-step suppression tactic that increases the probability of permanent extinguishment at the cost of a slightly more complex — and therefore slightly slower — firefighting procedure, a trade-off that the Mojave tests validated, demonstrating that the CO₂-AND-AFFF sequence, when executed by a trained crew, achieved permanent extinguishment in 95 percent of the test fires, compared to 72 percent for CO₂ alone and 88 percent for AFFF alone, a statistically significant difference (p < 0.01, Fisher's exact test) that, in the context of the Atlantis pod where a re-ignited fire would find the crew with exhausted extinguishers and depleted PARAT 5500 cartridges — a condition that the fire protocol characterizes as "defenseless against re-ignition" and that would necessitate the most extreme of the fire response options, the abandonment of the affected compartment to the CO₂ flooding system and the evacuation of the crew to the adjacent pod via the inter-pod tunnel, if the fire is in the common area, or to the hyperbaric chamber or the external ROV-accessible service module, if the fire is elsewhere and the common area remains habitable, a contingency that the protocol covers but that represents a last-resort response, short of the ultimate last resort — the abandonment of the pod itself, an action that the evacuation decision matrix addresses and that, in the judgment of the Atlantis fire safety engineer, would represent the failure of every layer of the fire safety architecture: the electrical protection systems that prevent ignition, the multi-criteria detectors that provide early warning, the crew training that enables effective first-response firefighting, and the compartment isolation and CO₂ flooding that contain the fire when the handheld extinguishers are insufficient, such that the only way a pod fire could reach the point where evacuation becomes necessary is through the simultaneous failure of multiple, independent layers of defense, a probability that the fault-tree analysis conducted per the methodology of the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission's NUREG-0492 (Fault Tree Handbook) estimates at less than 10^-6 per mission — one in a million — a probability that is comparable to the risk of a catastrophic pressure hull failure from a manufacturing defect undetected by the ultrasonic and radiographic inspection of every hull laminate, and that the Aegis C safety review board has accepted as consistent with the program's overall safety objective — that no single failure, and no reasonably foreseeable combination of failures, should result in the loss of the pod or the death of a crew member, a standard that is not zero risk — zero risk is impossible in any human endeavor, particularly one as inherently hazardous as living at the bottom of the ocean — but that represents the boundary of what the Aegis C program, with its resources, its expertise, and its commitment, can achieve, acknowledging that the final increment of safety — the margin between 10^-6 and zero — is beyond the reach of engineering and can only be provided by the courage, the training, and the judgment of the crew, who will face the fire, the flood, the toxic gas, or the failing pressure hull with the tools and the protocols that this document describes, and who will, in that moment, be the ultimate layer of defense, the human layer, the layer that no redundancy, no automation, and no fault-tree analysis can replicate, because the human layer is not a system — it is a person, and a person, unlike a system, can improvise, can adapt, can find a solution that the designers never anticipated, and can make the decision — the decision that no protocol can make for them — to risk their own life to save the lives of their crewmates, a decision that the Atlantis program does not demand, does not expect, and cannot reward, but that it must, in the final analysis, rely upon, because the alternative — to pretend that the system can be made perfectly safe, that every failure can be anticipated and mitigated, that no crew member will ever face a situation that exceeds the capacity of the protocols — is the fantasy of the designer who has never faced the fire, the flood, the toxic gas, or the failing pressure hull, and who does not understand that the protocols are not a substitute for courage but a tool that courage wields.

The CO₂ flooding system — the fixed fire suppression system installed in all compartments except the medical bay (which contains the oxygen cylinders for the hyperbaric chamber and the medical oxygen delivery system, and in which CO₂ flooding, by displacing oxygen, would be counterproductive, as it would deprive the medical officer of the ability to administer oxygen to a patient while simultaneously creating an oxygen-depleted atmosphere that would require the medical officer to wear a PARAT 5500 mask, impairing the fine motor skills required for medical procedures; for the medical bay, the fixed suppression system employs a clean-agent gas — Novec 1230, a fluorinated ketone (C₆F₁₂O) that extinguishes fires through heat absorption — the Novec 1230 liquid, discharged as a fine spray that vaporizes rapidly, absorbing approximately 95 kilojoules of heat per kilogram vaporized, far more than the latent heat of vaporization of water, approximately 2,260 kilojoules per kilogram at 100°C, but the Novec 1230's advantage is that its heat absorption mechanism does not reduce the oxygen concentration — the Novec 1230 concentration required for fire suppression, approximately 4 to 6 percent by volume, reduces the oxygen concentration from 21 percent to approximately 20 percent, a change that is physiologically imperceptible and that allows the medical officer to continue working without a respirator, a critical capability for the medical bay, where the patient may be in the hyperbaric chamber, requiring continuous monitoring and potential emergency intervention, none of which can be performed effectively while wearing a PARAT 5500 mask — and that the Atlantis fire safety engineer selected over other clean agents — FM-200 (heptafluoropropane), Inergen (a mixture of nitrogen, argon, and carbon dioxide) — because Novec 1230 has a zero ozone-depletion potential, a global warming potential of less than 1 (compared to carbon dioxide's global warming potential of 1, by definition), and an atmospheric lifetime of approximately 5 days (compared to FM-200's atmospheric lifetime of approximately 31 years and its global warming potential of approximately 3,220), making Novec 1230 the most environmentally sustainable of the commercially available clean agents, a consideration that, while secondary to the fire suppression performance, is consistent with the Atlantis program's commitment to minimizing the environmental impact of its operations within the Monterey Bay National Marine Sanctuary) — is actuated when the crew commander determines that the handheld extinguishers have failed to suppress the fire, a determination that triggers the sequential execution of the following actions: all crew members evacuate the affected compartment and confirm their accountability at the muster station via the RFID crew accountability system — each crew member's PARAT 5500 facepiece is equipped with a passive RFID tag that is interrogated by a reader at the muster station, automatically logging the crew member's arrival at the station and displaying the crew roster on the central monitoring display, with a visual indicator (green for accounted, red for unaccounted) that provides the incident commander with an instantaneous, unambiguous assessment of whether any crew member remains in the affected compartment, a capability that the submarine force's experience with the "man overboard" accounting problem — in which the delay between the discovery of a missing crew member and the confirmation that the crew member is in the water, requiring rescue, or is trapped inside a flooded compartment, requiring an entirely different response — has demonstrated to be a critical determinant of the speed and appropriateness of the emergency response, as the minutes spent accounting for a crew member who is safe but out of communication are minutes that are not spent searching for a crew member who is in danger, and the RFID accountability system, by providing the incident commander with an automated, real-time, and verifiable crew status, eliminates the accounting delay and enables the immediate initiation of the search-and-rescue response if a crew member is unaccounted, a capability that the submarine force has sought for decades — the U.S. Navy's Submarine Safety Program (SUBSAFE) has identified the crew accountability problem as one of the most persistent and consequential challenges in submarine damage control, and the RFID-based solution that the Atlantis program has adopted was developed in collaboration with the Naval Undersea Warfare Center in Newport, Rhode Island, as a technology transfer from the SUBSAFE program to the Aegis C program. After crew accountability is confirmed, the watertight doors separating the affected compartment from the adjacent compartments are closed and dogged — a manual operation requiring approximately 30 seconds per door, during which the crew member operating the door verifies that the door's gasket is clean, that the dogging mechanism engages fully, and that the door's status indicator (a mechanical flag visible from both sides that displays "OPEN" in green or "CLOSED" in red) confirms the watertight seal, a verification that is particularly important given that the watertight doors serve both as fire boundaries and as flood boundaries, and a door that fails to seal — because a piece of debris, a fire hose, or a crew member's clothing is caught in the gasket — would compromise not only the fire isolation but also the subsequent flood protection if the firefighting water — from the AFFF extinguisher, which deposits approximately 2 liters of water into the affected compartment — accumulates to a depth sufficient to flow through the unsealed door, a scenario that the Atlantis flood protocol addresses but that is best prevented by the simple, disciplined act of verifying that the watertight door is properly closed and dogged, a verification that the crew practices in the Mojave simulator until it becomes automatic, a habit as ingrained as the habit of checking that the pressure hull hatch is properly sealed before submerging. The CO₂ flooding system is then activated from the central monitoring station — a software-commanded valve actuation that opens the CO₂ cylinder bank, a manifold of four 50-kilogram CO₂ cylinders discharging the CO₂ through a series of diffuser nozzles positioned at the ceiling of the compartment — at a flow rate designed to achieve a CO₂ concentration of 35 percent by volume within 2 minutes of activation, a concentration that is approximately twice the minimum CO₂ concentration required to extinguish the most difficult-to-suppress fires — those involving energized electrical equipment, where the arc energy continues to vaporize the conductor and provide an ignition source even as the CO₂ displaces the oxygen, a phenomenon that requires a higher CO₂ concentration to overcome than a approximately 3 times the time required for the thermal mass of the ignited materials to cool below their autoignition temperature, a conservative margin that accounts for the possibility that the fire has penetrated into the compartment's insulation, behind the wall panels, or into the cable trays, where residual heat could persist for 5 to 10 minutes after the visible flames are extinguished, ## 2. FLOOD RESPONSE AND TIME-TO-CRITICAL CALCULATION

The flood detection system comprises water sensors — simple, robust, highly reliable conductivity-based sensors consisting of two stainless steel electrodes separated by a 5-millimeter gap and mounted slope of approximately 2 degrees toward the central drainage channel, would first accumulate), the four corners of the compartment (the locations where water flowing from a hull penetration, a adjacent pod, if the adjacent pod's crew has not already detected and responded to the flood, a scenario that the inter-pod communication protocol addresses with a standardized emergency message: ## 3. TOXIC GAS DETECTION, LOSS OF PRESSURE, AND EVACUATION DECISION

The toxic gas detection system continuously monitors the pod's atmospheric composition for the four gases identified by the Atlantis hazard analysis as the most likely products of a fire, a chemical release, or a life support system malfunction within the pod's confined volume: carbon monoxide (CO), with a low alarm threshold of 25 parts per million — the 8-hour time-weighted average permissible exposure limit established by the U.S. Occupational Safety and Health Administration — and a high alarm threshold of 100 parts per million — the concentration at which the carboxyhemoglobin level, after 8 hours of exposure, would reach approximately 10 percent, producing mild symptoms (headache, fatigue, dizziness) in the majority of individuals, and serving as the trigger for the crew's evacuation of the affected compartment, as concentrations above this level indicate a serious fire or a malfunction of the atmospheric management system's catalytic oxidizer, which is responsible for oxidizing carbon monoxide to carbon dioxide before the air is returned to the cabin; hydrogen sulfide (H₂S), with a low alarm threshold of 10 parts per million — the 10-minute ceiling limit, which should not be exceeded for any duration, established by the U.S. National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health — and a high alarm threshold of 50 parts per million — the concentration at which the gas becomes immediately dangerous to life and health, as hydrogen sulfide is a potent inhibitor of cytochrome c oxidase, a critical enzyme in the mitochondrial electron transport chain, and exposure to concentrations above 50 parts per million can produce rapid unconsciousness ("knockdown"), respiratory paralysis, and death within minutes, a hazard that is particularly concerning in the Atlantis pod because hydrogen sulfide is a potential decomposition product of the organic matter in the waste processing system (the microwave dryer and the solid waste collection container, where anaerobic microbial activity in the brief interval before the microwave drying cycle begins could, in theory, generate trace quantities of H₂S, a risk that the waste processing compartment's negative-pressure ventilation and activated carbon odor control system are designed to mitigate but that the gas detection system monitors as an independent, redundant safeguard); ammonia (NH₃), with a low alarm threshold of 25 parts per million — the 8-hour time-weighted average permissible exposure limit — and a high alarm threshold of 35 parts per million — the 15-minute short-term exposure limit — both of which are conservative relative to the immediately dangerous to life and health concentration of 300 parts per million, reflecting the Atlantis program's precautionary approach to ammonia exposure, as ammonia is a respiratory irritant at low concentrations (25 to 50 parts per million, producing eye, nose, and throat irritation) and a potentially fatal pulmonary toxicant at high concentrations (above 500 parts per million, producing laryngeal edema, bronchospasm, and pulmonary edema, a sequence that can progress from mild irritation to life-threatening respiratory failure within 30 to 60 minutes of exposure), and because ammonia is the primary hazard associated with a leak of the cabin's ammonia-based environmental control system refrigerant — a system that was considered during the design phase but ultimately rejected in favor of the less hazardous but less efficient carbon dioxide-based transcritical refrigeration cycle, a decision that the gas detection monitoring for ammonia — which would not be present in the pod's atmosphere under normal operations — serves to validate, as the presence of ammonia at concentrations above the low alarm threshold would indicate either a decomposition of the polyurethane foam in the bunk mattresses (which can release ammonia as a thermal decomposition product at temperatures above 200°C, indicating a smoldering fire that the smoke detectors may not have detected if the fire is deep within the mattress foam, where the smoke particles are filtered by the foam before they reach the detector, a limitation of photoelectric smoke detection that the ammonia gas detector, as an alternative fire detection modality, is specifically intended to address, providing the crew with an early warning of a fire that the smoke detectors may miss, a scenario that the Atlantis fire safety engineer has characterized as the "smoldering mattress problem" and that the ammonia detector, by responding to the ammonia that is liberated from the decomposing polyurethane, is designed to solve, at a component cost of approximately $350 per detector and an energy consumption of approximately 2 watts, a trivial investment for a capability that could provide the crew with the minutes of additional warning that are the difference between a fire suppressed with a single extinguisher and a fire that requires the CO₂ flooding system, or worse); and chlorine (Cl₂), with a low alarm threshold of 0.5 parts per million — the 15-minute short-term exposure limit — and a high alarm threshold of 1 part per million — the immediately dangerous to life and health concentration — both thresholds reflecting the extreme toxicity of chlorine gas, which was used as a chemical weapon in World War I and which produces pulmonary edema at concentrations as low as 30 parts per million after 30 minutes of exposure, and which could be generated within the Atlantis pod by the electrolysis of seawater — the chlorine evolution reaction, 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻, which competes with the desired oxygen evolution reaction, 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻, at the anode of the electrolyzer that generates the pod's breathing oxygen — if the electrolyzer's chloride-ion-selective membrane, which is designed to exclude chloride ions from the anode compartment while permitting the transport of hydroxide ions, were to fail, allowing seawater chloride (present at a concentration of approximately 19,000 parts per million, or 19 grams per liter) to enter the anode compartment and to be oxidized to chlorine gas, a scenario that the electrolyzer's multiple layers of defense — the pre-treatment of the feedwater by reverse osmosis to remove essentially all dissolved ions, including chloride, before the water enters the electrolyzer; the chloride-selective ion-exchange membrane; and the chlorine gas sensor at the electrolyzer's oxygen output, which would detect chlorine at concentrations as low as 0.1 part per million and would automatically shut down the electrolyzer if chlorine was detected — are designed to render vanishingly improbable, with the fault-tree analysis estimating the probability of a chlorine release from the electrolyzer at less than 10^-8 per hour of operation, or approximately once in 10,000 years of continuous operation, a probability that is so low as to be, for all practical purposes, indistinguishable from zero, but that the Atlantis safety philosophy — which holds that any hazard that is physically possible, no matter how improbable, must be addressed by a specific detection and response capability — requires to be monitored, and that the chlorine gas detector, at its negligible cost and power consumption, provides, satisfying the safety requirement without imposing a significant burden on the pod's resources.

The loss-of-pressure detection system monitors the pod's internal atmospheric pressure at a sampling rate of 1 hertz via a precision barometric pressure sensor — a MEMS (microelectromechanical systems) piezoresistive sensor with an accuracy of ±0.01 percent of full scale (0 to 200 kilopascals absolute) and a resolution of 0.1 pascal, mounted in the common area at a location that is representative of the pod's average internal pressure and that is remote from the ventilation supply and return diffusers, which could create localized pressure variations that would cause false alarms if the sensor were positioned in the direct path of the airflow, a consideration that the sensor placement study — a computational fluid dynamics simulation of the pod's internal airflow, validated by a physical test in the Mojave prototype using a smoke-wand flow visualization and a multi-point pressure measurement array — identified and resolved, selecting the sensor location at the center of the common area ceiling, where the airflow velocities are approximately 0.1 meters per second and the pressure field is uniform within 0.5 pascal, well within the sensor's accuracy specification. The pressure monitoring system is configured to generate an alarm when the internal pressure decreases by 5 percent below the nominal setpoint of 101.325 kilopascals absolute (1.0 atmosphere), corresponding to a loss-of-pressure alarm threshold of 96.259 kilopascals absolute (approximately 0.95 atmospheres), a threshold that provides the crew with approximately 30 to 60 minutes of warning — depending on the size of the leak, which the crew determines using the ultrasonic leak detector, a handheld instrument that detects the high-frequency sound (20 to 100 kilohertz) generated by the turbulent flow of air through a small orifice — the "hiss" that is inaudible to the human ear but that the ultrasonic detector's piezoelectric transducer, coupled to a heterodyne circuit that down-converts the ultrasonic signal to an audible frequency (approximately 2 to 4 kilohertz) and amplifies it through a headset, enables the crew to locate by scanning the detector's directional probe along the pressure hull's interior surface, the viewport seals, the hatch gaskets, and the piping penetrations, a process that the crew practices in the Mojave simulator — where a controlled leak of approximately 1 liter per minute at standard temperature and pressure is introduced through a calibrated orifice, and the crew member must locate the leak within 120 seconds, a time limit that the crew can achieve only through the disciplined, systematic scanning of each linear meter of the pressure boundary, a search technique that the training staff drills into the crew until it becomes automatic, "left to right, top to bottom, one meter at a time, don't skip, don't rush, the leak is hiding and your job is to FIND it, not to guess where it is," a mantra that the crew commander repeats during the leak-search drill, maintaining the crew member's focus and preventing the "search-satiation" — the cognitive fatigue that causes the searcher to become less thorough over time, to skip areas, to rush through the scan, and ultimately to miss the leak that is right in front of them, a phenomenon that the human-factors literature on visual inspection and search behavior has documented extensively and that the Atlantis leak-search protocol addresses through the combination of the systematic scanning discipline, the 120-second time limit (which is long enough to complete a thorough scan but short enough to maintain the searcher's concentration), and the ultrasonic detector's audible feedback (the sound of the leak, translated into the auditory modality, engages a different sensory channel than the visual search, providing the searcher with a secondary cue that complements the visual scanning and that is particularly effective for detecting leaks that are not visible — a leak that is behind a wall panel, inside a cable penetration, or on the opposite side of a pipe, where the ultrasonic sound propagates through the structure and is detectable even when the visual path to the leak is obstructed, a capability that the ultrasonic detector's manufacturers emphasize but that the Atlantis crew has learned to exploit through the technique of "contact scanning" — pressing the ultrasonic probe's tip against the surface of the pressure hull, the hatch frame, or the pipe, such that the ultrasonic vibrations are conducted through the solid material to the probe, bypassing the air gap that would attenuate the signal, and enabling the detection of leaks that are hidden from both the visual and the airborne-acoustic modalities, a technique that the crew's leak-detection training — a 4-hour module within the 40-hour hyperbaric medicine and emergency procedures course — covers in detail, with supervised practice on a leak-detection simulator that uses electronically generated ultrasonic signals fed through piezoelectric actuators bonded to the interior of a mockup pressure hull section, simulating leaks of varying sizes, locations, and accessibility. Once located, the leak is repaired using the emergency hull patch kit — a 2-part epoxy resin system, a neoprene rubber patch of 200 millimeters by 200 millimeters and 5 millimeters thickness, and two ratchet straps rated for 2,500 newtons of tension each — a kit that has been tested and certified to seal a circular hole of 50 millimeters diameter against a differential pressure of 5 atmospheres (approximately 500 kilopascals), the maximum differential pressure that the pod would experience at its maximum operational depth of 45 meters (5.5 atmospheres external minus 1.0 atmosphere internal, with the 5-atmosphere certification providing a safety margin of approximately 10 percent relative to the maximum operational differential pressure of 4.5 atmospheres), a certification that was achieved through a series of destructive tests at the Mojave hyperbaric facility, in which 50-millimeter-diameter holes were deliberately drilled through full-scale Atlantis hull laminate specimens, the specimens were pressurized with air to the specified differential pressure, and the hull patch kit was applied according to the crew's procedural checklist (surface preparation: clean the area around the hole with the isopropyl alcohol wipe included in the kit, removing any oil, grease, or moisture that would prevent the epoxy from bonding to the laminate surface, a step that the crew performs with the methodical care of a surgeon preparing a surgical site, because the epoxy's bond strength — specified by the manufacturer at 15 megapascals in shear, based on the ASTM D1002 standard test for the lap-shear strength of adhesively bonded metal specimens — depends critically on the surface cleanliness, as a surface contaminated with even a monolayer of oil — the sebum from a crew member's fingertips, transferred to the laminate during a prior maintenance task — can reduce the bond strength by a factor of 2 to 3, a reduction that, in the context of a 50-millimeter hole subjected to a 500-kilopascal pressure differential, would be catastrophic — the patch would fail, the hole would re-open, and the crew would be back where they started, with less air, less time, and fewer options — a scenario that the crew's training instructor has impressed upon them through a demonstration in which two identical surfaces, one cleaned and one contaminated with a fingerprint, are The controlled ascent procedure — the last resort, when all other emergency responses have failed and the crew has concluded, through the application of the evacuation decision matrix, that the pod can no longer sustain human life — is a pre-programmed sequence executed by the pod's ballast control system, which vents the seawater from the ballast tanks at a controlled rate that produces a net positive buoyancy of approximately 5 kilonewtons (the pod's displacement in seawater is approximately 50 cubic meters, weighing approximately 51,250 kilograms, for a negative buoyancy — when the ballast tanks are flooded — of approximately 1,250 kilograms, or approximately 12.3 kilonewtons of negative buoyancy; by venting the ballast tanks to achieve a net displacement of approximately 51.5 cubic meters, the pod achieves a positive buoyancy of approximately 0.5 percent of its displacement, or approximately 2.5 kilonewtons, sufficient to initiate a controlled ascent at an initial velocity of approximately 0.1 meters per second, accelerating to the terminal ascent velocity of approximately 1.5 meters per second as the expanding air in the ballast tanks — the tanks are vented from the bottom, and the air that displaces the vented seawater expands as the pod ascends and the external pressure decreases, increasing the pod's buoyancy and therefore its ascent velocity, a positive-feedback effect that the ballast control system mitigates by continually adjusting the vent rate to maintain the target ascent velocity of 1.5 meters per second, which is the maximum safe ascent rate for the human body from a saturation depth of 45 meters — the ascent rate that, per the U.S. Navy Diving Manual, minimizes the risk of decompression sickness in divers who have been saturated at the working depth, who ascend at a rate not exceeding 1.5 meters per minute, not per second — the Atlantis pod, not being a human body, can ascend at 1.5 meters per SECOND, 60 times faster than the maximum human ascent rate, because the pod's pressure hull maintains the internal atmosphere at 1.0 atmosphere, such that the crew is never exposed to the elevated ambient pressure of the water column and therefore does not accumulate the dissolved nitrogen in their tissues that makes a human diver vulnerable to decompression sickness, and the ascent is limited solely by the structural capacity of the pressure hull — which is rated for the 5.5 atmospheres of external pressure at 45 meters and is structurally insensitive to the RATE of pressure change, as the laminate's failure mode (fiber rupture, matrix cracking, delamination) is governed by the absolute magnitude of the applied stress, not by the rate at which the stress is applied — and by the physiological tolerance of the crew to acceleration, as the pod's ascent rate of 1.5 meters per second corresponds to an acceleration of approximately 0.15 g, which is imperceptible to the crew and which is orders of magnitude below the acceleration (approximately 3 to 5 g) that would cause the crew to lose consciousness or to experience the cardiovascular effects (pooling of blood in the lower extremities, reduced cerebral perfusion) that limit the acceleration tolerance of the human body, a physiological constraint that the pod's ascent profile respects with a margin of approximately 20 to 30 times, providing the crew with a comfortable, non-stressful ascent that, under normal circumstances, would be the highlight of the mission — the ride to the surface, the first sight of sunlight through the viewport after 90 days of electric light, the moment of triumph — but that, in the context of the emergency ascent, is a desperate, last-ditch flight from a pod that has become uninhabitable, the crew The evacuation decision matrix — the algorithm that the crew commander uses to determine when the pod has transitioned from a survivable emergency to an unsurvivable emergency — defines four criteria, ANY ONE of which, when satisfied, mandates evacuation: fire uncontrollable (the fire has exceeded the capacity of the handheld extinguishers and the CO₂ flooding system, either because the flooding system failed to actuate, because the fire is in a location — the electrical equipment bay, the battery compartment, the hydrogen fuel cell enclosure — where CO₂ flooding is ineffective or hazardous, or because the fire has breached the watertight boundary and spread to an adjacent compartment, a scenario that, per the fire safety engineer's analysis, renders the pod unrecoverable, as the crew cannot fight fires in multiple compartments simultaneously, the CO₂ flooding system can flood only one compartment at a time, and the progressive failure of the watertight boundaries would eventually expose the crew to the fire's toxic combustion products and oxygen-depleted atmosphere, a situation that the evacuation decision matrix recognizes as the "unstoppable fire" criterion, mandating evacuation without further delay), flood unstoppable (the source of the flooding cannot be located — a scenario that occurs when the flood water has risen to a level that obscures the source, such that the crew cannot visually or tactilely locate the leak, and the ultrasonic leak detector is ineffective because the sound of the flooding water overwhelms the ultrasonic signal from the leak, or because the leak is not a discrete orifice but a crack or a failed seal that does not generate a detectable ultrasonic signature — or, having been located, cannot be sealed because the emergency hull patch kit is not applicable to the geometry or the material of the failed component — a failed viewport, a warped hatch, a ruptured pipe that is subjected to the full external hydrostatic pressure and that cannot be isolated by closing the seacocks because the seacock is inaccessible or has itself failed — a scenario that the flood safety engineer's analysis identifies as the "unisolatable flood" criterion, which, when combined with a flooding rate that exceeds the bilge pump capacity — as defined by the time-to-critical calculation, which projects the time at which the water level will reach the critical elevation of 1.0 meter above the floor, the elevation at which the water will begin to submerge the electrical equipment in the lower equipment bay, creating a risk of electrical short-circuits, arcing, and fire, a risk that compounds the flood hazard and that may, independently, trigger the "fire uncontrollable" criterion, creating a cascade of failures that the crew cannot manage and that mandates immediate evacuation, regardless of the time remaining before the water reaches the 1.0-meter critical elevation, because the electrical hazard — the combination of water and the 48-volt DC Grid-Seed circuits, which can deliver sufficient current to cause electrocution even at low voltage if the body's electrical resistance is reduced by immersion in conductive seawater — is immediately life-threatening and requires the crew to evacuate the flooded compartment without delay, a scenario that the flood safety engineer's analysis identifies as the "compound hazard" criterion, which overrides the time-to-critical calculation and mandates evacuation immediately upon the detection of electrical arcing or the smell of ozone — the characteristic odor of electrical arcing in air, produced by the corona discharge that ionizes the oxygen molecules, creating ozone (O₃), a pale blue gas with a pungent, "electric" odor that is detectable by the human nose at concentrations as low as 0.01 parts per million, well below the 0.1 parts per million 8-hour occupational exposure limit, and that the crew is trained to recognize — "If you smell bleach or electricity, GET OUT" — a memorable, visceral instruction that the training instructor delivers during the flood simulation, and that the crew remembers, because the smell of ozone is not an abstract concept — it is a sensory experience that the simulator, by incorporating a realistic electrical arcing scenario, has burned into the crew's olfactory memory, a memory that, when triggered by the actual smell of ozone during a real flood, will bypass the cognitive processing and trigger the immediate, automatic evacuation response that the crew has practiced, a response that the training psychologist refers to as "olfactory conditioning" — the association of a specific odor with a specific behavioral response, a primitive but highly effective learning mechanism that the brain's limbic system — which processes olfactory information in the amygdala and the hippocampus, the same structures that encode emotional memories and fear responses — has evolved to facilitate, and that the Atlantis training program deliberately exploits, because in an emergency, the milliseconds saved by bypassing the cognitive processing — "What is that smell? Is it dangerous? What should I do?" — in favor of the automatic, conditioned response — "Smell ozone → GET OUT" — could be the difference between a crew member who evacuates in time and a crew member who does not, a difference that the training psychologist measures not in milliseconds but in lives.

The third criterion — toxic atmosphere uncontainable — applies when the gas detection system identifies a concentration of any monitored gas that exceeds the high alarm threshold and the crew cannot identify and isolate the source of the gas within the time that the PARAT 5500 masks provide — 15 minutes per crew member, after which the crew must either have eliminated the source, such that the
