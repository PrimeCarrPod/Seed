## 1. VACUUM TOILET AND PRIMARY SOLID-LIQUID SEPARATION

The waste management architecture deployed aboard the Atlantis-class subsea habitation module commences at the point of human waste generation with a vacuum-flush toilet system derived from marine and aerospace sanitary engineering, specifically adapted from the Evac 900-series vacuum toilet that has accumulated over four decades of operational heritage across naval surface combatants, cruise ships, offshore platforms, and more recently the International Space Station's Russian-built orbital segment, where the fundamental constraint — the absolute minimization of flush-water consumption — is identical to the Atlantis pod's operational requirement, namely, that potable water derived from the reverse osmosis and forward osmosis systems described in the atmospheric management subsystem represents a closed-cycle resource whose diversion to sanitary transport must be ruthlessly minimized to sustain the 90-day autonomous endurance specification without exceeding the pod's total freshwater buffer of 1,200 liters. The vacuum toilet operates on a pressure differential of approximately 0.5 atmospheres between the cabin interior, maintained at 1.0 atm absolute (101.325 kPa) by the environmental control and life support system, and the waste collection tank, which is maintained at approximately 0.5 atm absolute (50.7 kPa) by a dedicated rotary-vane vacuum pump drawing 120 watts continuous electrical power from the Grid-Seed power distribution bus, such that when the flush valve — a pneumatically actuated 75-millimeter-diameter gate valve with EPDM soft-seal seats rated for 100,000 cycles of operation — opens for a duration of 2.5 seconds, the pressure differential accelerates the toilet bowl contents into the transfer pipe at a velocity of approximately 12 meters per second, carrying the solid and liquid waste together with a minimal flush-water volume of precisely 0.5 liters, a volume that represents a 90 percent reduction from the 5 liters per flush characteristic of conventional marine gravity-flush toilets operating at atmospheric pressure and a 95 percent reduction from the 10 liters per flush typical of residential-grade terrestrial toilets, translating over the course of a 90-day mission with 24 occupants — each averaging 5 toilet uses per day, totaling 10,800 flush events — into a potable water savings of approximately 48,600 liters, or roughly 40 times the pod's total freshwater buffer, a calculation that renders the vacuum toilet not merely an engineering optimization but an existential requirement for any closed-cycle underwater habitat that cannot replenish its freshwater supply through the energy-intensive reverse osmosis process more frequently than once every 90 days.

The waste stream exiting the flush valve — a three-phase mixture of solids, liquids, and entrained flush water traveling at 12 meters per second through a 75-millimeter-diameter schedule-40 stainless steel transfer pipe — enters a centrifugal solid-liquid separator of the type developed by the European Space Agency for the MELiSSA (Micro-Ecological Life Support System Alternative) closed-loop life support program and subsequently flight-qualified on the International Space Station's Waste and Hygiene Compartment, wherein the mixture is admitted tangentially into a conical hydrocyclone chamber of 300 millimeters internal diameter at the inlet, tapering to 50 millimeters at the underflow discharge orifice over a length of 600 millimeters, a geometry that induces a rotational velocity of approximately 3,000 revolutions per minute in the fluid phase, generating a centrifugal acceleration of approximately 1,500 g at the hydrocyclone wall, sufficient to drive the denser solid fraction — fecal matter with an effective density of approximately 1,050 to 1,100 kilograms per cubic meter, substantially denser than the surrounding liquid phase at approximately 1,000 to 1,020 kilograms per cubic meter — radially outward to the hydrocyclone wall, where it spirals downward along the conical section under the influence of both gravity and the axial velocity component of the rotating flow field, exiting through the underflow orifice as a concentrated slurry containing approximately 15 to 20 percent solids by mass, while the clarified liquid fraction — urine mixed with the 0.5-liter flush water and carrying dissolved organic compounds, urea at concentrations of approximately 15 to 25 grams per liter, creatinine, uric acid, and trace pharmaceutical metabolites — reverses its axial direction at the base of the conical section and flows upward through the central vortex core, exiting through the overflow orifice as a liquid stream of approximately 2.5 to 3.0 liters per flush event, which is immediately directed to the liquid treatment subsystem for the sequential processes of urease hydrolysis, ammonia electro-oxidation, and nitrate ion exchange polishing that together recover approximately 85 percent of the input water volume for reintegration into the pod's closed-cycle water management system. The centrifugal separator achieves a solid-liquid separation efficiency of approximately 92 to 95 percent as measured by the total suspended solids reduction across the unit, a performance level that has been validated through 5,000 hours of continuous operation at the Carrington Storm Motors life support test facility in Mojave using a synthetic fecal simulant developed in accordance with NASA-STD-3001 Volume 2 Revision C, which specifies the physical and chemical properties of human waste for the qualification testing of spacecraft life support equipment and which the Atlantis program has adopted as its testing standard in the absence of a dedicated underwater habitat waste management specification.

## 2. SOLID WASTE MICROWAVE DRYING AND THERMAL PATHOGEN ELIMINATION

The concentrated solid slurry exiting the hydrocyclone underflow — approximately 0.8 to 1.2 liters per flush event at 15 to 20 percent solids content, or approximately 4.0 to 6.0 liters per day per pod of four occupants — is transferred via a progressing-cavity pump with a stainless steel rotor and a nitrile stator, selected for its tolerance of abrasive slurries and its ability to deliver a consistent volumetric flow rate independent of the slurry's variable viscosity, into a microwave drying chamber that constitutes the core innovation of the Atlantis solid waste processing system and that addresses simultaneously the three critical objectives of volume reduction for long-term storage, pathogen elimination for crew health protection, and odor control for habitability maintenance within the 3.3-meter internal diameter of the pod's pressure hull. The microwave drying chamber is a cylindrical vessel of 400 millimeters internal diameter and 600 millimeters length, fabricated from 316L stainless steel with a PTFE internal liner to prevent waste adhesion to the chamber walls — a phenomenon that plagued early prototype designs and was resolved through the application of a hydrophobic fluoropolymer coating deposited via plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition at a thickness of 50 micrometers and exhibiting a water contact angle of 115 degrees, at which fecal simulant slurries exhibit a contact angle exceeding 90 degrees and therefore bead up on the surface rather than spreading and adhering, enabling complete discharge of the dried solids into the collection bin through a simple gravity-assisted drop when the chamber's bottom hatch opens at the conclusion of each drying cycle. The microwave energy is supplied by a magnetron operating at the Industrial, Scientific, and Medical (ISM) band frequency of 2.45 gigahertz — the same frequency employed in commercial microwave ovens, which ensures the availability of mature, reliable, and cost-effective magnetron technology with mean time between failures exceeding 10,000 hours for continuous-duty industrial units — with a power output of 1.2 kilowatts, delivered to the drying chamber through a WR-340 rectangular waveguide terminated in a mode stirrer that rotates at 60 revolutions per minute to randomize the standing wave pattern within the chamber and thereby ensure uniform heating of the waste mass despite the inherently inhomogeneous dielectric properties of a multi-component slurry containing regions of high water content (dielectric constant ε' ≈ 78, loss tangent tan δ ≈ 0.15 at 2.45 GHz), regions of high organic solid content (ε' ≈ 5-15, tan δ ≈ 0.05-0.10), and regions of intermediate composition at the solid-liquid interface where the dielectric heating rate is determined by the local effective permittivity weighted by the volumetric mixing ratio of the two phases. The magnetron delivers its 1.2 kW of microwave power to the approximately 1.0 kilogram wet waste mass — which is primarily water, as the 15 to 20 percent solids content implies that 80 to 85 percent of the wet mass is liquid water that must be evaporated to achieve the target dried solids concentration of 90 percent — over a drying cycle of approximately 35 to 45 minutes, during which the waste temperature is ramped from the ambient cabin temperature of approximately 22°C to the target terminal temperature of 120°C, held at that temperature for a dwell period of 15 minutes to ensure the thermal inactivation of pathogens including Escherichia coli (thermal death time at 120°C: approximately 2 seconds), Salmonella enterica (approximately 5 seconds), Clostridium difficile endospores (approximately 3 minutes at 120°C moist heat, per FDA guidelines for the thermal processing of low-acid canned foods), hepatitis A virus (approximately 30 seconds at 100°C), and the full spectrum of enteric viruses and protozoan cysts identified in the World Health Organization's Guidelines for the Safe Use of Wastewater, Excreta, and Greywater, Volume IV, with the 15-minute dwell period at 120°C providing a safety factor of approximately 300 relative to the most thermally resistant pathogen of concern (C. difficile endospores), a margin that the Atlantis program's medical officer has certified as sufficient to reduce the probability of crew infection via the fecal-oral route to below 10^-6 per 90-day mission, a standard derived from the NASA Human Integration Design Handbook (NASA/SP-2010-3407) requirements for spacecraft microbial contamination control and adopted by the Aegis C program as its internal safety criterion for all closed-cycle habitation systems.

The water vapor generated during the microwave drying process — approximately 0.75 to 0.95 liters per cycle, representing the evaporation of approximately 80 to 85 percent of the initial water mass in the slurry — is extracted from the drying chamber by a vacuum pump that maintains the chamber pressure at approximately 20 kPa absolute, a sub-atmospheric pressure that reduces the boiling point of water to approximately 60°C and thereby accelerates the evaporation rate through the increased vapor pressure driving force between the wet waste surface and the surrounding atmosphere, while simultaneously reducing the energy required to heat the waste to the evaporation temperature because the sensible heat that must be supplied to raise the water from 22°C to 60°C (approximately 159 kilojoules per kilogram) is substantially less than the sensible heat required to raise it to 100°C at atmospheric pressure (approximately 326 kilojoules per kilogram), a difference of approximately 167 kilojoules per kilogram that, multiplied by the approximately 0.85 kilograms of water evaporated per cycle and the approximately 1,800 drying cycles executed over a 90-day mission, translates to an energy savings of approximately 256 megajoules, or approximately 71 kilowatt-hours, which is approximately 1.4 percent of the pod's total battery capacity of 5,000 kilowatt-hours and therefore represents a meaningful contribution to the overall energy efficiency of the life support system, consistent with the Grid-Seed architecture's design philosophy that every joule of energy conserved in one subsystem is a joule available for another subsystem, and that the aggregate effect of multiple small efficiency improvements across all life support components — the vacuum toilet's 90 percent water reduction, the microwave dryer's sub-atmospheric operation, the forward osmosis system's waste-heat-driven regeneration, the LED lighting system's 80 percent efficiency relative to fluorescent alternatives — compounds to extend the pod's autonomous endurance from the baseline 60 days to the specified 90 days without increasing the battery capacity or the ocean-current turbine generation capacity, a systems-engineering optimization that ZIRCONIA validated through a Monte Carlo simulation of 10,000 randomized mission scenarios with stochastic variations in crew metabolic rates, equipment power draws, and environmental conditions, confirming that the integrated life support system achieves a 96.7 percent probability of maintaining all environmental parameters within specified limits for the full 90-day mission duration.

The dried solid waste, reduced to approximately 0.15 to 0.25 kilograms per flush event at 90 percent solids content from an initial wet mass of approximately 1.0 kilogram — representing an 85 percent volume reduction from the original slurry, as the density of the dried solids (approximately 800 to 900 kilograms per cubic meter for the porous, desiccated organic matrix) is roughly comparable to the density of the wet slurry (approximately 1,020 kilograms per cubic meter), so that the mass reduction translates approximately linearly into a volume reduction of the same magnitude — is discharged from the microwave drying chamber into a collection container that is hermetically sealed after each drying cycle by a heat-sealing mechanism that thermally welds a multi-layer barrier film — composed of an outer layer of polyethylene terephthalate for mechanical strength, a middle layer of aluminum foil for gas impermeability, and an inner layer of linear low-density polyethylene for heat-sealability, the same trilaminate construction used in the space food packaging developed for the Apollo program and subsequently refined for the International Space Station's food system — across the container opening at a temperature of 160°C for a dwell time of 2 seconds, creating a gas-tight seal that prevents the release of odors into the cabin atmosphere and that maintains a negative pressure of approximately 5 kPa within the sealed container relative to the cabin, such that any leakage through a defective seal — an event with a probability of approximately 10^-4 per seal cycle based on statistical process control data from the packaging machine manufacturer and validated through helium leak testing of 1,000 consecutive seals at the Mojave test facility — would result in an in-leakage of cabin air into the container rather than an out-leakage of malodorous compounds into the breathable atmosphere, a fail-safe design principle that the Kiddo register insists upon for any barrier separating crew from waste in a closed-volume habitation environment.

## 3. LIQUID WASTE TREATMENT: UREASE HYDROLYSIS, ELECTRO-OXIDATION, AND ION EXCHANGE

The liquid waste stream exiting the hydrocyclone overflow — approximately 2.5 to 3.0 liters per flush event, consisting of urine, flush water, and dissolved organic and inorganic compounds, with a total daily volume of approximately 12.5 to 15.0 liters per pod of four occupants — enters a three-stage treatment sequence whose primary objective is the removal of nitrogenous compounds, specifically urea (CO(NH₂)₂) and its hydrolysis product ammonia (NH₃), which constitute the dominant chemical oxygen demand and the primary toxicity challenge in human urine, with urea concentrations of 15 to 25 grams per liter and ammonia concentrations (after hydrolysis) of 5 to 10 grams per liter, levels that if discharged untreated into the forward osmosis brine stream would poison the osmotic membrane through ammonia permeation — ammonia, being a small, uncharged molecule at neutral pH, diffuses through polyamide reverse osmosis and forward osmosis membranes with a rejection coefficient of only approximately 70 to 85 percent, compared to the >99.5 percent rejection achieved for sodium chloride, and would therefore accumulate in the recycled water stream, reaching concentrations that cause neurological toxicity at blood ammonia levels exceeding 100 micromoles per liter — and that must therefore be eliminated to below 5 milligrams per liter in the treated effluent to meet the Atlantis program's internal water quality standard, which is approximately an order of magnitude more stringent than the World Health Organization's guideline value of 1.5 milligrams per liter for ammonia in drinking water.

The first stage — urease-catalyzed hydrolysis of urea to ammonia and carbon dioxide — is effected in a packed-bed bioreactor of 200 millimeters internal diameter and 500 millimeters bed height, packed with porous silica gel beads of 2 to 4 millimeters diameter onto which the enzyme urease (urea amidohydrolase, EC 3.5.1.5) has been immobilized via covalent bonding to glutaraldehyde-activated amino groups on the silanized silica surface, a immobilization technique adapted from the industrial biocatalysis literature and optimized for the Atlantis application through the selection of a mesoporous silica substrate with an average pore diameter of 15 nanometers, which is large enough to accommodate the urease molecule (a hexameric protein of approximately 480 kilodaltons with a hydrodynamic diameter of approximately 10 nanometers) while providing a specific surface area of approximately 250 square meters per gram for enzyme loading, achieving a final enzyme loading density of approximately 50 milligrams of urease protein per gram of silica support, a loading that provides sufficient catalytic activity to hydrolyze the urea contained in the daily liquid waste volume of 12.5 to 15.0 liters at a hydraulic retention time of approximately 4 hours, during which the urea concentration is reduced from 15-25 grams per liter at the reactor inlet to below 100 milligrams per liter at the reactor outlet, corresponding to a urea conversion efficiency of approximately 99.5 percent, with the hydrolysis reaction proceeding according to the stoichiometric equation CO(NH₂)₂ + H₂O → 2NH₃ + CO₂, which liberates two moles of ammonia and one mole of carbon dioxide for each mole of urea hydrolyzed, resulting in an effluent pH of approximately 9.0 to 9.5 — elevated from the inlet pH of approximately 6.0 to 7.0 — due to the ammonia's behavior as a weak base (pK_b = 4.75) in aqueous solution, a pH shift that the electro-oxidation stage is specifically designed to accommodate through the selection of a nickel-based anode whose catalytic activity for ammonia oxidation is optimal in the alkaline pH range of 9 to 11, where the predominant ammonia species is the un-ionized NH₃ molecule rather than the ammonium ion NH₄⁺ (pK_a = 9.25), the former being the electrochemically active species at the nickel hydroxide anode surface.

The second stage — ammonia electro-oxidation to dinitrogen gas — employs a flow-through electrochemical reactor in which the ammonia-laden effluent from the urease bioreactor passes through a series of ten parallel-plate electrode pairs, each consisting of a nickel hydroxide/nickel oxyhydroxide (Ni(OH)₂/NiOOH) anode and a stainless steel cathode, with an inter-electrode gap of 3 millimeters and an applied cell voltage of 1.4 volts relative to the standard hydrogen electrode, a potential that is deliberately maintained below the thermodynamic threshold for water electrolysis (1.23 volts at standard conditions) to minimize the parasitic oxygen evolution reaction that would compete with ammonia oxidation for the available anodic current and reduce the faradaic efficiency of the ammonia-to-dinitrogen conversion. The electrochemical oxidation of ammonia at the Ni(OH)₂/NiOOH anode proceeds via the mechanism first elucidated by Botte and colleagues in their seminal 2004-2012 investigations of ammonia electrolysis for wastewater treatment, in which the Ni(OH)₂ species is electrochemically oxidized to NiOOH at the anode surface at potentials above approximately 1.3 volts, and the NiOOH species subsequently oxidizes ammonia through a direct chemical reaction — rather than through electron transfer at the electrode surface — that regenerates Ni(OH)₂ and produces nitrogen gas, water, and protons in a process that can be summarized by the overall reaction 2NH₃ + 6OH⁻ → N₂ + 6H₂O + 6e⁻ for the alkaline electrolyte conditions prevailing in the Atlantis reactor, with the six electrons per two ammonia molecules transferred to the anode and conducted through the external circuit to the cathode, where they reduce water to hydrogen gas (2H₂O + 2e⁻ → H₂ + 2OH⁻), generating hydrogen at a rate of approximately 90 liters per day (at standard temperature and pressure) that is directed to the hydrogen fuel cell backup power system for electrical energy recovery rather than vented, a design feature that recovers approximately 0.5 kilowatt-hours of electrical energy per day from the ammonia oxidation process — a modest but non-negligible contribution representing approximately 0.4 percent of the pod's daily energy consumption of approximately 120 kilowatt-hours, consistent with the "no joule wasted" design philosophy. The ammonia removal efficiency of the electrochemical reactor is approximately 90 percent at the nominal hydraulic retention time of 2 hours, reducing the ammonia-nitrogen concentration from approximately 5,000 to 10,000 milligrams per liter at the reactor inlet to approximately 500 to 1,000 milligrams per liter at the reactor outlet, a level that exceeds the Atlantis internal standard of 5 milligrams per liter by a factor of approximately 100 to 200 and that necessitates the third treatment stage — ion exchange polishing — to achieve the final water quality specification required for reintegration into the forward osmosis feed stream.

The third stage — nitrate and residual ammonia removal via ion exchange — employs a mixed-bed ion exchange column of 150 millimeters internal diameter and 600 millimeters bed height, packed with a homogeneous mixture of strong-acid cation exchange resin (polystyrene-divinylbenzene matrix with sulfonic acid functional groups, in the sodium form, with a total exchange capacity of 2.0 equivalents per liter of wet resin) and strong-base anion exchange resin (polystyrene-divinylbenzene matrix with quaternary ammonium functional groups, in the chloride form, with a total exchange capacity of 1.4 equivalents per liter of wet resin), in a volumetric ratio of 60 percent cation resin to 40 percent anion resin, a ratio that reflects the predominance of ammonium cations over nitrate anions in the electro-oxidation effluent. The ion exchange column reduces the ammonia-nitrogen concentration from approximately 500-1,000 milligrams per liter at the column inlet to below 5 milligrams per liter at the column outlet and simultaneously reduces the nitrate concentration — which forms as a minor byproduct of the electro-oxidation process through the over-oxidation of ammonia at anode potentials that transiently exceed the 1.4-volt setpoint, a phenomenon that accounts for approximately 5 to 10 percent of the total ammonia oxidation — from approximately 50-100 milligrams per liter to below 10 milligrams per liter, both values comfortably satisfying the Atlantis internal water quality standard and the World Health Organization's guideline value of 50 milligrams per liter for nitrate in drinking water. The ion exchange resins are regenerable: once the column reaches exhaustion — determined by an online ammonia ion-selective electrode at the column outlet that triggers an alarm when the ammonia concentration exceeds the 5 milligram-per-liter threshold — the column is isolated from the treatment stream and the resins are regenerated using a 10 percent sodium chloride solution (for the cation resin) and a 5 percent sodium chloride solution (for the anion resin), with the spent regenerant — a concentrated brine containing ammonium chloride and sodium nitrate — being directed to the brine management subsystem for further treatment via electrodialysis, which recovers approximately 90 percent of the sodium chloride for reuse in subsequent regeneration cycles and concentrates the ammonium and nitrate into a small volume (approximately 2 liters per regeneration) of high-concentration brine that is combined with the dried solid waste for surface disposal at the conclusion of the mission, a zero-liquid-discharge approach that avoids the environmental impact of releasing nitrogenous compounds into the marine environment at the Atlantis demonstration sites, which are located within the Monterey Bay National Marine Sanctuary and adjacent to the Corcovado National Park marine protected area, respectively, and are therefore subject to the most stringent environmental discharge regulations enforced by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration and the Costa Rican Ministry of Environment and Energy.

The treated liquid effluent from the ion exchange column — approximately 10.6 to 12.8 liters per day, representing approximately 85 percent of the original liquid waste volume, with the 15 percent loss attributable to water vapor evaporated during the microwave drying process and not recovered because the vacuum pump exhaust — water vapor at approximately 60°C and 20 kPa — is vented to the pod's humidity control system for condensation and reintegration into the grey water recycling loop rather than the potable water loop, a precaution against potential carryover of volatile organic compounds that may have survived the urease hydrolysis and electro-oxidation processes at trace concentrations below the detection limits of the online water quality monitors — is directed to the forward osmosis system, where it serves as the draw solution diluent, its low solute concentration (total dissolved solids below 50 milligrams per liter) making it ideal for this purpose because it maximizes the osmotic pressure gradient across the forward osmosis membrane and thereby maximizes the water flux for a given membrane area, reducing the forward osmosis system's energy consumption — which is dominated by the pumping energy required to circulate the draw solution — by approximately 5 to 8 percent compared to the use of untreated seawater as the draw solution diluent.

## 4. GREY WATER RECYCLING AND NON-BIOLOGICAL WASTE COMPACTION

The grey water recycling system processes the Atlantis pod's non-toilet wastewater streams — shower water, galley sink water, hand-washing station effluent, and the condensate from the atmospheric humidity control system — which together constitute a flow of approximately 40 to 60 liters per day per pod of four occupants, or approximately 3.6 to 5.4 cubic meters over the 90-day mission, a volume that, if discharged untreated, would represent both an environmental impact on the marine sanctuary and a significant loss of water from the closed-cycle life support system, equivalent to approximately 40 to 60 percent of the pod's total freshwater buffer of 1,200 liters and therefore an unacceptable engineering compromise. The grey water treatment train consists of three sequential unit operations: a 50-micrometer stainless steel prefilter — a wedge-wire screen with a filtration area of 0.2 square meters, selected for its resistance to biofouling in the warm, nutrient-rich grey water environment, where heterotrophic bacterial growth rates can reach 0.5 per hour at the 25-30°C water temperatures typical of the pod's plumbing system, corresponding to a biofilm doubling time of approximately 1.4 hours and a potential for rapid filter clogging that is mitigated by the wedge-wire screen's self-cleaning geometry, in which the triangular cross-section of the wedge wires creates a narrowing gap in the direction of flow that prevents particles from becoming permanently lodged between adjacent wires — rated for the removal of suspended solids larger than 50 micrometers (hair, skin cells, food particles, soap precipitates, and lint from crew clothing), which constitute approximately 80 to 90 percent of the total suspended solids load in grey water and which, if not removed, would foul the downstream activated carbon bed within approximately 100 hours of operation, reducing its adsorption capacity for dissolved organic compounds by more than 90 percent and requiring premature carbon replacement that is logistically impossible during a 90-day sealed-habitat mission; an activated carbon adsorption column of 200 millimeters internal diameter, 800 millimeters bed height, packed with granular activated carbon derived from coconut shell feedstock, steam-activated at 900°C to produce a specific surface area of approximately 1,200 square meters per gram (BET nitrogen adsorption method), and exhibiting an iodine number of 1,100 milligrams per gram, an industry-standard metric that correlates with the carbon's capacity for adsorbing small organic molecules, which in the grey water context include surfactants from soaps and detergents (linear alkylbenzene sulfonates and alcohol ethoxylates at typical concentrations of 10 to 50 milligrams per liter), humic substances derived from skin and food residues, and trace pharmaceutical compounds excreted through the skin in sweat — particularly caffeine, acetaminophen, and ibuprofen at nanogram-per-liter concentrations that are nonetheless detectable by the pod's online total organic carbon analyzer and that, if accumulated in the recycled water loop over multiple reuse cycles, could reach concentrations exceeding the no-observed-adverse-effect levels established by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency's Guidelines for Water Reuse (EPA/600/R-12/618) — with the activated carbon column reducing the total organic carbon concentration from approximately 50 to 150 milligrams per liter at the column inlet to below 5 milligrams per liter at the column outlet, corresponding to a removal efficiency of approximately 95 to 97 percent and a column service life of approximately 45 days before breakthrough occurs and the carbon must be replaced, a task that the crew performs during the mid-mission resupply visit scheduled at day 45 of the 90-day mission; and an ultraviolet disinfection reactor employing a low-pressure mercury vapor lamp emitting at the germicidal wavelength of 253.7 nanometers, with a UV dose of 40 millijoules per square centimeter — the dose recommended by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency's Ultraviolet Disinfection Guidance Manual for the inactivation of viruses, which represent the most UV-resistant class of waterborne pathogens, with adenovirus requiring a dose of approximately 40 mJ/cm² for 4-log (99.99 percent) inactivation — delivered through a reactor chamber of 100 millimeters internal diameter with the UV lamp mounted concentrically within a quartz sleeve of 30 millimeters outer diameter, providing an annular flow path of 35 millimeters radial width through which the grey water flows at a rate of approximately 2 liters per minute, corresponding to a hydraulic residence time in the irradiated zone of approximately 30 seconds, during which each fluid element receives an average UV dose of 40 mJ/cm² as calculated by computational fluid dynamics modeling of the reactor's dose distribution using the Lagrangian particle-tracking method with 100,000 simulated particle trajectories through the velocity field solved by the Navier-Stokes equations for the reactor geometry, confirming that 99.9 percent of fluid elements receive a dose exceeding 40 mJ/cm², with the 0.1 percent that receive lower doses being subject to multiple passes through the reactor due to the recirculating nature of the grey water system — the treated effluent is stored in a 200-liter intermediate tank from which it is drawn for toilet flushing (approximately 12 liters per day, replacing the 0.5-liter-per-flush vacuum toilet demand with recycled grey water rather than potable water from the reverse osmosis system, a substitution that conserves approximately 1,080 liters of potable water over the 90-day mission) and for irrigation of the living plant wall (approximately 5 to 10 liters per day), with the balance of approximately 30 liters per day being reintegrated into the forward osmosis feed stream after passing through an additional ultrafiltration membrane with a molecular weight cutoff of 10 kilodaltons, which provides a second barrier against any microbial or viral contaminants that may have survived the UV disinfection step at UV doses below the 40 mJ/cm² design value.

The non-biological solid waste stream — consisting of food packaging materials (primarily aluminum-laminated polymer films from the retort-pouch meal system, accounting for approximately 1.5 kilograms per person per day, or approximately 144 kilograms for a 4-person pod over 90 days), personal hygiene products (approximately 0.3 kilograms per person per day), worn-out clothing and textiles (approximately 0.2 kilograms per person per day), expended air filters (HEPA and activated carbon, replaced monthly, approximately 2 kilograms each), and miscellaneous maintenance waste including spent ion exchange resins from the water treatment system (approximately 5 kilograms per resin regeneration, with four regenerations over the 90-day mission for a total of approximately 20 kilograms) — is processed through a hydraulic compaction system that reduces the waste volume by a factor of approximately 20:1, from approximately 6 to 8 cubic meters of loose, uncompacted waste with a bulk density of approximately 50 to 80 kilograms per cubic meter to approximately 0.3 to 0.4 cubic meters of dense, compacted pucks with a bulk density of approximately 1,000 to 1,200 kilograms per cubic meter, a volume that can be accommodated within the pod's external unpressurized waste storage locker — a 1.5-cubic-meter compartment mounted on the pod's exterior pressure hull within the ROV-accessible service module, fabricated from 316L stainless steel with a corrosion allowance of 3 millimeters for the 50-year design life in seawater, and equipped with a remotely operated hatch that allows the Charlemagne fleet's work-class ROV to retrieve the waste pucks during the scheduled 45-day resupply visit for transport to the surface, where they are transferred to the Charlemagne mothership for onshore disposal at a licensed solid waste facility, or, in the emergency scenario where surface access is unavailable during a crustal displacement event, the waste pucks are designed to be jettisoned from the storage locker via a pneumatically actuated ejection mechanism that propels them clear of the pod's hull — at a velocity of approximately 2 meters per second, sufficient to overcome the negative buoyancy of the compacted waste in seawater (density approximately 1,100 kilograms per cubic meter versus seawater at 1,025 kilograms per cubic meter) and to carry the pucks approximately 5 meters from the hull before they settle to the seabed, where they will eventually be colonized by benthic organisms and incorporated into the seafloor sediment in a process that, while not ideal from an environmental perspective, is considered acceptable in the context of a planetary-scale catastrophe where the survival of the pod's occupants takes precedence over the localized environmental impact of disposing of approximately 0.3 cubic meters of compacted waste onto the seabed — a decision that has been reviewed by the Atlantis program's environmental compliance officer and documented in the project's Environmental Assessment, filed with the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration in accordance with the National Environmental Policy Act.

## 5. ELECTRIC INCINERATION ALTERNATIVE AND WASTE STORAGE ARCHITECTURE

As an alternative to compaction and surface disposal — and as the preferred method for the processing of biologically hazardous waste including used medical supplies (syringes, bandages, surgical drapes, and biological sample containers), expired medications from the 120-drug formulary, and any waste materials that have been in contact with crew members diagnosed with infectious diseases during the mission, for which the microwave drying and hydraulic compaction processes may not provide sufficient pathogen inactivation — the Atlantis pod is equipped with an electric incineration system consisting of a cylindrical combustion chamber of 300 millimeters internal diameter and 500 millimeters length, fabricated from Inconel 625 nickel-chromium-molybdenum alloy — selected for its exceptional resistance to high-temperature oxidation and to the corrosive combustion products of chlorinated plastics and pharmaceutical compounds, which generate hydrochloric acid (HCl) gas at concentrations of up to 500 parts per million in the flue gas when polyvinyl chloride medical waste is incinerated — and heated by silicon carbide resistance heating elements operating at a temperature of 850°C, the minimum temperature specified by the European Union's Waste Incineration Directive (2000/76/EC) for the complete destruction of organic compounds, at which polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs), dioxins, and furans — the most thermally stable and toxicologically significant products of incomplete combustion — undergo greater than 99.9999 percent destruction, with the 850°C operating temperature being maintained for a residence time of at least 2 seconds, as measured from the point of last combustion air injection to the point of flue gas exit from the combustion chamber, in accordance with the design requirements of 40 CFR Part 60 (Standards of Performance for New Stationary Sources) Subpart Ec (Hospital/Medical/Infectious Waste Incinerators). The incinerator processes waste at a rate of approximately 2 kilograms per hour — a deliberately low throughput that reflects the pod's limited electricity budget, as the 2-kilogram-per-hour throughput requires approximately 3 kilowatts of electrical power to maintain the 850°C combustion temperature, representing approximately 2.5 percent of the pod's continuous power budget of 120 kilowatt-hours per day — and reduces the waste mass by approximately 97 percent, leaving an ash residue of approximately 3 percent by mass that is collected in a sealed stainless steel canister for surface disposal at the conclusion of the mission or during the mid-mission resupply visit, with the ash volume being approximately 0.05 cubic meters per 90-day mission, sufficiently small to be stored within the pod's internal waste locker without occupying more than 3 percent of the locker's 1.5-cubic-meter capacity.

The flue gas from the electric incinerator — comprising carbon dioxide, water vapor, trace hydrochloric acid, sulfur dioxide (from the combustion of sulfur-containing amino acids in biological waste), and particulate matter with an aerodynamic diameter of less than 2.5 micrometers (PM2.5) at concentrations that would exceed the pod's internal air quality standard of 35 micrograms per cubic meter (24-hour average, per the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency's National Ambient Air Quality Standards) — is treated by a two-stage emission control system before being vented to the pod's cabin atmosphere. The first stage is a wet scrubber consisting of a packed-bed column of 100 millimeters internal diameter and 400 millimeters packed height, filled with 10-millimeter polypropylene Pall rings, through which the flue gas flows countercurrent to a recirculating scrubbing solution of 5 percent sodium hydroxide (NaOH) in water, which neutralizes the acid gases — HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O, SO₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₃ + H₂O — with a removal efficiency exceeding 99 percent for both acid species, as validated by pH monitoring of the scrubbing solution, which is automatically replenished with fresh sodium hydroxide from a 5-liter reservoir when the pH drops below 10, indicating that the scrubbing solution's alkalinity has been consumed. The second stage is a high-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filter rated at 99.97 percent removal efficiency for particles with a diameter of 0.3 micrometers — the most penetrating particle size for fibrous filtration media, at which the combined effects of diffusion (dominant below 0.1 micrometers), interception (dominant at 0.1 to 1 micrometer), and impaction (dominant above 1 micrometer) produce a minimum in the filtration efficiency curve that defines the HEPA rating standard per IEST-RP-CC001.6 — which captures the particulate matter, including the sub-micron metal oxide particles that may be entrained in the flue gas from the incineration of pharmaceutical compounds containing metals (such as iron from iron supplements or zinc from zinc oxide ointments), reducing the particulate concentration in the treated flue gas to below 1 microgram per cubic meter, which is then further diluted by the pod's ventilation system operating at 10 air changes per hour, ensuring that the steady-state particulate concentration in the cabin atmosphere — even with the incinerator operating continuously at its maximum throughput of 2 kilograms per hour — does not exceed 5 micrograms per cubic meter, well below the 35 micrograms per cubic meter 24-hour standard and providing a comfortable margin of safety for the crew.

The primary waste storage architecture for the 90-day mission — regardless of whether the compaction or incineration pathway is employed — is the external unpressurized storage locker, a compartment of approximately 1.5 cubic meters internal volume mounted on the pod's exterior within the ROV-accessible service module, accessed via a hatch of 400 millimeters diameter that is sealed with a double O-ring closure mechanism rated for the full 5-atmosphere external pressure at the pod's 45-meter operational depth plus a 50 percent safety factor, for a design pressure of 7.5 atmospheres (approximately 760 kilopascals). The locker is isolated from the pod's interior pressure boundary by the hatch — there is no direct connection between the locker's interior atmosphere (seawater that enters when the hatch is opened underwater) and the pod's cabin atmosphere, eliminating any risk of odor migration from the stored waste into the habitable volume — and the waste pucks or ash canisters are transferred from the interior waste processing compartment to the external locker via a rectangular transfer port of 300 millimeters by 400 millimeters cross-section that passes through the pressure hull, fitted with pressure-rated doors on both the interior and exterior sides, such that the interior door is opened to load waste, then closed and sealed, after which the exterior door is opened by the ROV to extract the waste, a procedure that never exposes the cabin atmosphere to seawater and that maintain the pressure hull's integrity at all times, consistent with the American Bureau of Shipping's Guide for Underwater Vehicles, Systems, and Hyperbaric Facilities (ABS Publication #5) requirements for pressure boundary penetrations in human-occupied underwater structures.

Odor control within the waste processing compartment — a 2.5-cubic-meter space located in the pod's lower equipment bay, separated from the main living compartment by a watertight door that is normally kept closed — is achieved through a combination of activated carbon adsorption, negative pressure ventilation, and air exchange rate management. The compartment is maintained at a negative pressure of approximately 25 pascals relative to the adjacent living spaces by a dedicated exhaust fan that draws air from the waste processing compartment through a bed of activated carbon — the same coconut-shell-derived granular activated carbon used in the grey water treatment system, with a specific surface area of 1,200 square meters per gram and an iodine number of 1,100 milligrams per gram, configured in a replaceable cartridge format that is changed monthly — before discharging the treated air into the cabin's main ventilation return duct, where it is further diluted by a factor of approximately 100 through mixing with the main cabin air circulation of approximately 500 cubic meters per hour (or approximately 10 air changes per hour in the pod's 50-cubic-meter habitable volume, a ventilation rate that is approximately double the minimum requirement of 5 air changes per hour specified by the American Society of Heating, Refrigerating, and Air-Conditioning Engineers Standard 62.1 for enclosed occupied spaces, reflecting the conservatism inherent in the Atlantis life support design philosophy, which applies a safety factor of 2.0 to all ventilation specifications that relate to crew health in recognition of the impossibility of opening a window or evacuating to fresh air at a depth of 45 meters below the ocean surface). The activated carbon bed removes volatile organic compounds, including hydrogen sulfide (H₂S, the "rotten egg" odor), methyl mercaptan (CH₃SH), dimethyl sulfide ((CH₃)₂S), and the suite of volatile fatty acids (acetic, propionic, butyric) that are produced by the anaerobic microbial decomposition of fecal matter in the brief interval — approximately 30 to 60 minutes — between the toilet flush event and the initiation of the microwave drying cycle, during which the waste is held in the centrifugally separated solids collection hopper, a holding period that is deliberately minimized through the scheduling of microwave drying cycles to commence immediately after each flush event rather than accumulating multiple flush events for batch processing, a design decision that reduces the maximum holding time to the 35-45 minute drying cycle duration of the preceding flush event and thereby limits the production of malodorous volatile compounds through microbial metabolism to levels that the activated carbon adsorption system can comfortably manage, maintaining the odor concentration in the waste processing compartment — as measured by an electronic nose instrument employing an array of metal oxide semiconductor sensors calibrated against a human odor panel per ASTM E544-18 (Standard Practice for Referencing Suprathreshold Odor Intensity) — at an odor intensity of less than 2 on the ASTM 0-to-5 scale, corresponding to an odor that is "faint" or "barely perceptible" and that would not be detectable in the main living compartment given the 25-pascal negative pressure differential and the intervening watertight door, a design target that has been validated through a 90-day continuous simulation of the waste processing system at the Mojave test facility, with daily odor panel assessments conducted by a team of five trained panelists who confirmed that the odor intensity in the simulated living compartment remained at 0 ("no perceptible odor") for the full 90-day test duration, even during periods when the microwave dryer was processing waste at its maximum design throughput.
