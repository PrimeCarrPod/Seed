# PART-067 — SOLID-OXIDE ELECTROLYSIS: OXYGEN GENERATION AND HYDROGEN CO-PRODUCTION FOR SUBSEA LIFE SUPPORT

## CSMFAB077-Deep | Life-Support Subsystem | Aegis C Material Architecture

---

The provision of breathable oxygen within a permanently submerged habitation module operating at 45 meters depth in the Monterey Canyon, with no surface umbilical and no possibility of atmospheric exchange during the crustal displacement event itself — a period of approximately 90 hours from initial wave arrival through stalling phase to turbidite recession completion — demands an oxygen generation technology that is simultaneously compact, efficient, thermally manageable, and capable of continuous operation for the full 90-day mission duration between surface resupply intervals, a set of constraints that eliminates from consideration the conventional oxygen supply methods employed in terrestrial and aerospace life support: compressed gaseous oxygen storage, which at 200 bar would require a storage volume of approximately 0.42 cubic meters per kilogram of O₂ and would consume roughly 1.4 cubic meters of internal pod volume for a single 90-day resupply interval, representing 2.1 percent of the total habitable volume of the Atlantis-class module, not accounting for the additional volume penalty of the Type IV composite overwrapped pressure vessels required for underwater pressure compensation; liquid oxygen storage, which despite its superior density of 1.141 kg/L at 90 K would require a vacuum-insulated dewar with an active cryocooler consuming approximately 150 watts of continuous electrical power to maintain the 90 K storage temperature, a parasitic load that would reduce the available power budget for other life support functions and introduce a single-point failure mode — loss of cryocooler power leading to LOX boil-off and catastrophic pressure buildup within a sealed underwater enclosure; and chlorate candle emergency oxygen generators, which are reliable and energy-independent but produce oxygen at a mass-specific rate of only 0.37 kg O₂ per kilogram of sodium chlorate, meaning that a 90-day supply for four persons consuming 3.3 kg O₂/day would require approximately 3,200 kilograms of chlorate candles occupying 2.9 cubic meters of pod volume, with the additional hazard that chlorate decomposition is exothermic and self-sustaining once initiated, producing internal temperatures exceeding 600°C that would pose an unacceptable fire risk within a sealed, oxygen-enriched underwater environment. The solid-oxide electrolysis cell (SOEC) operating at 850°C, with its capacity to split water molecules into gaseous oxygen and hydrogen at a Faraday efficiency exceeding 95 percent using electrical energy supplied by the ocean-current turbine and Grid-Seed battery architecture described in Parts 068 and 069, represents the optimal solution to the Atlantis-class oxygen generation requirement, offering a mass-specific oxygen production rate that is approximately 30 times higher than chlorate candles, a volumetric efficiency that permits integration within the pod's thermal management infrastructure — the 850°C operating temperature, rather than being a liability, becomes a thermal resource that can be recuperated through the balance-of-plant heat exchangers to preheat feedwater and to drive the forward osmosis draw-solution regeneration cycle described in Part 066 — and a dual-product output in which the hydrogen co-product, produced at a rate of 0.41 kg/day, provides the fuel stock for the 5 kW proton-exchange membrane (PEM) fuel cell that serves as the tertiary backup power source in the triple-redundant electrical architecture, closing a material cycle in which water is electrolyzed to produce oxygen for breathing and hydrogen for energy storage, the hydrogen is consumed in the fuel cell to produce electrical power and water, and the water is returned to the electrolysis feed stream, creating a closed-loop water-oxygen-hydrogen-energy cycle that approaches thermodynamic reversibility at the system level and eliminates the need for consumable oxygen or hydrogen resupply for the entire 90-day mission duration, with the sole external input being the electrical power from the ocean-current turbine, which the Monterey site provides at a continuous 2.5 kW from its 1.2-meter shrouded rotor operating in the 1.3 to 1.7 m/s diurnal current regime documented in the site survey.

The solid-oxide electrolysis cell is fundamentally distinguished from the more widely deployed proton-exchange membrane (PEM) electrolyzer by its operating temperature regime and the ionic charge carrier through the electrolyte membrane, a distinction that carries profound implications for the thermodynamic efficiency, the materials selection, the degradation mechanisms, and the system integration complexity of the Atlantis oxygen generation subsystem. A PEM electrolyzer operates at approximately 80°C with a perfluorosulfonic acid membrane — typically Nafion, a copolymer of tetrafluoroethylene and sulfonated perfluorovinyl ether, with a sulfonic acid group density expressed as an equivalent weight of approximately 1,100 grams per mole of sulfonic acid groups — as the proton-conducting electrolyte, requiring a minimum cell voltage of 1.23 V at 25°C to overcome the thermodynamic decomposition voltage of water, with practical operating voltages of 1.8 to 2.2 V per cell at current densities of 1 to 2 A/cm², yielding a stack-level efficiency of approximately 65 to 75 percent based on the higher heating value of hydrogen, with the balance of the input electrical energy dissipated as low-grade heat at 80°C that must be rejected to the surrounding seawater through a liquid-to-liquid heat exchanger, a thermal management challenge that is manageable but not negligible in a 10°C Monterey Canyon ambient, where the Carnot-limited heat rejection capacity of a compact plate-frame heat exchanger is approximately 200 to 300 watts per square meter of heat transfer area, requiring roughly 3 to 5 square meters of heat exchanger surface to reject the 1.2 kW of waste heat from a PEM electrolyzer sized for the Atlantis oxygen demand, with the additional complication that the Nafion membrane must be maintained in a fully hydrated state — proton conductivity in Nafion is linearly proportional to water content, dropping from approximately 0.10 S/cm at 100 percent relative humidity to approximately 0.01 S/cm at 50 percent relative humidity — necessitating a water management subsystem that humidifies the anode feed stream, recovers water from the cathode product hydrogen, and maintains a delicate water balance across the membrane to prevent both membrane dry-out (which increases ohmic resistance and reduces efficiency) and cathode flooding (which blocks gas diffusion and increases mass transport overpotential), a water management challenge that accounts for approximately 15 to 20 percent of the total system volume and complexity in terrestrial PEM electrolyzer installations and that would be exacerbated in the Atlantis pod's compact, vibration-isolated, underwater environment where maintenance access is limited to the 90-day resupply interval.

The SOEC, by contrast, operates at 850°C with a ceramic electrolyte — yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ), specifically 8 mol% Y₂O₃ doped into ZrO₂, which creates oxygen vacancies in the fluorite crystal structure through the substitution of trivalent Y³⁺ ions for tetravalent Zr⁴⁺ ions, with each pair of yttrium ions creating one oxygen vacancy to maintain charge neutrality, and these vacancies providing the migration pathway for O²⁻ ions through the electrolyte lattice under an applied electric field, with an ionic conductivity of approximately 0.10 S/cm at 1,000°C and approximately 0.02 S/cm at 800°C, values that are sufficient to support current densities of 0.5 to 1.0 A/cm² at cell voltages of 1.2 to 1.4 V, corresponding to stack-level electrical efficiencies of 85 to 95 percent based on the higher heating value — a dramatic improvement over the PEM efficiency that derives directly from the thermodynamics of water splitting: the Gibbs free energy of the reaction H₂O → H₂ + ½O₂ decreases from 237.2 kJ/mol at 25°C to 177.2 kJ/mol at 850°C, while the enthalpy of the reaction remains nearly constant at approximately 248 kJ/mol (HHV), meaning that at 850°C, a larger fraction of the total energy required for water splitting can be supplied as thermal energy rather than electrical energy, and since thermal energy at 850°C can be provided by resistive heating within the stack itself at nearly 100 percent efficiency — the electrical resistance of the YSZ electrolyte, the electrode polarization resistances, and the interconnect contact resistances all generate Joule heat that contributes directly to maintaining the stack temperature — the effective electrical-to-hydrogen conversion efficiency can exceed 100 percent when measured against the higher heating value of the produced hydrogen, a seeming violation of the First Law of Thermodynamics that is resolved by recognizing that the reaction enthalpy is being supplied partially by the electrical work input and partially by the thermal energy input at high temperature, and that the reversible cell voltage at 850°C is only 0.92 V compared to 1.23 V at 25°C, meaning that every volt above 0.92 V applied to the cell drives the reaction with excess electrical work that is converted to heat within the stack, maintaining the very temperature that reduces the electrical work requirement in the first place, a positive feedback cycle that makes the SOEC the most electrically efficient water-splitting technology available at any scale, with system-level electrical efficiencies routinely demonstrated at 90 to 95 percent in laboratory stacks and 80 to 88 percent in commercial-scale demonstration plants operated by Sunfire GmbH, Ceres Power, and Bloom Energy.

The YSZ electrolyte at the heart of the SOEC is a ceramic material whose mechanical and electrochemical properties have been optimized over four decades of solid-oxide fuel cell (SOFC) research, a technological heritage that the Atlantis SOEC directly leverages because the SOEC is essentially an SOFC operated in reverse — the same materials, the same sealing technologies, the same interconnect coatings, and the same thermal management strategies apply to both operating modes, and the Atlantis system is specifically designed as a reversible SOFC/SOEC dual-mode unit, capable of operating in electrolysis mode to produce oxygen and hydrogen during normal conditions when the ocean-current turbine provides excess electrical power, and switching to fuel cell mode to produce electrical power from stored hydrogen during periods when the turbine output is insufficient, such as during the slack-water periods between tidal cycles when the Monterey Canyon current velocity drops below the turbine's 0.8 m/s cut-in speed, a dual-mode capability that eliminates the need for a separate fuel cell stack and reduces the total system volume by approximately 35 percent compared to a configuration with separate electrolyzer and fuel cell units. The YSZ electrolyte is fabricated as a dense, gas-tight ceramic layer approximately 10 to 15 micrometers thick, supported on a thicker — approximately 300 to 500 micrometers — porous Ni-YSZ cermet cathode substrate that provides mechanical strength while permitting gas diffusion to the triple-phase boundary (TPB) where the Ni catalyst (electronic conductor), the YSZ ceramic (ionic conductor), and the gas phase (H₂O/H₂ mixture) intersect, and it is at this triple-phase boundary that the electrochemical water-splitting reaction occurs: H₂O + 2e⁻ → H₂ + O²⁻, with the oxygen ions being transported through the YSZ electrolyte to the anode, where they are oxidized back to molecular oxygen at the LSCF (La₀.₆Sr₀.₄Co₀.₂Fe₀.₈O₃₋δ) anode's own triple-phase boundary according to the reaction 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻, with the electrons completing the external circuit through the interconnect plates — typically fabricated from ferritic stainless steel alloys such as Crofer 22 APU or ZMG 232, which are specifically formulated with approximately 22 percent chromium to provide oxidation resistance at 850°C and with rare-earth additions of lanthanum and manganese to form a conductive MnCr₂O₄ spinel oxide scale that prevents chromium volatilization, a critical consideration because gaseous chromium species, primarily CrO₃ and CrO₂(OH)₂, evaporate from the interconnect surface at SOEC operating temperatures and deposit on the cathode's electrochemically active sites, where they poison the Ni catalyst by blocking the triple-phase boundary and increasing the cathode polarization resistance by a factor of 2 to 5 over 1,000 hours of operation, a degradation mechanism that the Atlantis SOEC stack mitigates through the application of a protective MCO (MnCo₂O₄) spinel coating applied to the interconnect ribs via atmospheric plasma spray at a thickness of 15 to 20 micrometers, which has been demonstrated to reduce chromium volatilization by approximately 85 percent and to extend the stack's degradation rate from approximately 2.0 percent per 1,000 hours (uncoated interconnects) to approximately 0.5 percent per 1,000 hours (MCO-coated), translating to a projected stack lifetime of approximately 40,000 hours before the voltage degradation reaches 20 percent of the initial value, at which point the stack efficiency would fall below the 80 percent threshold that triggers a scheduled replacement during the 90-day resupply interval, a replacement interval that is comfortably within the 40,000-hour stack life, with the Atlantis pod carrying one spare SOEC stack as a cold standby that can be swapped into operation within approximately 4 hours by a trained crew member using the pod's integrated maintenance manipulator arm, a remotely operated tool that extends from the equipment bay and is controlled from the pod's central monitoring station.

The Ni-YSZ cathode, which serves as both the structural support for the electrolyte and the electrochemical catalyst for the water-splitting reaction, is fabricated through a tape-casting and co-sintering process in which a slurry of NiO powder (approximately 50 volume percent) and YSZ powder (approximately 50 volume percent), mixed with organic binders, plasticizers, and pore-forming agents such as graphite or starch at approximately 10 to 20 volume percent to create the interconnected porosity required for gas transport, is cast into a green tape approximately 500 micrometers thick, dried, and then laminated with a thinner YSZ electrolyte tape cast from a slurry of YSZ powder with the same organic additives but without the NiO or pore-former components, the bilayer laminate being co-sintered at approximately 1,400°C for 4 hours in an air atmosphere to densify the YSZ electrolyte layer to greater than 95 percent of theoretical density — the density necessary to prevent gas crossover between the cathode and anode compartments that would reduce the Faraday efficiency by allowing the product hydrogen and oxygen to recombine chemically rather than through the external electrical circuit — while the NiO-YSZ cathode support layer sinters to approximately 35 to 40 percent porosity, with the NiO component being subsequently reduced to metallic Ni in situ during the initial stack heat-up by flowing a H₂/N₂ gas mixture through the cathode compartment at 800°C, a reduction process that converts the NiO particles to metallic Ni with an accompanying volume contraction of approximately 40 percent that creates additional microporosity within the Ni particles themselves, increasing the total triple-phase boundary length by approximately 25 percent compared to a pre-reduced Ni electrode and significantly enhancing the cathode's electrochemical activity, as the water-splitting reaction rate at the cathode is directly proportional to the triple-phase boundary length, with measured area-specific resistances of 0.15 to 0.25 Ω·cm² at 850°C and 50 percent H₂O/50 percent H₂ feed gas composition, values that are dominated by the cathode charge-transfer resistance at low current densities and by the gas diffusion resistance through the porous electrode at high current densities, with the transition between these regimes occurring at approximately 0.5 A/cm² for a cathode thickness of 400 micrometers and a porosity of 35 percent, per the dusty-gas model of gas diffusion in porous electrodes that accounts for both Knudsen diffusion — the dominant transport mechanism when the pore diameter is smaller than the mean free path of the gas molecules, which at 850°C and atmospheric pressure is approximately 350 nanometers for water vapor, comparable to the 200 to 500 nanometer pore sizes in the Ni-YSZ cathode — and molecular diffusion, which dominates in the larger pores and at higher pressures, the interplay between these two mechanisms determining the concentration overpotential that limits the maximum practical current density in the SOEC to approximately 1.0 to 1.5 A/cm² for the electrode thicknesses and porosities employed in the Atlantis stack.

The LSCF anode on the oxygen-evolving side of the SOEC represents a more challenging materials problem than the cathode because the anode operates in a pure oxygen environment at 850°C, conditions under which most electrically conductive materials oxidize rapidly and lose their electronic conductivity, and because the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) — 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻ — has inherently slower kinetics than the hydrogen evolution reaction at the cathode, with an exchange current density on LSCF of approximately 10⁻⁷ A/cm² compared to approximately 10⁻⁵ A/cm² for the H₂O reduction reaction on Ni-YSZ, meaning that a larger anode overpotential is required to achieve the same current density, and the anode's area-specific resistance typically accounts for 40 to 50 percent of the total cell resistance despite the anode being only 30 to 40 micrometers thick — an order of magnitude thinner than the cathode because the anode does not serve a structural support function and because the oxygen diffusion through the LSCF lattice is sufficiently rapid, with an oxygen self-diffusion coefficient of approximately 10⁻⁹ cm²/s at 850°C, to supply the electrochemical reaction without excessive concentration polarization in a thin electrode, whereas a thicker anode would increase the ohmic resistance without improving the reaction kinetics because the reaction zone — the region within approximately 10 micrometers of the electrolyte interface where the oxygen chemical potential gradient drives the oxygen ion flux through the mixed ionic-electronic conducting (MIEC) LSCF lattice — is limited to this near-interface region by the ambipolar diffusion length, the characteristic distance over which the coupled fluxes of oxygen ions and electrons can sustain the reaction before the oxygen chemical potential equilibrates with the gas phase, a length scale that is determined by the ratio of the ambipolar conductivity to the surface exchange coefficient and that for LSCF at 850°C is approximately 5 to 15 micrometers, depending on the specific Sr content — higher Sr doping increases the oxygen vacancy concentration and the ionic conductivity but also increases the tendency for Sr segregation to the LSCF surface, where Sr reacts with CO₂ in the ambient atmosphere or with CrO₃ vapor from the interconnect to form insulating SrCO₃ or SrCrO₄ surface layers that block the oxygen exchange reaction and increase the anode polarization resistance by a factor of 3 to 10 over 1,000 to 5,000 hours, a degradation mechanism that is accelerated under the high anodic polarization of SOEC operation compared to SOFC operation — in SOFC mode, the anode operates at a reducing potential that suppresses Sr oxidation, whereas in SOEC mode, the anode operates at an oxidizing potential that promotes Sr segregation and subsequent reaction with contaminants — making Sr segregation the dominant long-term degradation mechanism in SOEC anodes, a finding that has been confirmed through post-mortem transmission electron microscopy and atom-probe tomography of SOEC stacks operated for 5,000 to 20,000 hours at Forschungszentrum Jülich and the National Energy Technology Laboratory, which revealed Sr-enriched surface layers approximately 5 to 20 nanometers thick with Sr concentrations 3 to 5 times the bulk composition, beneath which a Sr-depleted zone approximately 50 to 100 nanometers thick exhibited reduced oxygen vacancy concentration and correspondingly reduced ionic conductivity, effectively increasing the anode polarization resistance by creating a series resistance at the electrode surface that impedes the oxygen evolution reaction. The Atlantis SOEC addresses the Sr segregation problem through a combination of materials optimization — the LSCF composition is adjusted to La₀.₆Sr₀.₄Co₀.₂Fe₀.₈O₃₋δ, the composition that Forschungszentrum Jülich's long-term testing identified as the optimal balance between high ionic conductivity (from the Sr doping creating oxygen vacancies) and acceptable Sr segregation kinetics (from the La-rich A-site composition reducing the thermodynamic driving force for Sr surface enrichment) — and through an innovative electrochemical rejuvenation procedure in which the stack is periodically operated in SOFC mode at 900°C for 2 hours with a hydrogen fuel stream, a procedure that reduces the anode surface (reversing the Sr oxidation reaction and converting SrO or SrCO₃ surface phases back into the perovskite lattice) and that has been demonstrated to recover approximately 85 percent of the voltage degradation accumulated during the preceding SOEC operating period, effectively extending the anode lifetime from approximately 10,000 hours (without rejuvenation) to approximately 40,000 hours (with rejuvenation every 500 hours), matching the interconnect degradation-limited stack lifetime and enabling a uniform 40,000-hour stack replacement interval that aligns with the Atlantis pod's 90-day resupply cycle — at 24 hours per day and 90 days per cycle, the SOEC operates for 2,160 hours per resupply interval, meaning that the 40,000-hour stack life covers approximately 18.5 resupply intervals or approximately 4.6 years of continuous operation, a lifetime that the ZIRCONIA cost analysis has determined represents the optimal economic balance between the capital cost of stack replacement ($4,200 per stack) and the operational cost of unscheduled maintenance interventions at the seabed, which would require an ROV deployment costing approximately $12,000 per intervention and presenting a non-trivial risk of pod flooding during the maintenance operation.

The Faraday efficiency of the SOEC — the ratio of the actual oxygen production rate to the theoretical production rate calculated from the electrolysis current via Faraday's law, where each mole of O₂ produced requires four moles of electrons (4 Faradays, or 385,940 coulombs) per the stoichiometry of the oxygen evolution reaction — is a critical performance parameter for the Atlantis life support system because any loss of Faraday efficiency represents not merely an energy penalty but a direct reduction in the oxygen production rate relative to the electrical power input, with the potential to create an oxygen deficit that would progressively lower the pod's internal O₂ partial pressure below the 21 kPa (0.21 atm) normobaric setpoint, triggering increasingly severe physiological responses in the crew — at 17 kPa O₂ partial pressure, equivalent to an altitude of approximately 1,500 meters, most individuals experience no symptoms; at 14 kPa (approximately 3,000 meters equivalent altitude), night vision impairment and mild cognitive effects begin; at 12 kPa (approximately 4,000 meters), judgment impairment and headaches occur; and at 10 kPa, loss of consciousness is imminent — a cascade that the Atlantis atmospheric management system (Part 071) prevents by maintaining a continuous O₂ partial pressure monitoring and control loop, but that would nevertheless impose an operational stress on the system if the SOEC Faraday efficiency were to degrade below the 95 percent specification. The dominant Faraday efficiency loss mechanisms in the SOEC are, in order of significance: electronic leakage through the YSZ electrolyte, in which a small fraction — typically 1 to 3 percent — of the electrical current is carried by electrons rather than oxide ions, these electrons being generated by the partial reduction of Zr⁴⁺ to Zr³⁺ at the cathode-side electrolyte surface under the strongly reducing conditions of the H₂/H₂O gas mixture, with the resulting electronic conductivity creating an internal short-circuit that reduces the external current measured by the power supply but does not produce oxygen at the anode; gas crossover through microcracks or pinholes in the electrolyte, a mechanical integrity issue that is controlled through the tape-casting and co-sintering fabrication process described above, with quality control based on helium leak testing of every cell before stack assembly, rejecting any cell with a helium leak rate exceeding 10⁻⁵ mbar·L/s, a threshold that corresponds to an equivalent oxygen crossover rate of approximately 0.02 percent of the total oxygen production and is therefore negligible for Faraday efficiency purposes; and seal leakage at the interface between the ceramic cell and the metallic interconnect frame, where a glass-ceramic seal — typically a BaO-CaO-Al₂O₃-SiO₂ (BCAS) glass that crystallizes to a barium aluminosilicate glass-ceramic during the initial stack heat-up, forming a rigid, gas-tight bond between the YSZ electrolyte and the Crofer 22 APU interconnect — can develop microcracks during thermal cycling if the coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) mismatch between the seal (approximately 10.0 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹) and the YSZ electrolyte (approximately 10.5 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹) generates tensile stresses exceeding the glass-ceramic's fracture strength of approximately 50 to 80 MPa, with thermal cycling being minimized in the Atlantis application because the SOEC operates continuously at 850°C throughout the 90-day mission, experiencing only a single thermal cycle per resupply interval for the scheduled stack inspection and rejuvenation procedure, a total of approximately 4 thermal cycles per year that the BCAS seal material has been demonstrated to survive for over 100 cycles without measurable leakage increase in the SOFC durability testing programs at Pacific Northwest National Laboratory.

The oxygen production rate required by the Atlantis pod is derived from the crew metabolic oxygen consumption, which for a standard crew of four adults engaged in moderate activity — the Atlantis pod's interior volume of approximately 65 cubic meters (teardrop geometry with a 7-meter major axis and 2.3-meter minor axis) and the assigned tasks of equipment monitoring, maintenance, food preparation, and hydroponic cultivation correspond to a metabolic rate of approximately 1.2 to 1.5 times basal, or approximately 100 to 125 watts per person — is approximately 0.83 kg O₂ per person per day, based on the respiratory quotient of 0.85 for a mixed diet and the stoichiometric relationship that 1 liter of O₂ at standard temperature and pressure (STP, 0°C, 1 atm) supports the metabolism of approximately 4.83 kcal of food energy, with the typical 2,500 kcal/day diet requiring approximately 518 liters of O₂ per day, which at an O₂ density of 1.429 g/L at STP translates to 0.74 kg/day, plus a 12 percent safety margin to account for variations in metabolic rate, for an emergency exercise regimen (the Atlantis crew protocol includes a mandatory 30-minute daily exercise session on a compact cycle ergometer to maintain cardiovascular fitness and bone density in the reduced-activity underwater environment), and for the oxygen demand of the catalytic oxidizer in the trace contaminant control system (Part 071), which consumes approximately 0.05 kg O₂/day in oxidizing CO and CH₄ to CO₂ and H₂O, yielding a total oxygen demand of 3.3 kg/day for the four-person crew, a demand that defines the SOEC sizing requirement. At a Faraday efficiency of 95 percent and a cell voltage of 1.3 V (corresponding to a current density of approximately 0.5 A/cm² for the YSZ electrolyte thickness of 10 micrometers and the electrode area-specific resistances described above), the electrical power required to produce 3.3 kg O₂/day is calculated as follows: 3.3 kg O₂ corresponds to 103.1 moles of O₂ (molecular weight 32.0 g/mol), requiring 412.5 moles of electrons (4 Faradays per mole O₂), or 39.8 million coulombs per day, equivalent to an average electrolysis current of 461 amperes continuously, and at 1.3 V per cell, this corresponds to 599 watts of electrical power per cell, which with 30 cells connected in series to achieve a stack voltage of 39 V and a total stack power of 18.0 kW — noting that the stack produces both oxygen and hydrogen simultaneously, with the 0.41 kg/day of hydrogen (206.2 moles, molecular weight 2.016 g/mol) representing 49.5 percent of the electrolysis energy input, meaning that of the 18.0 kW of stack power, approximately 8.9 kW is attributable to hydrogen production and could be recovered as electrical power through the PEM fuel cell at approximately 50 percent efficiency, yielding 4.45 kW of recovered power for an effective net power consumption of 13.55 kW, although in practice the Atlantis energy management system (Part 070) prioritizes hydrogen storage for the fuel cell backup rather than immediate reconversion, recognizing that the ocean-current turbine's 2.5 kW continuous output is sufficient to cover the net electrolysis power after accounting for the turbine's capacity factor and the battery's buffering capability.

The hydrogen co-produced by the SOEC at a rate of 0.41 kg/day is compressed to 350 bar and stored in a Type IV composite overwrapped pressure vessel with a carbon fiber/epoxy structural shell wound over a high-density polyethylene (HDPE) liner, a storage technology that achieves a gravimetric hydrogen storage density of approximately 5.7 weight percent — 0.41 kg of hydrogen requires a storage volume of approximately 7.2 liters at 350 bar and 15°C (the pod's internal ambient temperature), corresponding to a cylindrical pressure vessel approximately 300 mm in diameter and 400 mm in length, weighing approximately 15 kg including the composite overwrap, the HDPE liner, the boss and valve assembly, and the mounting bracket, a volume and mass that are readily accommodated within the Atlantis pod's equipment bay adjacent to the SOEC stack, with the connecting hydrogen line being a 6 mm outer diameter, 1 mm wall thickness 316L stainless steel tube with Swagelok VCR metal gasket face seal fittings rated for 700 bar, providing a burst pressure safety factor of 2.0 relative to the 350 bar maximum operating pressure, and incorporating a normally-closed solenoid valve that isolates the hydrogen storage vessel from the SOEC stack during standby conditions, with the solenoid being powered open only during active electrolysis, such that any loss of electrical power — including the very loss of power that would trigger the transition to fuel cell backup — causes the hydrogen isolation valve to close automatically, preventing the uncontrolled release of hydrogen into the pod's interior atmosphere, where even a 4 percent hydrogen concentration (the lower flammability limit in air) would constitute a catastrophic fire hazard in the oxygen-enriched environment of a sealed underwater habitation module.

The balance of plant (BOP) for the Atlantis SOEC subsystem encompasses the ancillary components required to deliver feedwater to the stack at the specified temperature, pressure, flow rate, and purity; to remove the product oxygen and hydrogen streams and condition them for their respective destinations (oxygen to the atmospheric management system for crew breathing, hydrogen to the storage vessel for fuel cell backup); to recuperate the high-temperature thermal energy from the product gas streams and return it to the feedwater; to maintain the stack temperature at 850°C ± 10°C through a combination of internal Joule heating and external furnace elements; and to interface with the pod's thermal management system (Part 072) for waste heat rejection during steady-state operation and for heat input during startup from cold. The feedwater supply for the SOEC is deionized water with a resistivity exceeding 18 MΩ·cm — the standard for semiconductor-grade ultrapure water, achieved by passing the forward osmosis permeate (Part 066) through a mixed-bed ion exchange resin column containing strong-acid cation exchange resin (sulfonated styrene-divinylbenzene copolymer in the H⁺ form) and strong-base anion exchange resin (quaternary ammonium-functionalized styrene-divinylbenzene copolymer in the OH⁻ form) at a flow rate of approximately 0.5 L/min, a flow rate that is maintained by a positive-displacement diaphragm pump with PTFE wetted parts for chemical compatibility with the ultrapure water, which is aggressive to most metals and elastomers due to its extremely low ionic strength — ultrapure water has a conductivity of 0.055 μS/cm, corresponding to a resistivity of 18.2 MΩ·cm at 25°C, and its solvent power for metal ions and organic contaminants is substantially higher than that of ordinary tap water, a property that makes ultrapure water corrosive to 316L stainless steel at flow velocities exceeding approximately 1.5 m/s, which is why all wetted components in the Atlantis feedwater system downstream of the deionization column are fabricated from PTFE, PFA (perfluoroalkoxy alkane), or electropolished 316L stainless steel with a surface roughness Ra of less than 0.4 micrometers to minimize the surface area available for metal ion leaching, which would otherwise contaminate the SOEC cathode and degrade its electrochemical performance through the deposition of silica, calcium, or sodium species at the triple-phase boundary, a well-documented degradation mode in SOEC stacks operated on impure water feeds.

ocean-current turbine output at the 2.5 kW rated power.

The steam generator that converts the preheated feedwater to 850°C steam at the cathode inlet is a critical BOP component whose reliability directly determines the SOEC's ability to produce oxygen, because any interruption in the steam supply — even for a few seconds — exposes the Ni-YSZ cathode to an oxidizing atmosphere, causing the metallic Ni catalyst to oxidize to NiO with an accompanying volume expansion of approximately 40 percent that generates mechanical stresses exceeding the YSZ electrolyte's fracture strength of approximately 200 to 300 MPa, leading to electrolyte cracking, gas crossover, and irreversible stack failure, a failure mode that is well-documented in the SOEC literature and that the Atlantis system prevents through a triple-redundant steam generation architecture: primary steam generation via electrical heating of the PCHE gas channels using silicon carbide heating elements embedded in the PCHE stack and controlled by a proportional-integral-derivative (PID) temperature controller with a Type K thermocouple feedback sensor at the steam outlet, achieving a temperature stability of ±2°C at the 850°C setpoint; secondary steam generation via a backup resistive heating element wound around the steam delivery tube downstream of the PCHE, powered by a dedicated circuit from the Grid-Seed battery and capable of maintaining the steam temperature at 850°C for up to 2 hours following a failure of the primary heating elements, sufficient time for the crew to diagnose and repair the primary system or to initiate a controlled SOEC shutdown and switch to the backup oxygen supply — a bank of four compressed oxygen cylinders at 200 bar containing 12 kg of O₂, sufficient for approximately 3.6 days of crew respiration at the 3.3 kg/day rate; and tertiary steam generation via direct steam injection from the pod's auxiliary boiler, a compact once-through steam generator originally designed for the pod's hygiene water heating but plumbed with a crossover valve that allows its steam output to be directed to the SOEC cathode inlet in the event of a total electrical failure, providing a final line of defense against cathode oxidation.

The power conditioning system for the SOEC stack converts the pod's 48 V DC bus voltage — the standard voltage of the Grid-Seed battery and the triple-redundant power distribution architecture described in Part 070 — to the 39 V DC stack voltage at the 461 A electrolysis current, a conversion that is accomplished by a synchronous buck converter operating at a switching frequency of 100 kHz with silicon carbide (SiC) MOSFETs rated for 1,200 V and 100 A, the SiC devices being selected for their superior high-temperature performance compared to silicon IGBTs — SiC MOSFETs maintain a low on-resistance of approximately 25 mΩ at a junction temperature of 150°C, compared to approximately 75 mΩ for a comparable silicon IGBT with its associated forward voltage drop of approximately 1.5 V that would contribute an additional 690 W of conduction loss at the 461 A SOEC current, a loss that would reduce the power conditioning efficiency from the 97.5 percent achieved with SiC MOSFETs to approximately 94 percent with silicon IGBTs, wasting an additional 630 W of electrical power as heat that would need to be rejected to the pod's thermal management system, increasing the cooling load by approximately 30 percent and potentially exceeding the heat rejection capacity of the pod's seawater heat exchanger during the warm-water Osa site conditions — a subtle but consequential design decision that the Atlantis team validated through a detailed semiconductor loss model using the manufacturer's SPICE simulation parameters for the Cree/Wolfspeed C3M0016120K SiC MOSFET and the Infineon FF100R12YT3 IGBT module, confirming that the SiC solution's higher upfront cost of approximately $180 per MOSFET (compared to approximately $45 per IGBT) is recovered within the first 15 days of operation through the reduced cooling system energy consumption, a calculation that the ZIRCONIA economic analysis verified and that resulted in the SiC-based power conditioning being selected for all Atlantis SOEC installations.

The synergy between the SOEC oxygen generation system and the forward osmosis desalination system described in Part 066 extends beyond the simple provision of feedwater to encompass the thermal integration of the two subsystems in a manner that significantly improves the overall energy efficiency of the Atlantis life support architecture. The forward osmosis process requires the regeneration of the draw solution — typically a concentrated NaCl solution with an osmotic pressure of approximately 60 bar at 1.5 M concentration — through a thermal separation process: heating the diluted draw solution to evaporate water, which is then condensed as pure permeate, leaving behind a reconcentrated draw solution that is returned to the FO membrane module, a process that at 85 percent thermal efficiency requires approximately 680 kWh of thermal energy per cubic meter of permeate produced, or approximately 2.45 MJ (0.68 kWh) of thermal energy for the 3.7 kg of water required by the SOEC per day, a modest thermal demand that would represent approximately 28 watts of continuous thermal power, which is small enough to be supplied entirely by the waste heat from the SOEC stack itself — the 850°C stack radiates approximately 850 watts of thermal energy to its surroundings through the 50 mm of silica aerogel insulation that maintains the external surface temperature of the SOEC enclosure at approximately 45°C, a heat loss that, rather than being rejected to the seawater through the pod's thermal management system, is partially captured by a copper heat pipe array that transfers approximately 150 watts of thermal energy from the SOEC enclosure to the FO draw-solution regenerator, providing more than five times the thermal power required for the permeate production and enabling the FO-SOEC thermal integration to operate without any additional electrical heating for the draw-solution regeneration, a design feature that the Kiddo register identifies as "elegant thermal cascading" and that the Torvalds register confirms reduces the total Atlantis life support power consumption by approximately 12 percent compared to a non-integrated configuration in which the SOEC waste heat is rejected to the seawater and the FO regenerator is heated electrically, a 12 percent reduction that over the 90-day mission saves approximately 2,590 kWh of electrical energy, equivalent to approximately 52 full discharge cycles of the Grid-Seed battery, a substantial operational margin that could mean the difference between the crew completing the mission and the crew initiating an emergency ascent.

The reversible SOFC/SOEC dual-mode operation of the Atlantis stack introduces additional materials and control challenges that arise from the different chemical environments experienced by the electrodes in the two operating modes: in SOEC mode, the Ni-YSZ cathode is exposed to a H₂O/H₂ mixture that is predominantly steam (typically 70 to 90 percent H₂O at the inlet, decreasing as the steam is consumed along the flow channel), an oxidizing environment relative to the Ni/NiO equilibrium at the operating temperature, and the cathode must be protected from Ni oxidation by maintaining a minimum H₂ concentration of approximately 5 percent throughout the flow field, typically by recycling a portion of the product hydrogen to the cathode inlet, a recycle loop that adds complexity and parasitic power consumption — the recycle blower consumes approximately 50 watts, or approximately 0.3 percent of the stack power — but that is essential for preventing cathode oxidation during startup, shutdown, and transient conditions when the steam flow rate or temperature deviates from the design point; in SOFC mode, the same Ni-YSZ electrode becomes the anode (fuel electrode) and is exposed to pure hydrogen fuel, a strongly reducing environment that poses no oxidation risk but that introduces the risk of carbon deposition if the hydrogen fuel contains CO or hydrocarbon impurities — carbon deposition (coking) on the Ni catalyst blocks the active sites and can lead to the growth of carbon filaments that exert sufficient mechanical force to fracture the YSZ electrolyte, a well-known failure mode in SOFCs operated on reformed hydrocarbon fuels that is avoided in the Atlantis application by using pure hydrogen from the SOEC product stream, eliminating the carbon source entirely, a design decision that simplifies the fuel processing compared to terrestrial SOFC systems that operate on natural gas and that eliminates the need for the steam reformer, water-gas shift reactor, and preferential oxidation reactor that would otherwise be required to convert natural gas to a hydrogen-rich reformate with CO concentration below approximately 10 ppm, the threshold for stable Ni catalyst operation without coking. The oxygen electrode in the reversible SOFC/SOEC — the LSCF anode in SOEC mode, which becomes the cathode in SOFC mode, where it catalyzes the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR), O₂ + 4e⁻ → 2O²⁻, the reverse of the oxygen evolution reaction — presents a unique challenge because the surface chemistry that optimizes the OER kinetics (high oxygen vacancy concentration, low surface Sr segregation) is different from the surface chemistry that optimizes the ORR kinetics (high electronic conductivity at the surface, high oxygen adsorption and dissociation rates), creating a materials optimization problem for which no single LSCF composition is simultaneously optimal for both reactions; the Atlantis solution is to operate the reversible stack with a bias toward SOEC optimization — the LSCF composition is selected for maximum OER activity, since oxygen production for crew breathing is the mission-critical function, while accepting a modest penalty in the SOFC-mode power density, which is compensated by oversizing the stack by approximately 15 percent relative to the SOFC power requirement, a design margin that increases the stack cost by approximately $630 but that eliminates the need for a separate fuel cell stack, saving approximately $3,800 in total system cost and approximately 0.12 cubic meters of installation volume.

The degradation mechanisms that limit the SOEC stack lifetime to the 40,000-hour specification have been extensively characterized through post-mortem analysis of stacks operated at Forschungszentrum Jülich, the National Energy Technology Laboratory, the Technical University of Denmark (DTU Energy), and the industrial SOEC developers Sunfire and Ceres Power, with the degradation rates for the various mechanisms being quantified through electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements conducted at regular intervals during long-term testing, deconvolving the total area-specific resistance increase into its constituent components — ohmic resistance (attributable to the electrolyte, the electrode bulk, the interconnect scale growth, and the contact resistances at the various interfaces), polarization resistance at the cathode (charge transfer and gas diffusion), and polarization resistance at the anode (charge transfer and surface exchange) — and tracking the evolution of each component over time to identify the dominant degradation mechanism at each stage of the stack's life. Chromium poisoning of the Ni-YSZ cathode is the dominant degradation mechanism during the first 5,000 to 10,000 hours of operation, driven by the volatilization of CrO₃ and CrO₂(OH)₂ from the interconnect steel under the oxidizing conditions on the air/O₂ side of the cell, with the gaseous chromium species being transported through the porous LSCF anode and the cracks or pinholes in the YSZ electrolyte — or, more commonly, through the seal interfaces between the cell and the interconnect frame, which provide a bypass path for chromium vapor to reach the cathode compartment without passing through the electrolyte — and depositing at the cathode's electrochemically active triple-phase boundaries, where they react with Ni to form a NiCr₂O₄ spinel phase that blocks the charge-transfer reaction and increases the cathode polarization resistance at a rate of approximately 2 to 5 mΩ·cm² per 1,000 hours, depending on the chromium vapor pressure (which increases exponentially with temperature above approximately 750°C) and the effectiveness of the MCO protective coating on the interconnect; the rate of chromium poisoning can be reduced by approximately 85 percent through the MCO coating described above, by an additional approximately 10 percent through the use of a cathode contact layer — a porous LSM (La₀.₈Sr₀.₂MnO₃) or LSCF layer screen-printed onto the interconnect ribs to provide a sacrificial chromium getter that reacts with the chromium vapor before it reaches the cathode — and by a final approximately 5 percent through careful control of the SOEC operating temperature at the lower end of the acceptable range, specifically 800°C to 825°C rather than 850°C, reducing the chromium vapor pressure by approximately 50 percent at the cost of a approximately 15 percent increase in the area-specific resistance (due to the temperature dependence of the YSZ ionic conductivity and the electrode reaction kinetics), a trade-off that the Atlantis system evaluates dynamically through a real-time degradation model that balances the stack efficiency loss at lower temperature against the lifetime extension from reduced chromium poisoning, selecting the operating temperature that minimizes the total cost of ownership — the sum of the electrical energy cost over the stack lifetime plus the stack replacement cost — which for the Atlantis mission profile, with its 90-day resupply intervals and $4,200 stack replacement cost, yields an optimal operating temperature of approximately 830°C.

Nickel coarsening in the Ni-YSZ cathode is the second major degradation mechanism, operating on a longer timescale of 10,000 to 40,000 hours and driven by the minimization of the Ni/YSZ interfacial energy — the Ni particles, which are initially approximately 0.5 to 1.0 micrometers in diameter after the in-situ NiO reduction process, gradually coarsen through a combination of Ostwald ripening (the dissolution of smaller Ni particles and the growth of larger Ni particles through nickel atom diffusion along the Ni surface or through the gas phase as Ni(OH)₂, which has a vapor pressure of approximately 10⁻⁸ atm at 850°C and 50 percent H₂O, sufficient to transport nickel atoms over distances of several micrometers, the characteristic spacing between Ni particles in the cermet cathode) and particle coalescence (the merging of adjacent Ni particles that come into contact as the YSZ matrix undergoes slow sintering at the operating temperature, a process that is accelerated by the presence of water vapor, which catalyzes the surface diffusion of YSZ cations and promotes the necking and eventual coalescence of adjacent YSZ grains, reducing the surface area available for Ni particle anchoring and allowing the Ni particles to migrate and coalesce), with the overall effect being a reduction in the triple-phase boundary length by approximately 20 to 40 percent over 40,000 hours, corresponding to an increase in the cathode polarization resistance of approximately 30 to 60 mΩ·cm², which at the Atlantis stack's design current density of 0.5 A/cm² translates to an additional voltage loss of 15 to 30 mV, or approximately 1.2 to 2.3 percent of the cell voltage, a contribution that is within the acceptable degradation budget of 20 percent over 40,000 hours and that the Atlantis system accommodates by gradually increasing the stack operating voltage through the power conditioning system's software-controlled voltage setpoint, maintaining the design oxygen production rate despite the increasing cell resistance, a control strategy that is commonly employed in terrestrial SOEC installations and that has been demonstrated to maintain constant production rates for operating periods exceeding 30,000 hours in the Sunfire HYLINK-1000 demonstration plant in Salzgitter, Germany.

Strontium segregation at the LSCF anode, described in detail above, is the third major degradation mechanism and the one that ultimately determines the anode lifetime, with the 2-hour SOFC-mode rejuvenation procedure at 900°C every 500 hours being the primary mitigation strategy, supplemented by the selection of the La₀.₆Sr₀.₄Co₀.₂Fe₀.₈O₃₋δ composition for optimal segregation resistance and by the operation of the anode at the lowest practical overpotential — the anode polarization in SOEC mode is approximately 50 to 100 mV at the design current density of 0.5 A/cm², and operating at higher current densities would accelerate Sr segregation through the increased anodic potential, which drives the electrochemical oxidation of Sr²⁺ to SrO at the LSCF surface, a potential-dependent degradation that follows a Butler-Volmer-type kinetics with an apparent activation energy of approximately 80 kJ/mol, as determined by chronoamperometric measurements at controlled anode potentials at DTU Energy, where LSCF electrodes polarized at 200 mV anodic overpotential degraded approximately 4 times faster than electrodes polarized at 50 mV anodic overpotential, a finding that directly informs the Atlantis SOEC's design current density of 0.5 A/cm² as the value that balances oxygen production rate against anode degradation rate, with the 0.5 A/cm² point being selected as the "knee" in the trade-off curve beyond which further increases in current density yield diminishing returns in oxygen production rate while accelerating anode degradation to the point where the 40,000-hour lifetime cannot be guaranteed, a design optimization that was conducted by the Atlantis SOEC team using a multi-physics degradation model implemented in COMSOL Multiphysics and validated against the DTU experimental data.

The integration of the SOEC oxygen generation subsystem with the broader Atlantis life support architecture is mediated by the pod's central control system, which operates on a triple-modular-redundant (TMR) computing architecture with three independent single-board computers (SBCs) running a real-time operating system (RTEMS, the Real-Time Executive for Multiprocessor Systems, selected for its space-heritage reliability and its DO-178C Level A certification pedigree in aerospace applications) and voting on all control outputs to ensure that no single SBC failure can cause an incorrect command to be issued to the SOEC power conditioning system or to any of the valves, pumps, or heaters in the BOP, a fault-tolerant architecture that is standard throughout the Atlantis pod's safety-critical systems and that the Aegis C safety philosophy requires for any subsystem whose failure could result in loss of crew. The SOEC control software implements a state machine with five defined states — OFF, HEATUP (temperature ramp from ambient to 850°C at 3°C/min, the maximum rate that avoids thermal shock to the YSZ electrolyte, which has a thermal shock resistance parameter R' of approximately 200 W/m, where R' = σ_f·(1-ν)·k/(α·E), with σ_f being the fracture strength (approximately 200 MPa), ν the Poisson's ratio (approximately 0.3), k the thermal conductivity (approximately 2 W/m·K at 850°C), α the coefficient of thermal expansion (approximately 10.5 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹), and E the Young's modulus (approximately 200 GPa), yielding a maximum allowable temperature gradient of approximately 100°C across the 100 mm cell width, which at a ramp rate of 3°C/min is not exceeded, as confirmed by finite-element thermal-stress analysis using ANSYS Mechanical), STANDBY (at 850°C with a low flow of H₂/N₂ purge gas to maintain reducing conditions at the cathode, consuming approximately 150 watts to maintain temperature against the insulation heat loss), ELECTROLYSIS (normal oxygen production mode, with the steam flow rate and the stack current being controlled in closed-loop feedback from the pod's atmospheric O₂ partial pressure sensor, a zirconia-based potentiometric oxygen sensor operating on the Nernst principle: E = (RT/4F)·ln(P_O₂_reference/P_O₂_sample), with a measurement accuracy of ±0.1 kPa O₂ partial pressure and a response time of less than 5 seconds), and REGENERATION (the 2-hour SOFC-mode rejuvenation cycle at 900°C, during which the SOEC is temporarily unavailable for oxygen production and the pod's atmosphere is maintained by the backup O₂ cylinders), with state transitions being initiated automatically by the control software based on sensor inputs (O₂ partial pressure, H₂ storage pressure, stack voltage, and stack temperature) or manually by the crew through the pod's human-machine interface, a ruggedized 15-inch LCD touchscreen with redundant physical buttons for all critical functions, mounted in the pod's central monitoring station and visible from both the primary crew workstation and the secondary workstation in the pod's equipment bay.
