# PART-071 — ATMOSPHERIC MANAGEMENT: CO₂ SCRUBBING, O₂ GENERATION, TRACE CONTAMINANT CONTROL, AND HUMIDITY REGULATION

## CSMFAB077-Deep | Life-Support Subsystem | Aegis C Material Architecture

The atmospheric management subsystem is the most safety-critical of all Atlantis pod life support functions, maintaining the composition, pressure, temperature, and humidity of the pod's internal atmosphere within the narrow physiological tolerance bands that sustain human life — the partial pressure of oxygen (pO₂) between 19.5 kPa and 23.1 kPa, corresponding to a fractional concentration of 19.5 to 23.1 percent at the pod's 101.3 kPa (1 atmosphere) total pressure, with the nominal setpoint being 21.0 kPa, equivalent to the Earth's atmospheric O₂ partial pressure at sea level, a value that the human respiratory system has evolved to expect over approximately 400 million years of vertebrate evolution and that the physiological research community, from Paul Bert's pioneering 1878 work on oxygen toxicity ("La Pression Barométrique") to the NASA Human Research Program's extensive studies on the ISS (where the atmosphere is maintained at 101.3 kPa with 21 percent O₂, identical to the Atlantis specification, a decision that the ISS program made in the 1990s after extensive debate between the advocates of a low-pressure, high-O₂ atmosphere — which would reduce the structural mass of the station's modules by approximately 30 percent, since the pressure differential across the hull would be lower — and the advocates of an Earth-normal atmosphere, which eliminates the risk of decompression sickness during transition between the station and the visiting vehicles, and which simplifies the design of the life support systems by allowing them to use terrestrial standards for O₂ sensors, CO₂ scrubbers, and trace contaminant control equipment, a decision that the Atlantis program revisited in its own trade study, but which was settled quickly in favor of the Earth-normal atmosphere for the same reasons that the ISS program chose it, with the additional consideration that the Atlantis pod, unlike the ISS, is not mass-constrained — the pod's structural mass is dominated by the pressure hull's survival of the 45-meter external pressure and the turbidite impact loads, not by the 101.3 kPa internal pressure, which requires a hull capable of withstanding a 0.55 MPa external pressure (45 meters depth) and which therefore has a substantial margin on the internal pressure requirement — and the 101.3 kPa total pressure eliminates the need for a pre-breathe protocol before the crew exits the pod in an emergency, since there is no dissolved nitrogen in their tissues at a partial pressure different from the Earth's surface, and the crew can ascend to the surface without risk of decompression sickness, provided they ascend at a rate of less than 9 meters per minute, the US Navy's standard ascent rate for free-swimming ascents from depths up to 60 meters, a rate that the pod's ballast blow system is designed to achieve by controlling the rate at which the ballast water is expelled from the tanks, with the ascent rate being measured by a depth sensor — a Paroscientific Digiquartz pressure transducer with an accuracy of 0.01 percent of full scale (100 meters, so ±0.01 m) — and the blow valve being modulated by a PID controller to maintain the 9 m/min ascent rate, a control loop that the crew practices during the pre-deployment training at Mojave, where the pod is submerged in a 15-meter-deep test tank (the maximum depth available at the Mojave facility, sufficient to verify the blow system's functionality but not the full 45-meter ascent profile, which is verified analytically using a 1D dynamic model of the pod's ascent that accounts for minimum, exceeding the 90-day resupply interval, and the carbon bed is replaced at each resupply visit, along with the other TCCS consumables — the ZnO bed, the Pt/Al₂O₃ catalyst, and the HEPA filters The catalytic oxidation bed — 0.5 kg of 0.5 percent Pt/Al₂O₃ catalyst, in the form of 3 mm diameter spherical pellets, housed in a stainless steel reactor tube 50 mm in diameter and 200 mm in length, heated to 300°C by a cylindrical furnace — oxidizes CO (carbon monoxide) and CH₄ (methane) to CO₂ and H₂O, with the CO being produced by the incomplete combustion of trace organics in the SOEC's steam generator (the 850°C steam can crack the organic contaminants in the feedwater — humic acids, fulvic acids, and other dissolved organic matter that the forward osmosis membrane passes at approximately 1-2 percent, corresponding to a TOC (total organic carbon) concentration of approximately 0.5 mg/L in the SOEC feedwater, and the cracking of these organics produces CO, CO₂, CH₄, and H₂, with the CO being the primary concern — CO binds to hemoglobin with an affinity approximately 210 times greater than O₂, and at a CO concentration of 50 ppm, approximately 8 percent of the hemoglobin is bound to CO (carboxyhemoglobin, COHb), reducing the blood's O₂-carrying capacity and causing headaches, fatigue, and impaired judgment, symptoms that are indistinguishable from CO₂ toxicity and that could mask a CO₂ scrubber failure, making CO monitoring and removal a critical TCCS function), and the catalytic oxidizer converts CO to CO₂ according to the reaction 2CO + O₂ → 2CO₂, with a conversion efficiency of greater than 99.9 percent at 300°C and a space velocity (the volumetric flow rate of the gas divided by the catalyst bed volume) of 10,000 h⁻¹, and the oxidizer consumes approximately 0.05 kg O₂/day, as accounted for in the SOEC sizing in Part 067. The CH₄ oxidation reaction — CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O — requires a higher temperature (approximately 300°C for the Pt/Al₂O₃ catalyst, compared to approximately 150°C for CO oxidation on the same catalyst), and the oxidizer's 300°C operating temperature is selected to ensure complete CH₄ oxidation, eliminating the methane that would otherwise accumulate to concentrations of several hundred ppm over the 90-day mission, based on the crew's intestinal CH₄ production of approximately 0.5 L/day per person, or 2 L/day for the crew of four, which at the pod's 65 m³ atmospheric volume and 1 atm pressure corresponds to a concentration increase of 2/(65,000) = 31 ppm per day, reaching 2,790 ppm at the end of a 90-day mission without CH₄ removal — below the 5 percent (50,000 ppm) lower flammability limit for CH₄ in air, but above the odor threshold of approximately 1 ppm, and the crew would certainly notice the accumulation of methane (though methane is odorless, the associated intestinal gases — hydrogen sulfide, indole, skatole, and mercaptans — are notoriously malodorous and would make the pod uninhabitable long before the methane concentration reached a hazardous level, which is why the TCCS also addresses the odorants through the activated carbon bed). The HEPA (High-Efficiency Particulate Air) filtration — a 300 mm × 300 mm × 150 mm filter element with a Minimum Efficiency Reporting Value (MERV) of 17, corresponding to a filtration efficiency of 99.97 percent for 0.3 μm diameter particles (the most penetrating particle size, or MPPS, for HEPA filters, which is the particle size at which the combined mechanisms of impaction, interception, and diffusion are least effective, resulting in the lowest filtration efficiency, and the 99.97 percent efficiency at the MPPS is the defining characteristic of a HEPA filter, per the US Department of Energy standard DOE-STD-3020-2015) — removes the particulate matter generated by the crew (skin flakes, hair, clothing fibers, and the dust that is inevitably tracked into the pod from the Mojave facility, despite the cleanroom protocols that the crew follows during the pod's outfitting and that are designed to minimize the initial particulate load) and by the pod's equipment (the SOEC's ceramic insulation can shed microscopic YSZ particles, which if inhaled could cause lung irritation, and the HEPA filter captures these particles before they enter the pod's ventilation system, protecting the crew's respiratory health).

The gas chromatography (GC) plus electrochemical cells plus photoionization detector (PID) monitoring system provides continuous, quantitative analysis of the pod's atmospheric composition, with the GC analyzing a sample of the pod's atmosphere every 30 minutes — a SRI Instruments 8610C gas chromatograph with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD) for the permanent gases (O₂, N₂, CO₂, and CH₄) and a flame ionization detector (FID) for the VOCs, the GC column being a 2-meter × 3 mm ID stainless steel packed column with a molecular sieve 5A packing for the permanent gas separation and a 30-meter × 0.53 mm ID fused silica capillary column with a 5 percent phenyl / 95 percent dimethylpolysiloxane stationary phase for the VOC separation, the columns being housed in a temperature-programmable oven that ramps from 40°C to 250°C at 10°C/min, with the entire analysis cycle taking approximately 25 minutes, and the GC's detection limits being approximately 100 ppm for the permanent gases (via the TCD, which has a sensitivity of approximately 10⁻⁶ g/mL for most gases, and the 1 mL sample loop provides an on-column mass of approximately 1 μg for a gas at 1,000 ppm concentration, which is easily detected by the TCD — the TCD's noise level is approximately 0.1 μV, and the signal for 1,000 ppm CO₂ is approximately 10 μV, giving a signal-to-noise ratio of 100, corresponding to a detection limit of approximately 10 ppm, consistent with the 100 ppm specification when a factor-of-10 safety margin is included) and approximately 10 ppb for the VOCs (via the FID, which has a sensitivity of approximately 10⁻¹² g/s for hydrocarbons, and with a 0.5 mL sample injection, the FID can detect sub-ppb concentrations — the Atlantis TCCS monitoring specification of 10 ppb for VOCs is comfortably achieved by the FID), with the GC data being logged to the pod's central control system and compared against the SMACs for each contaminant, and any exceedance triggers an alarm on the central monitoring station, alerting the crew to take corrective action — typically, increasing the flow rate through the activated carbon bed, which reduces the bed's contact time but increases the total VOC removal rate, a trade-off that the crew can manage through the TCCS control panel, which provides manual control over the bed's bypass valve and the circulation fan speed.
