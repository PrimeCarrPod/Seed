## CSMFAB077-Deep | Life-Support Subsystem | Aegis C Material Architecture

The fundamental physics governing the ocean-current turbine's energy extraction from the marine current is captured by the Betz limit, the theoretical maximum power coefficient C_p,max = 16/27 ≈ 0.593 that represents the fraction of the kinetic energy flux through the rotor swept area that can be converted to mechanical shaft power by an ideal actuator disk operating in an incompressible, inviscid fluid with no rotational wake — a limit that was first derived by the German physicist Albert Betz in 1919 and that applies equally to wind turbines and water turbines, with the important distinction that the fluid density for seawater (ρ = 1,025 kg/m³ at 10°C and 35 ppt salinity, the conditions at the Monterey deployment depth) is approximately 835 times greater than the fluid density for air at sea level (ρ = 1.225 kg/m³ at 15°C), meaning that a water turbine of a given rotor swept area operating in a 1.5 m/s current extracts the same kinetic power as a wind turbine of the same swept area operating in a wind speed of 1.5 × √835 ≈ 43.3 m/s, approximately 156 km/h or a Category 2 hurricane on the Saffir-Simpson scale, a comparison that illustrates why ocean-current turbines can achieve commercially viable power densities with rotor diameters of only 1 to 3 meters — the Atlantis turbine's 1.2-meter rotor diameter corresponds to a swept area of A = π(D/2)² = π × (0.6)² = 1.13 m², and at a current velocity of 1.5 m/s, the kinetic power flux through this area is P_kinetic = ½ρAv³ = ½ × 1,025 × 1.13 × 1.5³ = ½ × 1,025 × 1.13 × 3.375 = 1,955 watts, of which the Betz-limited maximum extractable power is P_max = 0.593 × 1,955 = 1,159 watts, a value that appears at first glance to fall short of the 2.5 kW rated power, but this apparent discrepancy is resolved by the addition of the shroud (diffuser-augmented turbine, or DAT), which accelerates the flow through the rotor plane by creating a low-pressure region downstream of the turbine through the Venturi effect — the shroud is a ring-shaped hydrofoil with a carefully designed cross-sectional profile that generates lift directed radially inward, and the circulation associated with this lift induces an increase in the axial velocity through the rotor plane by a factor of approximately 1.5 to 2.0 relative to the free-stream velocity, with the augmentation factor being limited by flow separation on the diffuser's inner surface, which occurs when the diffuser expansion angle exceeds approximately 10 to 15 degrees, at which point the adverse pressure gradient overcomes the turbulent boundary layer's ability to remain attached, causing separation that drastically reduces the augmentation factor and increases the drag on the shroud, potentially negating the shroud's benefit entirely — a critical design constraint that the Atlantis turbine's shroud geometry addresses through a NACA 662-415 hydrofoil profile with a chord length of 300 mm, a maximum thickness of 15 percent of chord, and a camber of 2 percent, arranged in a ring of 600 mm inlet diameter, 720 mm outlet diameter, and 250 mm axial length, corresponding to a diffuser expansion angle of approximately 14 degrees (the half-angle of the diffuser cone, calculated as arctan((720-600)/(2×250)) = arctan(120/500) = arctan(0.24) = 13.5°), an angle that computational fluid dynamics analysis using ANSYS Fluent with the k-ω SST (Shear Stress Transport) turbulence model — selected for its ability to accurately predict flow separation in adverse pressure gradients, a capability that the k-ε model, with its assumption of isotropic turbulence and its wall function treatment of the near-wall region, lacks, leading to systematic overprediction of the separation angle and consequent underprediction of the drag on diffuser-augmented ducts — predicts will maintain attached flow up to a free-stream velocity of 2.5 m/s, beyond which the shroud stalls and the augmentation factor drops from approximately 1.7 to approximately 1.1, a reduction that the turbine's control system accommodates by feathering the blades to reduce the angle of attack and prevent rotor overspeed, a protective measure that is triggered by the rotor RPM sensor — a Hall-effect sensor detecting the passage of a magnet embedded in the rotor hub, providing RPM measurement with a resolution of 0.1 RPM and an update rate of 100 Hz — exceeding 450 RPM, the design maximum for the NdFeB permanent magnet generator's mechanical stress limits, which are determined by the centrifugal stress in the rotor laminations at 450 RPM, approximately 15 MPa in the electrical steel at the outer diameter of the 200 mm rotor, well below the 350 MPa yield strength of the M270-35A non-oriented electrical steel specified for the generator laminations.

The blade element momentum (BEM) theory that underpins the rotor blade design is a classical aerodynamic method that combines the one-dimensional momentum theory — which relates the thrust on the rotor disk to the change in axial momentum of the fluid flowing through the disk, as described by the axial induction factor a = (V∞ - V_disk)/V∞, where V∞ is the free-stream velocity and V_disk is the velocity at the rotor plane, with the power coefficient being C_p = 4a(1-a)² and the thrust coefficient being C_T = 4a(1-a), these relationships holding for a ≤ 0.5 (the windmill brake state), beyond which the simple momentum theory breaks down because the wake becomes turbulent and the flow transitions to the turbulent wake state, where empirical corrections such as the Glauert correction for heavily loaded rotors must be applied, modifying the thrust coefficient to C_T = 4a(1-a/4) for a > 0.4, a correction that is relevant for the Atlantis turbine at low current velocities when the tip-speed ratio (TSR, λ = ωR/V∞, where ω is the rotor angular velocity and R is the rotor radius) is high and the axial induction factor approaches the turbulent-wake threshold — with blade element theory, which discretizes each blade into a series of spanwise elements, typically 15 to 25 elements from hub to tip, and for each element calculates the local lift coefficient C_l(α) and drag coefficient C_d(α) from the two-dimensional airfoil characteristics at the local angle of attack α, which is determined by the vector sum of the axial velocity V∞(1-a) and the tangential velocity ωr(1+a'), where a' is the tangential (angular) induction factor that accounts for the rotation imparted to the wake by the rotor, and the local solidity σ(r) = Bc(r)/(2πr), where B is the number of blades and c(r) is the local chord length at radius r, from which the local thrust and torque contributions are integrated along the blade span to obtain the total rotor thrust, torque, and power, with the induction factors a and a' being solved iteratively for each blade element until convergence is achieved at a tolerance of typically 10⁻⁴ on the change in a between successive iterations, a procedure that is implemented in the open-source BEM code QBlade, which the Atlantis turbine design team used to optimize the blade geometry for maximum annual energy production at the Monterey site's current velocity probability distribution, a Weibull distribution with a shape parameter k = 2.0 and a scale parameter c = 1.5 m/s, fitted to the 12-month ADCP dataset using the maximum likelihood estimation method, yielding an optimal TSR of 5.0, a three-bladed rotor (B = 3, selected as the standard compromise between the aerodynamic efficiency gained by adding more blades — which increases the solidity and reduces the tip-loss factor, but also increases the blade-root bending moment and the manufacturing cost — and the structural simplicity of a two-bladed rotor, which is lighter and cheaper but suffers from higher vibratory loads due to the asymmetric rotor loading when the blades pass through the tower shadow or the shroud support strut wakes, and which requires a teetering hub mechanism to accommodate the cyclic flapwise bending moments, adding mechanical complexity that the Atlantis design philosophy, with its emphasis on simplicity and reliability for unattended underwater operation, deliberately avoids in favor of the three-bladed configuration, whose inherent aerodynamic balance eliminates the need for a teetering hub and allows the use of a rigid hub with individual blade pitch bearings for the active pitch control system that provides both power regulation at high current velocities and thrust limiting during extreme events such as the turbidite flows associated with the crustal displacement event), and a blade geometry with a chord distribution that tapers from 120 mm at the root (r/R = 0.2) to 60 mm at the tip (r/R = 1.0), with a twist distribution that varies from 25 degrees at the root to 5 degrees at the tip to maintain the optimal angle of attack of approximately 6 degrees along the entire blade span at the design TSR of 5.0.

The hydrofoil selected for the Atlantis turbine blades is the NACA 63-8xx series, specifically the NACA 63-815, a 15-percent-thick laminar-flow airfoil from the NACA 6-series that was originally developed for aircraft wings but has been adapted extensively for marine hydrokinetic turbine applications because of its favorable cavitation characteristics — the minimum pressure coefficient C_p,min on the suction surface of the NACA 63-815 at an angle of attack of 6 degrees and a Reynolds number of 2 × 10⁶ (the characteristic Reynolds number for the Atlantis blade at the 75 percent radius location, where Re = ρV_rel c/μ, with V_rel being the local relative velocity at the blade element, approximately 8.2 m/s at the design TSR of 5.0 and free-stream velocity of 1.5 m/s, c = 0.07 m at the 75 percent radius, ρ = 1,025 kg/m³, and μ = 1.3 × 10⁻³ Pa·s for seawater at 10°C, yielding Re = 1,025 × 8.2 × 0.07 / 1.3 × 10⁻³ = 453,000, which is transitional between laminar and fully turbulent flow, meaning that the boundary layer state — and consequently the cavitation inception point — is sensitive to the surface roughness, the free-stream turbulence intensity, and the pressure gradient along the chord, all of which must be considered in the cavitation analysis) is approximately -1.2, corresponding to a minimum pressure on the suction surface of P_min = C_p,min × ½ρV_rel² + P_static, where P_static at 45 meters depth is P_atm + ρgh = 101,325 + 1,025 × 9.81 × 45 = 101,325 + 452,561 = 553,886 Pa ≈ 5.5 bar absolute, and ½ρV_rel² = ½ × 1,025 × 8.2² = 34,500 Pa, giving P_min = -1.2 × 34,500 + 553,886 = -41,400 + 553,886 = 512,486 Pa ≈ 5.1 bar absolute, which is far above the vapor pressure of water at 10°C (P_vap ≈ 1,228 Pa), indicating that cavitation — the formation of vapor bubbles when the local pressure falls below the vapor pressure, with the subsequent collapse of these bubbles generating shock waves that can erode the blade surface (cavitation pitting) and reduce the blade's hydrodynamic efficiency through the disruption of the smooth flow over the suction surface — will not occur under normal operating conditions at the Atlantis deployment depth, a finding that is consistent with the well-known observation that cavitation is rarely a concern for tidal turbines deployed at depths greater than approximately 20 to 30 meters, where the hydrostatic = (2π × 450/60) × 0.6 = 47.1 × 0.6 = 28.3 m/s at the maximum rotor speed of 450 RPM, corresponding to a tip-speed ratio of 28.3/1.5 = 18.9, far above the design TSR of 5.0, indicating that the blade P_vap)/(½ρV_tip²) = (553,886 - 1,228)/(½ × 1,025 × 7.5²) = 552,658/28,828 = 19.2, far above the critical cavitation number of approximately 2 to 3 for the NACA 63-815 at angles of attack below 8 The rotor's 1.2-meter diameter was selected through a systematic optimization that balanced the competing requirements of maximizing the swept area — and hence the power output, which scales with the square of the diameter for a given current velocity — against the constraints imposed by the pod deployment logistics (the turbine, including the shroud, the generator nacelle, and the support strut, must be transportable on the same multi-axle trailer as the pod and must be deployable by the Charlemagne-class vessel's 25-tonne crane, limiting the turbine assembly mass to approximately 1,800 kilograms), by the structural loading during turbidite events (a larger rotor diameter increases the drag force on the turbine during sediment-laden flow, the drag force scaling with the projected area and the square of the flow velocity, with a 2.0-meter diameter rotor experiencing approximately 2.8 times the drag force of a 1.2-meter rotor at the same flow velocity, a load that would require a correspondingly stronger — and heavier — support strut and seabed foundation, increasing the total system mass by approximately 60 percent and the deployment cost by approximately 45 percent, according to the ZIRCONIA cost model), and by the wake recovery distance between adjacent pods in the hexagonal community layout (a larger rotor diameter generates a larger wake with a longer recovery distance, requiring greater inter-pod spacing to prevent the downstream turbines from operating in the reduced-velocity wake of the upstream turbines, with the wake velocity deficit recovering to 90 percent of the free-stream velocity at a distance of approximately 8 to 10 rotor diameters downstream of the turbine, a rule of thumb derived from wind turbine wake studies and validated for tidal turbines through the ReDAPT (Reliable Data Acquisition Platform for Tidal) project conducted by the University of Edinburgh at the EMEC (European Marine Energy Centre) tidal test site in Orkney, Scotland, where acoustic Doppler velocimeter measurements of the wake behind a 1.0 MW tidal turbine demonstrated that the velocity deficit at 10 diameters downstream was approximately 12 percent of the free-stream velocity, corresponding to a 90 percent recovery, consistent with the wind turbine wake models when scaled by the appropriate turbulence intensity and atmospheric — or in this case, oceanic — stability parameters). The selected 1.2-meter diameter yields a swept area of 1.13 m² and, with the shroud augmentation factor of 1.7 at the design current velocity of 1.5 m/s, an effective power coefficient C_p,eff = C_p × (indicating that tip losses reduce the effective blade loading by approximately 3 percent at the tip region, a modest penalty that the BEM code accounts for by reducing the local thrust coefficient by a factor of F, ensuring that the calculated power output matches the experimentally measured values from the 1:3 scale model tests conducted at the Carrington Storm Motors hydrodynamic test facility — a recirculating water channel with a test section of 2.0 m × 1.5 m × 8.0 m, capable of flow velocities up to 3.0 m/s with a turbulence intensity of less than 2 percent, where a 0.4-meter diameter the BEM model for the full-scale design), and with the hub loss factor — similarly calculated but at the blade root, where the sudden change in geometry from the cylindrical hub to the airfoil-shaped value that exceeds the Betz limit of 0.593 and that has historically been a source of confusion in the diffuser-augmented turbine literature, with some early proponents claiming violation of the Betz limit, but which is resolved by recognizing that the Betz limit applies to the power extracted from the free-stream kinetic energy flux through the rotor swept area, and that the shroud effectively increases the mass flow through the rotor by drawing fluid from a larger upstream capture area — the "inlet capture area" of the shroud, which for the Atlantis design with a 600 mm inlet diameter is A_inlet = π × (0.3)² = 0.283 m², while the effective capture area — the cross-sectional area of the free-stream from which kinetic energy is extracted, which is larger than the physical inlet area because the low-pressure region created by the shroud's lift draws streamlines inward from beyond the physical inlet — is approximately A_effective ≈ A_inlet × (V_augmented/V_free-stream) ≈ 0.283 × 1.7 ≈ 0.481 m², and the power extracted from the free-stream through this effective area, at the Betz limit, is P = 0.593 × ½ × 1,025 × 0.481 × 1.5³ = 0.593 × ½ × 1,025 × 0.481 × 3.375 = 0.593 × 831 = 493 watts, while the actual shaft power at the rotor, with the rotor C_p of 0.45, is P_shaft = 0.45 × ½ × 1,025 × 1.13 × (1.5 × 1.7)³ = 0.45 × ½ × 1,025 × 1.13 × 16.6 = 0.45 × 9,610 = 4,325 watts, a value that exceeds the Betz-limited power extracted from the physical inlet area — 0.593 × ½ × 1,025 × 0.283 × 1.5³ = 0.593 × 490 = 291 watts — by a factor of approximately 15, not because of any violation of the Betz limit, but because the effective capture area is substantially larger than the physical inlet area, and the Betz limit applies to the power extracted from the free-stream through the effective capture area, not through the physical rotor area — a subtlety that the Atlantis design team, led by the Spruce-Drake persona's Dolittle register, clarified through a careful derivation The generator that converts the rotor's mechanical shaft power to electrical power is a direct-drive permanent magnet synchronous generator (PMSG) with neodymium-iron-boron (NdFeB) N52-grade magnets — the highest commercially available energy product, with a maximum energy product (BH)_max of approximately 400 kJ/m³ and a remanence B_r of approximately 1.45 T at 20°C, properties that enable the generator to achieve a high power density of approximately 1.2 kW/kg in the 2.5 kW rated power range, compared to approximately 0.3 kW/kg for a comparable induction generator, meaning that the direct-drive PMSG eliminates the need for a gearbox — a major source of mechanical failure in conventional wind and tidal turbines, with gearbox failures accounting for approximately 20 percent of all wind turbine downtime and requiring heavy-lift crane vessels for offshore replacement, an operation that would be prohibitively expensive and logistically complex at the Atlantis deployment depth of 45 meters — and instead couples the rotor directly to the generator, with the rotor hub mounted on the generator shaft through a tapered roller bearing assembly that accommodates both the axial thrust from the rotor (approximately 2,500 N at the design point, directed downstream) and the radial loads from the rotor weight and the hydrodynamic imbalance forces (approximately 500 N at the design point, varying with the blade pitch angle and the current turbulence intensity), the bearing assembly being a paired set of SKF 32208 J2/Q tapered roller bearings in a face-to-face (X) arrangement that provides axial location in both directions and accommodates thermal expansion of the shaft without inducing excessive preload, with the bearings being lubricated for life with Klüber Isoflex NBU 15, a synthetic high-speed grease with a base oil viscosity of 15 cSt at 40°C, a dropping point of 250°C, and a service temperature range of -50°C to +140°C, providing a calculated bearing life L_10h The copper-nickel antifouling system and the ultrasonic anti-biofouling system represent complementary approaches to the problem of marine biofouling — the accumulation of microorganisms, algae, barnacles, mussels, and other marine organisms on submerged surfaces, a process that begins within hours of immersion with the adsorption of dissolved organic molecules (the "conditioning film"), followed within days by the attachment of bacteria and diatoms (the "biofilm" or "slime layer"), and within weeks to months by the settlement of macrofouling organisms such as barnacles (Balanus spp.), mussels (Mytilus spp.), tubeworms (Hydroides spp.), and bryozoans, all of which increase the hydrodynamic drag on the turbine blades, reducing the power coefficient by up to 20 percent for a heavily fouled blade, and add mass to the rotor that shifts the blade's natural frequencies, potentially causing resonance with the rotor's rotational frequency or its harmonics, a condition that can lead to fatigue crack initiation and propagation in the composite blade structure, with the fatigue life of a fouled blade being reduced by up to 50 percent compared to a clean blade according to the DNV-ST-0164 standard for tidal turbine certification. The passive copper-nickel antifouling system consists of a 1.5-millimeter-thick cladding of 90/10 copper-nickel alloy (C70600, with 88.6 percent Cu, 10.0 percent Ni, 1.4 percent Fe, and trace Mn) that is metallurgically bonded to the blade's LBFRP composite surface during the blade fabrication process — the copper-nickel foil is placed in the mold before the AFP layup and co-cured with the Elium thermoplastic resin at 180°C, the resin penetrating the micro-roughened inner surface of the foil (prepared by grit-blasting with 120-grit alumina at 400 kPa) to create a mechanical interlock with a lap-shear strength of approximately 15 MPa, sufficient to prevent delamination of the foil under the cyclic bending loads experienced by the blade, which at the root experience a fully reversed (R = -1) bending stress of approximately ±45 MPa at the design point, giving a fatigue life — predicted by the Goodman diagram for the copper-nickel alloy, with an endurance limit of approximately 140 MPa at 10⁸ cycles — that exceeds the 25-year design life by a factor of approximately 3, confirming the mechanical integrity of the antifouling cladding. The copper-nickel alloy functions as an antifoulant through the controlled corrosion of the copper at the alloy-seawater interface, releasing cuprous ions (Cu⁺) at a rate of approximately 5 to 15 μg/cm²/day, a concentration that is toxic to the larval stages of most macrofouling organisms — the copper ion interferes with the enzyme systems responsible for cellular respiration and protein synthesis in the settling larvae, preventing their attachment and metamorphosis — but that is below the chronic toxicity threshold for most pelagic marine organisms, which is approximately 25 μg/L for the most sensitive species (sea urchin larvae, Strongylocentrotus purpuratus), and that is rapidly diluted in the oceanic water column to concentrations below 1 μg/L at a distance of approximately 1 meter from the turbine, ensuring that the Atlantis turbine's copper emissions contribute negligibly to the ambient copper concentration in the Monterey Bay National Marine Sanctuary, which the US Geological Survey has measured at 0.2 to 0.5 μg/L in the offshore waters of the Sanctuary, a finding that the Atlantis environmental impact assessment — conducted in accordance with the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) and reviewed by the NOAA Office of National Marine Sanctuaries — used to demonstrate that the copper emissions from all twelve Atlantis pods (six at Monterey, plus a margin for the Osa site) would increase the Sanctuary's copper concentration by less than 0.01 percent, a de minimis impact that the NOAA review concluded does not constitute a significant environmental effect under NEPA.

The active ultrasonic anti-biofouling system supplements the passive copper-nickel cladding by generating high-frequency acoustic waves — 20 to 40 kHz, at a power level of approximately 0.1 W/cm² at the blade surface — through piezoelectric transducers mounted inside the blade root, the ultrasonic energy being transmitted through the blade's LBFRP composite structure to the outer surface, where it creates microscopic cavitation and acoustic streaming effects that physically disrupt the attachment of microorganisms to the surface, preventing the formation of the biofilm that serves as the precursor to macrofouling settlement, a mechanism that has been demonstrated to reduce biofouling accumulation on ship hulls by 70 to 90 percent in trials conducted by the US Navy and the European Union's AMBIO (Advanced Nanostructured Surfaces for the Control of Biofouling) project, which tested ultrasonic antifouling on composite panels immersed at the Kristineberg Marine Research Station in Sweden for 12 months and found that panels with ultrasonic treatment exhibited approximately 85 percent less fouling by weight than untreated control panels, with the ultrasonic system consuming approximately 25 watts of electrical power per square meter of protected surface, which for the Atlantis turbine's three blades with a combined surface area of approximately 0.9 m² corresponds to a continuous power consumption of approximately 23 watts — a negligible fraction of the turbine's 2.5 kW output — and which the Atlantis system powers directly from the 48 V DC bus through a dedicated DC-AC inverter that generates the 40 kHz sinusoidal drive signal for the piezoelectric transducers, the inverter being a simple H-bridge configuration with four MOSFETs driven by a digital signal processor (DSP) that synthesizes the 40 kHz carrier with a frequency stability of ±0.1 percent through a phase-locked loop referencing a temperature-compensated crystal oscillator (TCXO), ensuring that the ultrasonic frequency does not drift into the hearing range of marine mammals — dolphins and porpoises have peak hearing sensitivity in the 20 to 150 kHz range, and the Atlantis ultrasonic system's 40 kHz operating frequency is above the most sensitive range for these species (which is 30 to 120 kHz for Tursiops truncatus, the common bottlenose dolphin, per the audiogram measurements of Johnson, 1967), while being below the frequency at which ultrasonic attenuation in seawater becomes prohibitive (attenuation increases approximately as the square of frequency above 100 kHz, reaching approximately 30 dB/km at 200 kHz, meaning that at 40 kHz the acoustic energy propagates only a few meters before being absorbed by the seawater, a distance that is sufficient to protect the turbine blades but insufficient to create a far-field acoustic impact).

The Monterey current data, collected over 12 months by the ADCP deployment at 45 meters depth, establishes the velocity distribution that defines the turbine's annual energy production: the mean current velocity is 1.4 m/s, with a standard deviation of 0.3 m/s, a minimum recorded velocity of 0.4 m/s (during a neap tide slack-water period in December, coinciding with a period of weak thermohaline circulation due to the winter breakdown of the seasonal thermocline), and a maximum recorded velocity of 2.1 m/s (during a spring tide in June, enhanced by the strong summer stratification that drives a baroclinic pressure gradient reinforcing the barotropic tidal flow). The diurnal variation shows a dominant semidiurnal period of 12.42 hours (the M₂ tidal constituent, with an amplitude of 0.25 m/s) superimposed on a weaker diurnal period of 24.0 hours (the K₁ constituent, with an amplitude of 0.10 m/s), producing the characteristic mixed semidiurnal pattern with two unequal velocity peaks per day, the higher peak ("higher high water" current) occurring approximately 2 hours after the higher high water tide at the Monterey tide gauge, and the lower peak ("lower high water" current) occurring approximately 3 hours after the lower high water tide, the phase lag between the tide gauge and the current meter being a consequence of the frictional effects of the seafloor on the tidal wave propagation. The turbine's annual energy production, calculated by integrating the turbine power curve — the relationship between the free-stream current velocity V and the electrical power output P_e(V), which is The Osa Peninsula site, with its mean current velocity of 1.3 m/s and a narrower velocity distribution (standard deviation of 0.2 m/s, reflecting the Costa Rican coast's predominantly diurnal tidal regime with a single high and low tide per day, driven by the dominance of the K₁ tidal constituent at low latitudes where the Coriolis parameter is small and the diurnal tidal potential is amplified relative to the semidiurnal potential), yields a lower gross annual energy production of approximately 9,800 kWh for the same 1.2-meter shrouded turbine, corresponding to a capacity factor of approximately 44.7 percent, and a net DC bus energy of approximately 9,800 × 0.92 × 0.89 × 0.95 = 7,630 kWh/year, or an average power of 0.87 kW — less than the Monterey site's 1.30 kW average, reflecting the lower mean current velocity and the cubic dependence of power on velocity, and requiring a larger Grid-Seed battery capacity at the Osa site (Part 070 specifies 45 kg of hydrogen storage at Monterey and 90 kg at Osa, the difference reflecting the Osa site's greater reliance on the fuel cell backup due to the lower turbine capacity factor), a site-specific adaptation that the Atlantis design accommodates through the modular architecture of the power system, in which the turbine, battery, and fuel cell are independent, standardized modules connected to the common 48 V DC bus, allowing the system to be configured for each deployment site by adjusting the number of battery modules and the hydrogen storage capacity without redesigning any of the individual components, a modularity that the ZIRCONIA cost analysis identifies as a key enabler of the Atlantis program's economic feasibility, as it allows the same pod design to be deployed at multiple sites with varying resource characteristics, amortizing the pod's non-recurring engineering (NRE) costs across a larger number of units.

The MPPT (Maximum Power Point Tracking) charge controller that interfaces the turbine's boost converter output to the 48 V DC bus is a critical subsystem that dynamically adjusts the generator's electrical loading to extract the maximum mechanical power from the rotor at any given current velocity, a control problem that is mathematically equivalent to maximizing the product of the generator torque τ_g and the rotor angular velocity ω, where the generator torque is proportional to the quadrature-axis current i_q in the field-oriented control (FOC) framework that the PMSG drive uses, with the relationship τ_g = (3/2) × (P/2) × λ_PM × i_q, where P is the number of poles (16), λ_PM is the permanent magnet flux linkage in the stator windings (approximately 0.12 Wb for the N52 magnets with the 16-pole, 18-slot fractional-slot concentrated winding configuration that the Atlantis generator employs, a winding topology that reduces the cogging torque — the torque ripple caused by the attraction between the permanent magnets and the stator teeth, which produces vibration and acoustic noise and, in a tidal turbine, would create periodic torque fluctuations at the blade-passing frequency that could excite blade resonances — to approximately 1.5 percent of the rated torque, compared to approximately 5 to 10 percent for an integer-slot distributed winding, by selecting a slot-pole combination with a greatest common divisor of 2, specifically 16 poles and 18 slots, which yields a cogging torque frequency of 144 cycles per mechanical revolution and a cogging torque magnitude that is minimized by the fractional offset between the rotor poles and the stator slots, a design technique that is well-established in the PM machine literature and that the Atlantis generator design team validated through finite-element electromagnetic analysis using ANSYS Maxwell, which predicted a cogging torque of 1.2 percent of rated torque, in excellent agreement with the measured value of 1.4 percent from the dynamometer test of the prototype generator), and i_q is the torque-producing component of the stator current, which the FOC algorithm controls independently of the flux-producing component i_d (the direct-axis current), setting i_d = 0 for maximum torque-per-ampere operation below the base speed (approximately 300 RPM, corresponding to the rated voltage of 60 V at the generator terminals) and applying a negative i_d (field-weakening) above the base speed to reduce the back-EMF and allow operation at higher speeds without exceeding the generator's voltage rating, a flux-weakening strategy that the Atlantis turbine uses during the high-current periods when the rotor speed would otherwise exceed the 450 RPM mechanical limit, with the i_d reference being calculated by a voltage controller that limits the modulation index of the space-vector PWM inverter to 0.95, ensuring that the inverter does not saturate and lose control of the stator current, which would result in overcurrent and potential demagnetization of the permanent magnets — NdFeB magnets irreversibly demagnetize if the operating point falls below the "knee" of the BH curve, which for N52 grade at 20°C occurs at an internal field of approximately -500 kA/m, and the FOC's overcurrent protection limits i_q such that the stator MMF does not exceed this threshold, with a safety factor of 2.0 to account for the elevated magnet temperature (the magnets operate at approximately 80°C in the Atlantis generator, at which temperature the intrinsic coercivity H_ci of N52 drops from approximately 1,100 kA/m at 20°C to approximately 800 kA/m at 80°C, still well above the demagnetization threshold but with reduced margin).

The MPPT algorithm is a perturb-and-observe (P&O) method that periodically perturbs the generator's electrical loading — specifically, the boost converter's duty cycle — by a small increment ΔD and observes the resulting change in the DC bus power P_dc = V_dc × I_dc, where V_dc is the regulated 48 V bus voltage and I_dc is the boost converter output current: if the power increases, the perturbation is continued in the same direction; if the power decreases, the perturbation direction is reversed, the algorithm converging to the optimal duty cycle that maximizes P_dc at the current turbine operating point, with the perturbation step size ΔD being 0.5 percent of the duty cycle range and the perturbation period being 100 milliseconds, parameters that have been tuned through simulation and hardware-in-the-loop testing to achieve a tracking efficiency — the ratio of the actual energy captured by the MPPT controller to the maximum possible energy that could be captured with perfect knowledge of the optimal operating point — of approximately 97 percent, a value that the MPPT controller performance was benchmarked against during the 1:3 scale model tests in the Carrington Storm Motors water channel, where the turbine was subjected to a representative current velocity time series — a 6-hour excerpt from the Monterey ADCP dataset, played back in real-time through the water channel's flow control system, which uses a variable-frequency drive on the 50 kW circulation pump to reproduce the velocity fluctuations with a bandwidth of 0.5 Hz, sufficient to capture the turbulent fluctuations that affect turbine performance at timescales of 2 seconds and longer — and the measured energy capture was 96.8 percent of the theoretical maximum calculated from the turbine's steady-state power curve, validating the MPPT controller's performance and confirming the 97 percent tracking efficiency specification.

The wake recovery distance of 8 to 10 rotor diameters — approximately 10 to 12 meters for the Atlantis turbine's 1.2-meter rotor — is a critical parameter for the hexagonal community layout, as described in Section 7 of the main CSMFAB077 document, because the downstream pod's turbine must operate in the wake of the upstream pod's turbine when the current direction aligns with the inter-pod axis, a condition that occurs for approximately 35 percent of the time at the Monterey site based on the ADCP current direction data, which shows that the principal axis of the tidal current (the direction of the M₂ tidal ellipse's major axis) is aligned within ±15 degrees of the 120-degree inter-pod axis for three of the six pods in the hexagonal layout, meaning that those pods experience wake interference during the ebb or flood tide, depending on their position relative to the current direction. The wake velocity deficit at 10 diameters downstream is approximately 12 percent of the free-stream velocity, per the ReDAPT project data, which for the Atlantis turbine at the 1.5 m/s rated velocity corresponds to an effective velocity at the downstream rotor of 1.5 × (1 - 0.12) = 1.32 m/s, reducing the power output of the downstream turbine by approximately (1.32/1.5)³ = 0.68, or a 32 percent reduction, for the approximately 35 percent of the time that the downstream turbine operates in the upstream turbine's wake, corresponding to an annual energy loss of approximately 0.35 × 0.32 = 11.2 percent for the affected pods, a loss that the Atlantis energy balance analysis accounts for by sizing the Grid-Seed battery and the hydrogen storage to provide sufficient buffering capacity for the entire community, with the battery's 50 kWh total capacity (30 kWh usable) being sufficient to absorb the wake-induced power fluctuations at the community level — the six pods share a common 48 V DC bus through the inter-pod cable network, which allows the pods that are not experiencing wake interference to supply power to the pods that are, with the bus's power-sharing being managed by a droop control algorithm in which each pod's boost converter adjusts its output voltage setpoint as a function of its output current, V_ref = V_nom - k_droop × I_out, where V_nom = 48.0 V and k_droop = 0.05 V/A, so that a pod supplying 50 A (2.4 kW) to the bus reduces its voltage setpoint by 0.05 × 50 = 2.5 V to 45.5 V, while a pod drawing 30 A (1.44 kW) from the bus increases its voltage setpoint by... actually, the droop control works in the opposite direction — a pod that is supplying power to the bus reduces its voltage as the current increases, while a pod that is drawing power sees a higher bus voltage and reduces its own generation accordingly, the net effect being that the pods with higher turbine output naturally supply a larger share of the community load, a decentralized control strategy that requires no central controller and no communication between pods, just the shared voltage reference on the common DC bus, a robustness feature that the Torvalds register validated through a Monte Carlo simulation of 10,000 randomly generated current velocity scenarios, confirming that the community's power balance is maintained — the total generation equals the total load plus the battery charging/discharging power — for all scenarios, with no scenario resulting in a bus voltage deviation exceeding ±5 percent from the 48 V nominal, well within the ±10 percent tolerance of the pod's power supplies, which are designed to accept input voltages from 43.2 V to 52.8 V, corresponding to the 48 V nominal ±10 percent.
