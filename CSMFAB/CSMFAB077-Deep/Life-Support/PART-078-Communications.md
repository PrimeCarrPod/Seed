## 1. ACOUSTIC MODEM NETWORK AND UNDERWATER TELEMETRY

The primary inter-pod and pod-to-surface communication modality — the acoustic modem network — is anchored by the EvoLogics S2CR 18/34 underwater acoustic modem, a device that transmits and receives digital data through the water column using orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing (OFDM) in the 18 to 34 kilohertz frequency band, a band that represents the optimal compromise — for the Monterey and Osa demonstration sites, with their respective water depths of 45 meters and 30 meters, their sediment types (fine sand and silt at Monterey, carbonate sand at Osa), and their typical ambient noise spectra (dominated at low frequencies — below 1 kilohertz — by the distant rumble of surface shipping and the wind-driven wave noise of the California Current system at Monterey and the Equatorial Countercurrent at Osa, and at higher frequencies — above 10 kilohertz — by the thermal noise of the water molecules themselves, which, per the classical Rayleigh-Jeans approximation to Planck's law, produces an acoustic noise spectral density that increases with the square of the frequency, such that at 30 kilohertz the thermal noise level is approximately 9 decibels above the thermal noise level at 10 kilohertz, an increase that, while modest relative to the approximately 20 to 30 decibel variability in the ambient noise level caused by changes in the surface weather and the local biological activity — the snapping shrimp (Alpheidae) that generate a distinctive crackling noise in the 2 to 15 kilohertz band, with peak spectral density at approximately 5 kilohertz, and that are present in significant numbers at both the Monterey and Osa sites, their collective acoustic output producing a sound pressure level of approximately 90 decibels referenced to 1 micropascal at 1 meter at the peak frequency, a level that is comparable to the ambient noise from a distant thunderstorm and that must be accounted for in the acoustic modem's signal-to-noise ratio budget — is nonetheless a fundamental physical limit that cannot be overcome by improvements in modem design and that determines the ultimate range and data-rate performance of any acoustic communication system operating in a given frequency band, a limit that the EvoLogics modem approaches through its implementation of advanced digital signal processing techniques — adaptive channel equalization, which compensates for the multipath propagation that results from the reflection of the acoustic signal off the sea surface, the seabed, and the thermocline, creating multiple copies of the transmitted signal that arrive at the receiver at different times and with different amplitudes and phases, a phenomenon that, if uncorrected, would cause intersymbol interference — the temporal smearing of one transmitted symbol into the adjacent symbols, a distortion that, at the data rates required for the Atlantis pod's telemetry and communication needs, would render the received data unintelligible — and that the EvoLogics modem mitigates through the use of a cyclic prefix — a guard interval inserted between OFDM symbols that exceeds the maximum expected multipath delay spread, which, at the Monterey site's 45-meter depth with a thermocline at approximately 15 to 25 meters and a seabed of fine sand (reflection coefficient approximately 0.3 at 20 kilohertz, corresponding to an acoustic impedance mismatch of approximately 1.5 between the water column and the seabed sediment), is approximately 10 to 15 milliseconds, such that the cyclic prefix duration of 20 milliseconds provides a margin of approximately 5 to 10 milliseconds, ensuring that the intersymbol interference from the delayed multipath arrivals does not contaminate the OFDM symbol that the receiver is currently decoding, at the cost of approximately 10 percent overhead — the cyclic prefix adds 20 milliseconds to each 200-millisecond OFDM symbol, a 10 percent overhead that reduces the net data rate from the gross channel capacity but that, for the Atlantis pod's telemetry requirements — a transmission of approximately 1 kilobit of data (pod status, life support parameters, sensor readings, and a 128-bit emergency beacon payload) every 5 minutes, or approximately 3.3 bits per second — is negligible, as the EvoLogics modem's maximum data rate of 13.9 kilobits per second is approximately 4,200 times the required telemetry rate, providing an enormous margin for the transmission of additional data — voice-grade audio (2.4 kilobits per second using a low-bitrate speech codec, sufficient for intelligible voice communication between pods, enabling the crew of Pod A to speak directly with the crew of Pod B without the delay and the formality of a text-based message, a capability that the crew psychologist has identified as an important contributor to the sense of social connectedness between the pods, as the sound of a human voice — the tone, the inflection, the emotional content that text cannot convey — is a powerful social signal that reassures the listener that the speaker is alive, well, and emotionally present, a reassurance that, in the isolated, confined environment of the Atlantis pod, where every crew interaction is freighted with the knowledge that these four people are each other's only face-to-face human contact for 90 days, is psychologically invaluable) and low-resolution still images (a compressed JPEG image of the exterior view from the pod's underwater camera, providing the adjacent pod's crew with a visual confirmation of the exterior conditions — the visibility, the presence of marine life, any debris or damage — that a text-based status report cannot convey, a capability that the pod's operations manual specifies should be exercised at least once per day, during the morning inter-pod check-in, when each pod transmits a "good morning" image to the other pods, a ritual that the crew psychologist has designed as a subtle but effective reinforcement of the inter-pod social bonds, a daily reminder that the crew is part of a community of 24 — or 16, at the Osa site — not merely a crew of 4, and that the challenges, the triumphs, and the daily routines of the mission are shared across the entire Atlantis demonstration community) — within the available acoustic channel capacity, a capacity that is itself variable, dependent on the instantaneous signal-to-noise ratio, the multipath propagation conditions, the presence or absence of the thermocline — which, at the Monterey site, is a seasonal feature that develops in the spring and summer months (April through September), when the surface waters are warmed by solar radiation to temperatures of 14 to 16°C, creating a density gradient — the thermocline — at a depth of approximately 15 to 25 meters that separates the warm, less-dense surface layer from the cold, more-dense deep layer at 10 to 12°C, and that acts as an acoustic waveguide, trapping the acoustic energy in the surface layer if the source and the receiver are both above the thermocline, or in the deep layer if both are below the thermocline, and reflecting the acoustic energy — like a mirror — if the source and the receiver are on opposite sides of the thermocline, such that an acoustic signal transmitted from a pod at 45 meters depth (below the thermocline) to the surface relay buoy (above the thermocline) would be partially reflected at the thermocline, losing approximately 3 to 6 decibels of signal strength — equivalent to reducing the effective range of the modem by approximately 30 to 50 percent — relative to the isothermal winter conditions (November through March), when the water column is well-mixed by winter storms, the temperature is approximately uniform at 12 to 13°C from the surface to the seabed, and the acoustic signal propagates without the reflection loss, achieving the maximum range of approximately 5 to 6 kilometers at the maximum data rate of 13.9 kilobits per second, a range that is approximately double the inter-pod spacing of 30 meters and that therefore provides an enormous margin for the inter-pod acoustic link, a margin that the acoustic engineer characterizes as "gross overkill for the inter-pod link, but exactly what you want for the pod-to-surface link when the surface buoy is destroyed by a megatsunami and the only remaining communication path is the acoustic modem broadcasting to any Aegis C vessel or shore station within range, a scenario in which the 5-kilometer summer range and the 6-kilometer winter range might be the difference between a pod that is heard and a pod that is not, and therefore between a crew that is rescued and a crew that is not, a distinction that justifies every decibel of acoustic link margin that the over-engineered inter-pod link provides, a margin that is not wasted — it is stored, it is banked, it is the acoustic reserve that the pod draws upon when the surface buoy is gone, when the fiber-optic cable is severed, when the Iridium link is dead, and when the only voice that the pod has is the acoustic voice, calling into the darkness, hoping that someone — anyone — is listening."

The acoustic modem network employs a time-division multiple access (TDMA) protocol in which each pod is assigned a 30-second transmission slot within a 5-minute TDMA frame — a frame structure that can accommodate up to 10 pods (10 slots of 30 seconds each, totaling 300 seconds, or exactly 5 minutes, the frame duration, with the 11th pod — if a future expansion of the Atlantis community beyond the 10-pod maximum were to occur — requiring the reconfiguration of the TDMA schedule, a flexibility that the protocol's software-defined implementation accommodates through a simple entry in the TDMA configuration file, which is transmitted to all pods via the surface relay buoy's Iridium link and which takes effect at the beginning of the next TDMA frame, a transition that is seamless because the frame boundaries are synchronized to the GPS-disciplined oscillator in the surface relay buoy, which distributes a precise 1-pulse-per-second timing signal to all pods via the fiber-optic inter-pod network, such that each pod's internal clock is synchronized to within approximately 1 microsecond of the GPS time standard — the time standard that the Global Positioning System's constellation of atomic clocks (each clock's frequency stability is approximately 1 part in 10^13 over a 1-day averaging interval, a level of precision that, if the clock were a ruler measuring the distance from the Earth to the Moon — approximately 384,400 kilometers — would be accurate to within approximately 4 micrometers, or one-tenth the width of a human hair, an astonishing precision that the Atlantis pods inherit from the GPS constellation through the surface buoy's GPS receiver, a $50 commercial off-the-shelf module that extracts the timing signal from the GPS carrier and delivers it to the buoy's embedded computer, which formats it as a network time protocol (NTP) packet and transmits it to the pods, a chain of technology that connects the Atlantis pods, through the surface buoy, through the GPS satellite constellation, to the United States Naval Observatory's Master Clock in Washington, D.C., the ultimate source of the precise time that enables the TDMA protocol to function without collisions — two pods transmitting simultaneously, their acoustic signals interfering with each other at the receiver, rendering both signals unintelligible, a collision that would waste the transmission slot, delay the delivery of the telemetry data, and potentially — if the collision occurred during an emergency transmission — prevent the pod's distress call from being received, a catastrophic failure of the communication system that the TDMA protocol and the GPS-derived time synchronization are specifically designed to prevent, at the cost of the approximately 1 microsecond of timing accuracy that the GPS system provides, a cost that, in the context of a $47.8 million project, is — quite literally, at the price of a $50 GPS receiver module — one of the most cost-effective investments in the entire Atlantis architecture, a fact that the acoustic engineer highlights during the design review with a slide that displays the $50 GPS module next to the $15,000 EvoLogics modem, the caption reading: "The modem is the voice. The GPS is the conductor. Without the conductor, the voice is noise. The conductor cost: $50. The value of the voice when you're drowning: Priceless." The 30-second transmission slot, allocated every 5 minutes, provides each pod with a dedicated, collision-free channel for the transmission of its telemetry data — the S-Bus beacon packet described in the S-Bus Beacon specification, plus the pod's environmental sensor data (temperature, pressure, humidity, oxygen concentration, carbon dioxide concentration, volatile organic compound concentration, particulate concentration, and the status of each life support subsystem, encoded as a structured binary message — a 1,024-bit telemetry frame that the pod's embedded computer assembles every 5 minutes from the sensor data stream and that the acoustic modem transmits, at its maximum data rate of 13.9 kilobits per second, in approximately 74 milliseconds, occupying less than 0.25 percent of the 30-second transmission slot, the remaining 29.926 seconds of the slot being available for higher-bandwidth data — voice, images, software updates, the crew's personal email messages to their families on the surface, which the crew composes during their off-duty hours and which are queued for transmission during the pod's next TDMA slot, a queuing mechanism that the communication protocol manages through a priority scheme: emergency data (S-Bus beacon, life support alerts, flood/fire/toxic gas alarms) is transmitted immediately, preempting any lower-priority data in the queue, using a reserved 5-millisecond interval at the beginning of each transmission slot — the emergency preemption window — that the TDMA protocol reserves for exactly this purpose, ensuring that even if the pod's transmission queue is full of non-emergency data at the moment an emergency is declared, the emergency data can be transmitted without waiting for the current slot to end and the next frame to begin, a delay that, in the worst case — the pod's transmission slot has just ended, and the next slot is 5 minutes (minus one slot duration) away — would be approximately 4.5 minutes, a delay that, in the context of a pod that is flooding, burning, or losing pressure, is some 270 seconds, each second of which brings the pod closer to the unsurvivable condition that the evacuation decision matrix defines, and each second of which is therefore unacceptable, a conclusion that led the acoustic engineer to incorporate the emergency preemption window into the TDMA protocol, an innovation that adds approximately 5 milliseconds of overhead to each 30-second slot — an overhead of approximately 0.017 percent — but that ensures that an emergency transmission can be initiated within 30 seconds of the emergency being declared, regardless of the pod's position in the TDMA rotation, a delay that the emergency response analysis determined to be acceptable, as the 30 seconds are consumed by the crew's donning of the PARAT 5500 masks, the crew commander's assessment of the situation, and the decision to declare the emergency — tasks that must be completed before the emergency transmission is sent — such that the 30-second delay between the emergency declaration and the initiation of the acoustic transmission does not represent an additional delay in the emergency response but rather the synchronization of the communication timeline with the crew's response timeline, a synchronization that the communication engineer characterizes as "latency that is hidden by the human response time — the best kind of latency, because it isn't latency at all, it's just the natural pause between the decision and the action, and the action — the transmission — happens as soon as the decision is made, which is as soon as it can happen, which is as soon as it should happen, and that is the definition of an optimally designed emergency communication system."

The acoustic channel characteristics at the two demonstration sites were measured during a comprehensive site survey conducted over a period of 12 months — from January through December 2025 — using a calibrated hydrophone array and a set of broadband acoustic transmitters deployed at the planned pod locations, a survey that characterized the ambient noise spectrum, the multipath delay spread, the transmission loss as a function of range and frequency, and the temporal variability of these parameters across the diurnal, tidal, and seasonal cycles, providing the data that the acoustic engineer used to configure the EvoLogics modems — the transmission power, the OFDM subcarrier allocation, the error-correction coding rate, and the automatic repeat-request (ARQ) retransmission parameters — for optimal performance at each site. At the Monterey site, the ambient noise spectral density at 20 kilohertz — the approximate center frequency of the EvoLogics modem's operating band — was measured at 95 decibels referenced to 1 micropascal squared per hertz (dB re 1 μPa²/Hz), a level that is approximately 10 decibels above the thermal noise floor at 20 kilohertz (approximately 85 dB re 1 μPa²/Hz) and that is dominated — the noise spectrum analysis, conducted using a 1/3-octave band decomposition, revealed a peak at 5 kilohertz (approximately 100 dB re 1 μPa²/Hz) corresponding to the snapping shrimp chorus, a peak at 20 kilohertz (approximately 95 dB) corresponding to the combined contributions of the thermal noise and the distant shipping traffic (the Monterey Bay is traversed by approximately 3,000 commercial vessels per year, most of them en route to or from the Port of Oakland, approximately 100 kilometers to the north, and the low-frequency noise — below 500 hertz — from these vessels propagates efficiently through the deep-water channel of the Monterey Submarine Canyon, contributing to the ambient noise at the pod depth of 45 meters, an effect that the site survey quantified by correlating the noise measurements with the Automatic Identification System (AIS) data that the surface relay buoy's AIS receiver collects, identifying the specific vessels responsible for the elevated noise periods and confirming the hypothesis — which the acoustic engineer had advanced during the site selection phase — that the commercial shipping noise at Monterey is a nighttime phenomenon, as the prevailing northwesterly winds strengthen during the afternoon, producing sea states of 3 to 4 on the Beaufort scale that increase the ambient noise at all frequencies, including — through the phenomenon of wind-driven wave noise, in which the oscillating bubbles entrained by breaking waves generate acoustic radiation at frequencies from approximately 500 hertz to 50 kilohertz, with a spectral peak at approximately 1 to 5 kilohertz — the frequency band of the EvoLogics modem, such that the optimal acoustic communication conditions occur during the early morning hours (02:00 to 06:00 local time), when the wind has subsided, the sea state is 1 to 2, the snapping shrimp chorus is at its nocturnal minimum — the shrimp are more active during the day, their collective acoustic output peaking at approximately 15:00 local time, when the water temperature at the seabed reaches its diurnal maximum of approximately 12°C, and declining to a minimum at approximately 03:00, a pattern that the site survey documented and that the acoustic modem's transmission schedule exploits, scheduling the highest-bandwidth data transfers — the daily download of the pod's scientific data (the oceanographic sensor data, the biological observations, the video footage from the exterior cameras, the crew's personal email and media downloads, which together total approximately 50 to 100 megabytes per day per pod) — during the 02:00 to 06:00 early-morning window, when the acoustic channel's signal-to-noise ratio is at its maximum, providing the highest data rate and the lowest error rate, and reserving the daytime slots for telemetry, voice, and emergency communications — the lower-bandwidth, higher-priority traffic that can tolerate the degraded channel conditions of the daytime real-time channel quality estimate that the EvoLogics modem computes from the signal-to-noise ratio of the received acknowledgment packets — a measure that reflects the instantaneous channel conditions, not the long-term average, and that the protocol uses to select, for each transmission slot, the data rate that maximizes the throughput for that slot's particular channel conditions, a rate-adaptive mechanism that, over the course of a typical day at the Monterey site, varies the data rate from approximately 2 kilobits per second (the minimum, during the afternoon wind event, when the channel quality is degraded by the combined effects of the wind noise and the snapping shrimp chorus) to approximately 13.9 kilobits per second (the maximum, during the early-morning calm, when the channel is at its quietest and the modem can operate at its full capability), a variation of a factor of approximately 7 that the rate-adaptive protocol exploits to deliver the highest average ## 2. SURFACE RELAY BUOY AND FIBER-OPTIC SHORE CONNECTION

The surface relay buoy is a tension-leg platform (TLP) — a buoyant, cylindrical hull of 3 meters diameter and 5 meters height, fabricated from the same LBFRP-001 lonsdaleite-reinforced basalt fiber composite as the pod's pressure hull, and anchored to the seabed by three CNT-fiber tendons, each of 25 millimeters diameter and a breaking strength of 500 kilonewtons — that floats at the ocean surface directly above the centroid of the Atlantis demonstration community, its position maintained within a watch circle of approximately 10 meters radius — a positional stability that is sufficient to prevent the fiber-optic cable, which descends from the buoy's underside to the seabed along one of the tendon paths, from experiencing excessive bending, which would increase the fiber's attenuation (bending loss, caused by the escape of the optical signal from the fiber core into the cladding when the fiber's bend radius is less than approximately 10 times the core diameter — for the single-mode fiber used in the Atlantis installation, with a core diameter of 9 micrometers, the minimum bend radius for negligible bending loss is approximately 90 micrometers, or 0.09 millimeters, a specification that the fiber-optic cable's armored construction — six single-mode fibers enclosed in a stainless steel loose tube, surrounded by a layer of aramid yarn for tensile strength, an outer jacket of high-density polyethylene for abrasion resistance, and an outer armor of galvanized steel wires for protection against the crushing force of the seabed sediment and the potential impact of falling debris, the entire cable having an outer diameter of approximately 25 millimeters and a minimum bend radius of approximately 250 millimeters — exceeds by a factor of approximately 10, providing the mechanical robustness that the cable requires to survive the deployment, the 50-year design life in the marine environment, and the potential impact of a turbidite flow, which could subject the cable to longitudinal tensions of up to 2 kilonewtons and lateral pressures of up to 500 kilopascals — loads that are within the cable's mechanical specifications, which are rated for a maximum working tension of 10 kilonewtons and a maximum crushing pressure of 2 megapascals, margins of approximately 5:1 and 4:1 respectively, margins that the cable engineer certified as adequate for the deployment environment, based on the results of a finite-element analysis of the cable's response to the predicted turbidite flow loading, an analysis that was validated through a 1:10 scale physical test in the Carrington Storm Motors hydrodynamic test facility, in which a scaled cable specimen was subjected to the scaled turbidite flow loading and was monitored for any increase in the fiber's optical attenuation — an increase that would indicate damage to the fiber, such as microcracking of the glass core, which can occur when the fiber is subjected to tensile stress exceeding approximately 0.7 gigapascals, or approximately 70 percent of the fiber's proof-test strength of 1.0 gigapascals (the industry-standard proof-test level for telecommunications-grade optical fiber, at which the fiber is guaranteed not to fail during the 25-year service life, a guarantee that the Atlantis program's 50-year design life requires to be extended — through the use of a higher proof-test level, 1.4 gigapascals, which is available from the fiber manufacturer as a custom order, at a cost premium of approximately $0.05 per meter, or approximately $50 for the 1,000 meters of fiber in the Monterey installation — a premium that the cable engineer authorized without hesitation, because a 50-cent-per-meter fiber that fails after 30 years at the bottom of the ocean, severing the pod's primary high-bandwidth communication link, would be the most expensive 50 cents ever saved, a principle that the Kiddo register articulates as "the cost of reliability is always less than the cost of cost-benefit analysis, all of which appear in the Kiddo analysis, because the Kiddo analysis is not standard, it is Aegis C, and Aegis C does not calculate cost — it calculates value, and the value of
