# CSMFAB077 — PART 128: EVACUATION PROTOCOLS — RAPID ASCENT (1.5 M/S), DECOMPRESSION STOP REQUIREMENTS, SURFACE RENDEZVOUS, HELICOPTER EXTRACTION

The Atlantis evacuation protocol addresses the singularly unforgiving operational constraint that distinguishes underwater habitation from all other forms of human occupancy: the occupants cannot simply open a door and walk outside to safety, because the pressure differential between the habitat interior at 5.5 ATA and the surface atmosphere at 1.0 ATA represents a physiologically lethal barrier that can only be crossed through a controlled decompression lasting 6.25 days — a timeline that renders conventional evacuation meaningless for any emergency that requires the crew to reach the surface within hours rather than days, and that therefore demands an evacuation strategy fundamentally different from the "exit the building and assemble in the parking lot" paradigm that governs terrestrial fire and flood evacuations. The Atlantis evacuation philosophy recognizes three distinct evacuation scenarios, each with its own timeline, physiological constraints, and operational procedures: (1) PLANNED EVACUATION — the crew completes the standard 6.25-day decompression profile (PART-121) within the pod's transfer module, ascends to the surface at the controlled rate of 0.5 meters per second (the standard ascent rate for saturation divers, limited by the need to avoid exceeding the M-value decompression limits even at the shallow depths where the pressure change rate is most physiologically hazardous), and is recovered by the surface support vessel (the Charlemagne fleet tender, which maintains station above the deployment site throughout the 90-day mission and which is equipped with a deck decompression chamber — a twin-lock hyperbaric chamber capable of accommodating up to 4 persons for the final phase of surface decompression, the "surface decompression using oxygen" (SurDO₂) protocol, which transfers the divers from the water directly into the deck chamber at surface pressure and immediately repressurizes them to 12 meters (2.2 ATA) for a final oxygen breathing period of 60 minutes, a procedure that reduces the total decompression time by approximately 12 hours compared to completing the full decompression in the water, and that the US Navy has validated through thousands of SurDO₂ procedures with a DCS incidence of less than 0.1 percent — a safety record that the Atlantis program considers sufficient to adopt the SurDO₂ protocol as the standard method for completing the planned evacuation decompression, with the additional precaution that the deck chamber is equipped with the same triple-redundant oxygen monitoring, the same BIBS oxygen delivery system, and the same medical lock recompression capability as the pod's medical facility, ensuring that any DCS symptoms that develop during the surface decompression phase can be treated immediately without transferring the patient to a shore-based facility, a capability that closes the treatment loop and eliminates the risk of DCS-related mortality or permanent disability during the planned evacuation scenario); (2) EMERGENCY EVACUATION WITH CONTROLLED DECOMPRESSION — a scenario in which the habitable volume of the pod has been compromised (by an uncontrollable fire, an unisolatable flood, or a toxic gas release that overwhelms the scrubbing capacity and cannot be purged within the 72-hour backup scrubbing endurance) to the point where the crew cannot REMAIN in the pod for the full 6.25-day decompression, but the pod's pressure boundary is sufficiently intact that the crew can DECOMPRESS WITHIN THE POD at an accelerated rate — the "expedited decompression" protocol, which accepts an increased risk of DCS (the predicted DCS incidence of 5 to 10 percent for the expedited schedule, compared to less than 1 percent for the standard schedule) in exchange for a reduction in the decompression time from 6.25 days to approximately 48 hours, achieved by increasing the ascent rate from 0.3 meters of seawater per hour (the standard continuous ascent rate) to 1.0 meter per hour, halving the decompression time while remaining within the M-value limits for the faster tissue compartments (the 2.5-minute and 5-minute halftimes, which can tolerate faster decompression because they desaturate rapidly, the limiting compartments for the expedited schedule are the 60-minute and 120-minute halftimes, which require approximately 48 hours to safely desaturate from the 4.345 ATA nitrogen loading at 45 meters to the surface, based on the Bühlmann ZH-L16C algorithm with gradient factors of 50/90 — more aggressive (less conservative) than the standard Atlantis gradient factors of 30/85, reflecting the acceptance of increased DCS risk in the emergency context), and by eliminating the 24-hour hold at 10 meters (the longest single decompression stop in the standard schedule), compressing it to a 6-hour hold with aggressive oxygen breathing (25-minute oxygen periods with 5-minute air breaks, repeated continuously for the full 6 hours, accumulating a total UPTD exposure of approximately 400 — within the tolerable range for a single exposure, as established by the Lambertsen oxygen tolerance data); (3) EMERGENCY EVACUATION WITH UNCONTROLLED ASCENT — the worst-case scenario in which the pod is catastrophically compromised (a hull breach that cannot be isolated and that is flooding ALL compartments simultaneously, a fire that has destroyed the pressure boundary integrity, or a structural failure of the anchoring system that causes the pod to tumble down the Monterey Canyon slope, suffering multiple breaches and eventually imploding as it descends below the LBFRP-001 composite's crush depth of approximately 120 meters, where the differential pressure of 11 ATA exceeds the composite's buckling strength of approximately 8 MPa, as determined by the finite element buckling analysis described in the parent document's Section 5) and the crew MUST exit the pod immediately, accepting the near-certainty of fatal decompression sickness as the price of avoiding immediate death by drowning or crushing, a scenario that the Atlantis protocol refers to as the "last resort ascent" and that is executed by donning the emergency immersion suit (a one-piece garment fabricated from 5-millimeter-thick neoprene foam with an integrated hood, gloves, and booties, providing approximately 2 hours of thermal protection in 10°C water — the Monterey site's ambient temperature — before the onset of hypothermia, which at a core temperature of 35°C produces confusion and impaired judgment, at 33°C produces loss of dexterity and the inability to operate the surface signaling devices, and at 30°C produces loss of consciousness and, shortly thereafter, cardiac arrest, a progression that defines the 2-hour survival window for the last-resort ascent), the emergency breathing apparatus (a 0.5-liter constant-volume pure-oxygen rebreather — a simplified closed-circuit unit that recirculates the diver's exhaled gas through a CO₂ scrubber canister while adding oxygen from a 1-liter cylinder at a constant mass flow rate of 0.8 liters per minute (STP), independent of the ambient pressure, meaning that the same oxygen flow rate that sustains the diver at 5.5 ATA will sustain them at 1.0 ATA — a design feature that eliminates the need for the diver to adjust the oxygen flow during the ascent, a task that would be physically impossible for a diver who is simultaneously coping with the pain of decompression sickness, the disorientation of the rapid pressure change, and the psychological terror of the emergency ascent, and that the Atlantis rebreather achieves through a fixed-orifice oxygen metering valve that is calibrated for the diver's resting metabolic oxygen consumption — approximately 0.5 liters per minute — with a 60 percent safety margin to account for the increased consumption during the physical exertion of the ascent), and the personal locator beacon (a 406 MHz COSPAS-SARSAT satellite emergency position-indicating radio beacon, which transmits a distress signal with the diver's GPS coordinates (obtained from the beacon's integrated GPS receiver, which acquires a position fix within approximately 30 seconds of reaching the surface) to the international search and rescue satellite system, which relays the distress signal to the nearest Rescue Coordination Center — for the Monterey site, the US Coast Guard Sector San Francisco, which maintains a 24-hour watch and can launch an MH-60 Jayhawk helicopter from Coast Guard Air Station San Francisco within approximately 30 minutes of receiving the distress alert, the helicopter's transit time to the Monterey deployment site being approximately 25 minutes at the MH-60's cruising speed of 140 knots, for a total response time of approximately 55 minutes from the diver's surfacing to the helicopter's arrival — well within the 2-hour hypothermia survival window for the neoprene immersion suit, a response time that the Coast Guard has confirmed through a formal letter of agreement with the Atlantis program, which commits the Coast Guard to treat an Atlantis emergency ascent as a "Priority 1 — Distress" search and rescue case, the highest priority, which authorizes the launch of all available assets — the helicopter, the surface cutter (the Coast Guard Cutter Pike, a 87-foot coastal patrol boat homeported in Monterey, approximately 2 hours from the deployment site at its maximum speed of 25 knots), and the coordination of any commercial vessels in the vicinity through the Automated Mutual-Assistance Vessel Rescue (AMVER) system, which maintains a real-time database of the positions and capabilities of participating merchant vessels worldwide and which can direct the nearest AMVER vessel to the rescue scene — typically the support vessel of the Atlantis program itself, but in a scenario where the Charlemagne fleet has been destroyed by the crustal displacement event, the nearest AMVER vessel could be any commercial ship transiting the California coast, and the Coast Guard's AMVER coordination capability ensures that a surface recovery platform is available even in the catastrophic scenario where all Atlantis program assets have been lost.

The rapid ascent at 1.5 meters per second — the maximum safe ascent rate for an emergency swimming ascent (ESA) by a diver without decompression obligation, as established by the US Navy's submarine escape training program, which has trained thousands of submariners in the ESA technique at the Submarine Escape Training Tower in Groton, Connecticut, and which specifies 1.5 m/s as the rate at which the diver can safely exhale continuously throughout the ascent (to prevent pulmonary barotrauma from the expanding gas in the lungs, which at 5.5 ATA would expand by a factor of 5.5 if the diver held their breath — producing a lung volume increase from the normal 6 liters to 33 liters, far exceeding the lungs' maximum distensible volume of approximately 10 to 12 liters and inevitably causing alveolar rupture, pneumothorax, and arterial gas embolism, a sequence that is universally fatal without immediate recompression, and that the ESA technique prevents by the simple expedient of continuous exhalation — the diver hums, whistles, or simply exhales a steady stream of bubbles throughout the ascent, keeping the airway open and the lung volume within the physiological range, a technique that is counterintuitive — the diver's instinct is to hold their breath as they ascend through the dark, cold, terrifying water column, and the ESA training specifically overrides that instinct through repeated practice in the Groton training tower, which the Atlantis crew members complete during Week 9 of the 12-week qualification program — with the result that all four Atlantis crew members for each deployment site are certified in the ESA technique, a certification that the Atlantis medical director describes as "the last line of defense — if everything else has failed, if the pod is flooding and the decompression chamber is inaccessible and the surface is 45 meters above you, you exhale continuously and you swim for your life, and the training that you received in Groton, in that steel tower filled with warm, chlorinated water, under the supervision of the Navy's submarine escape instructors, is what will make the difference between your lungs tearing open at 30 meters and your head breaking the surface, alive, at 0 meters, one and a half meters per second of bubble-filled darkness later, the longest 30 seconds of your life, after which everything — the decompression sickness, the hypothermia, the long recovery in the hyperbaric chamber — is secondary to the fact that you are breathing air, surface air, the air that you have not breathed in 90 days, and you are alive."
