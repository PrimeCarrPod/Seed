## 1.0 THE MONTEREY CFD MODEL: DOMAIN, MESH, BOUNDARY CONDITIONS, AND VALIDATION AGAINST THE MOJAVE PHYSICAL MODEL

The computational fluid dynamics simulations of the wave passage at the Monterey Canyon site represent the most computationally intensive component of the Atlantis design process, consuming approximately 2.4 million core-hours on the Aegis C high-performance computing cluster (a 1,024-node Linux cluster, each node equipped with dual 64-core AMD EPYC processors and 512 gigabytes of RAM, interconnected by a Mellanox InfiniBand HDR network with a bandwidth of 200 gigabits per second, a configuration that was assembled specifically for the Atlantis CFD program at a capital cost of approximately $4.2 million, an investment that the ZIRCONIA analysis has justified by the cost avoidance that the CFD simulations enable — without the CFD capability, the Atlantis design team would have had to rely on the physical model tests at Mojave (which are limited in scope, because the 1:50 scale of the physical model cannot capture the full complexity of the Monterey Canyon bathymetry, and which, if they were the sole source of the wave load predictions, would introduce an uncertainty of approximately ±40 to ±60 percent in the dynamic pressure estimates, an uncertainty that would force the design to use much larger safety factors — factors of 10 to 20 instead of the 1.5 to 2.0 that the CFD-validated predictions permit — and that, per the ZIRCONIA analysis, would increase the pod's fabrication cost by approximately 35 to 50 percent, from $2.24 million to approximately $3.0 to $3.4 million per pod, a cost increase that, multiplied by the ten pods of the demonstration communities, would add $7.6 to $11.6 million to the Atlantis program's total cost, an amount that exceeds the $4.2 million investment in the HPC cluster by a factor of 2 to 3, confirming the cost-effectiveness of the CFD approach, a confirmation that the Aegis C program management used to secure the funding for the cluster from the Carrington Storm Motors capital allocation committee, a committee that is responsible for approving all investments exceeding $1 million and that, in the case of the HPC cluster, was persuaded by the ZIRCONIA analysis and by the testimony of the Atlantis CFD team — who presented the results of the validation study that compared the CFD predictions of the wave forces on a 1:50 scale model of the pod to the forces measured in the Mojave physical model, a comparison that showed an agreement of ±8 percent for the dynamic pressure and ±5 percent for the mean pressure, an agreement that, per the ASME V&V 20 standard, qualifies the CFD model as "validated for the intended use" — a designation that gives the Atlantis design team the confidence to use the CFD predictions as the primary basis for the structural design, with the physical model results serving as the validation data set, not as the design data set, a reversal of the conventional approach (which would use the physical model for the design and the CFD for the validation) that reflects the greater fidelity of the full-scale CFD simulation relative to the scaled physical model, because the CFD simulation can represent the actual Reynolds number of the full-scale flow (Re ≈ 10^7 to 10^9), whereas the physical model, constrained by the Froude similarity, operates at a Reynolds number that is approximately 350 times smaller (Re_model ≈ 3 × 10^4 to 3 × 10^6), a discrepancy that introduces scale effects — the differences between the model-scale and the full-scale flow that arise because the viscous forces (which depend on the Reynolds number) are not scaled correctly, and that, for the Atlantis application, primarily affect the boundary layer thickness, the turbulence intensity, and the sediment transport dynamics, all of which the CFD simulation captures correctly (because the CFD solves the full-scale Navier-Stokes equations, with the turbulence model calibrated to the full-scale conditions, and with the sediment transport model validated against the field data from the Monterey Canyon turbidity currents that MBARI has observed and measured using the MARS observatory's instrumentation, providing a validation data set that the physical model, by its nature, cannot access — the physical model uses a simplified geometry (a flat-bottomed flume with a uniform slope, representing an idealized canyon) and a simplified sediment (uniform glass beads, representing an idealized, cohesionless sediment), whereas the CFD simulation uses the actual Monterey Canyon bathymetry (extracted from the MBARI AUV surveys, with a spatial resolution of 1 meter in the region of the pod's deployment) and the actual sediment properties (the particle size distribution, the mineralogy, and the rheology that were measured during the Charlemagne survey cruises and that are described in PART-010 and PART-014), a fidelity that the physical model cannot match and that makes the CFD simulation the superior basis for the design load predictions, a superiority that the Atlantis CFD team has documented in a 120-page validation report (Aegis C-CFD-VAL-077-017) that the Aegis C technical review board has reviewed and that the Atlantis design team uses as the reference for all of the load predictions that inform the pod's structural design, the anchoring system design, and the turbidite defense design, a report that this PART-017 summarizes and that the reader who requires the full detail — the mesh convergence study (which demonstrated that the predicted forces on the pod change by less than 2 percent when the mesh resolution is increased from 5 million cells to 20 million cells, indicating that the 10-million-cell mesh that was used for the production simulations is within the asymptotic range of convergence, a critical finding because an unconverged mesh would produce mesh-dependent results that could not be trusted, regardless of how well the simulation otherwise matched the validation data, a pitfall that the Atlantis CFD team avoided by following the systematic mesh-refinement procedure that is specified by the ASME V&V 20 standard and that is standard practice in the aerospace CFD community, a community whose standards the Atlantis CFD team — which includes two engineers with prior experience at NASA's Ames Research Center, where they worked on the CFD simulations of the Space Launch System's launch environment, a simulation that involved Mach numbers from 0 to 5, Reynolds numbers from 10^6 to 10^8, and complex geometries that included the launch tower, the flame trench, and the vehicle itself, a simulation that was validated against the wind-tunnel tests at the Ames Unitary Plan Wind Tunnel and that, despite the enormous complexity, achieved an agreement of ±10 percent for the surface pressures, a validation standard that the Atlantis CFD team has applied, with similar success, to the Monterey Canyon simulations, demonstrating that the CFD methodology that was developed for the space program — and that has been refined over decades of application to the most demanding fluid dynamics problems in aerospace, automotive, and marine engineering — is adequate for the Atlantis application, provided that it is applied with the rigor, the validation, and the expertise that the Atlantis CFD team possesses, a possession that the Aegis C program has invested in, through the recruitment of the NASA-experienced CFD engineers and through the acquisition of the HPC cluster, an investment that has paid for itself many times over, in the form of the validated, high-confidence load predictions that underpin the Atlantis pod's structural design, predictions that, without the CFD capability, would be uncertain by a factor of 2 to 3, and that, with the CFD capability, are uncertain by only ±15 to ±25 percent, a reduction that translates directly into the safety margins, the material costs, and — ultimately — the survival probability of the Atlantis communities, a probability that the CFD simulations, by their fidelity and their validation, have helped to maximize, within the constraints of the available time, budget, and computational resources, a maximization that is the purpose of the Atlantis design process and that this PART-017, by documenting the CFD methodology, the validation, and the results, contributes to, as one component of the comprehensive engineering documentation that the Aegis C program requires and that this Deep Expansion series provides.
