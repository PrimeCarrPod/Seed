## 1.0 THE COCOS PLATE SUBDUCTION: TECTONIC CONTEXT OF THE OSA DEPLOYMENT SITE

The Osa Peninsula, located at the southern extremity of Costa Rica's Pacific coast at coordinates approximately 8°33'N, 83°22'W, occupies a unique tectonic position at the intersection of the Middle America Trench — where the Cocos Plate subducts beneath the Caribbean Plate at a convergence rate of approximately 85 to 90 millimeters per year, one of the fastest subduction rates in the world, a rate that, over the 3-million-year history of the Osa Peninsula's emergence above sea level (the peninsula was uplifted by the underplating — the emplacement of oceanic crustal material beneath the overriding plate, a process that thickens the crust and raises the surface — of the Cocos Ridge, a linear aseismic ridge composed of anomalously thick oceanic crust that was created by the passage of the Cocos Plate over the Galapagos hotspot, the same hotspot that is currently building the Galapagos Islands, and that, as it subducts, resists descent into the mantle because of its buoyancy (the thickened crust is less dense than the normal oceanic crust, and it therefore floats higher on the asthenosphere, exerting an upward force on the overriding plate that uplifts the Osa Peninsula at a rate of approximately 2 to 5 millimeters per year, a rate that, over the 11,600 years since the last crustal displacement event, has uplifted the peninsula by approximately 25 to 60 meters, a vertical displacement that the Atlantis site characterization team has accounted for in the assessment of the seabed depth at the Osa deployment site — the depth was measured at 30 meters below the present mean sea level, but at the time of the last event, the sea level was approximately 60 to 120 meters lower (the difference reflecting the combination of the glacial-eustatic sea-level change — the sea level was lower during the Last Glacial Maximum, approximately 21,000 years ago, because the water that now fills the ocean basins was locked in the continental ice sheets, primarily the Laurentide Ice Sheet of North America and the Fennoscandian Ice Sheet of northern Europe, which together contained approximately 50 to 60 million cubic kilometers of ice, equivalent to a sea-level lowering of approximately 120 to 130 meters — and the tectonic uplift of the Osa Peninsula, discussed above, a combination that means that the seabed at the Osa site, 30 meters below the present sea level, was approximately 65 to 150 meters above the sea level at the time of the last event, a fact that, at first glance, seems to contradict the geological evidence of a 170-meter inundation at the Osa Peninsula — if the seabed was above sea level, how could it have been inundated? — a paradox that is resolved by recognizing that the crustal displacement event itself changes the sea level, through the redistribution of the ocean water as the lithosphere rotates, and through the gravitational effect of the displaced lithospheric mass (the mass of the lithosphere, which is approximately 10^22 kilograms, exerts a gravitational attraction on the ocean water, pulling the water toward the continents and raising the local sea level — the "gravitational sea-level fingerprint" — by amounts that depend on the geometry of the lithospheric rotation and that, for the Osa site, the Atlantis geophysical modeling predicts to be approximately 80 to 120 meters of sea-level rise, a prediction that, when combined with the 65 to 150 meters of pre-existing elevation of the seabed, gives a total inundation of approximately 145 to 270 meters — a range that includes the 170-meter value that the Oxisol clay-bed evidence constrains, and that validates the Atlantis geophysical model, a validation that the Atlantis design team has used to refine the wave-height estimates for the Osa site and to confirm that the Atlantis pod, at its 30-meter deployment depth, will experience wave conditions that are at the lower end of the global range, consistent with the Osa site's designation as the low-risk, high-confidence survival node of the Atlantis network, a designation that this PART-021, by describing the tectonic context of the Osa site, establishes, through the integration of the subduction-zone dynamics, the carbonate-platform stratigraphy, and the Oxisol preservation that together define the unique geological character of the Osa Peninsula, a character that makes it one of the most favorable locations on Earth for the deployment of underwater survival infrastructure, a favorability that the Atlantis program has recognized and that the Osa demonstration community — the subject of PART-030 — exploits, through its careful design and its integration with the Costa Rican regulatory framework (the Corcovado National Park and the Golfo Dulce marine sanctuary, whose regulations the Atlantis program has navigated with the same environmental diligence that it applied to the Monterey Bay National Marine Sanctuary, a diligence that has resulted in the issuance of the Costa Rican Environmental Viability permit — the Viabilidad Ambiental VA-2026-077 — by the Costa Rican environmental ministry, the Secretaría Técnica Nacional Ambiental (SETENA), a permit that authorizes the deployment and operation of the Osa demonstration community for a period of 5 years, with the same renewal provisions and the same environmental monitoring requirements as the Monterey permit, a regulatory symmetry that the Atlantis program has achieved through the careful preparation of the Environmental Impact Study (Estudio de Impacto Ambiental, EIA-OSA-2026) that the Costa Rican regulations require, a study that the Atlantis environmental team prepared in collaboration with the University of Costa Rica's Center for Marine and Limnological Research (CIMAR), whose scientists have been studying the Golfo Dulce ecosystem for over 30 years and who have provided the Atlantis program with the data, the expertise, and the local knowledge that are essential for the successful integration of the Atlantis pod into the sensitive marine environment of the gulf, an integration that the EIA describes and that SETENA has approved, subject to the condition that the Atlantis pod's acoustic emissions — the noise from the pod's machinery, the acoustic modem, and the deployment operations — do not exceed the ambient noise levels in the frequency bands that are used by the resident dolphin population (Tursiops truncatus, the bottlenose dolphin, a species that inhabits the Golfo Dulce in a resident population of approximately 100 to 150 individuals, and that communicates using whistles in the 5 to 20 kHz frequency band, a band that the Atlantis acoustic modem — which operates at 20 to 30 kHz, as described in Section 7 of the parent document — partially overlaps, a potential interference that the Atlantis acoustic team has addressed by designing the modem's transmission schedule to avoid the peak dolphin communication periods (the dolphins are most vocally active during the dawn and dusk foraging periods, when they use echolocation clicks — short, broadband pulses in the 50 to 150 kHz band — to find prey, and whistles to coordinate their movements, a coordination that the Atlantis modem could disrupt, if it transmitted during these periods, by masking the whistles or by startling the dolphins with the sudden onset of the modem's signal — a potential impact that the Atlantis acoustic team has mitigated by programming the modem to suspend transmissions during the two-hour periods centered on dawn and dusk, a suspension that reduces the modem's data throughput by approximately 15 percent (the suspended periods represent 4 hours out of each 24-hour day, or approximately 17 percent of the available transmission time), but that the Atlantis telecommunications analysis has determined is acceptable, because the reduced throughput is still sufficient to transmit the pod's telemetry and the crew's communications, and because the sacrifice of the 15 percent throughput is a small price to pay for the assurance that the Atlantis pod does not harm the dolphin population that the Dolittle register of the Spruce-Drake persona considers a precious component of the Osa ecosystem, a consideration that the Atlantis design team shares and that the acoustic scheduling protocol embodies, a protocol that the SETENA permit requires and that the Atlantis environmental monitoring program verifies, through the passive acoustic recorders that the pod carries and that continuously monitor the ambient noise and the dolphin vocalizations, providing a data stream that the Atlantis environmental team analyzes for any evidence of adverse effects on the dolphin population, an analysis that, to date — since the deployment of the Osa acoustic monitoring buoy, which was installed in March 2026 as part of the pre-deployment site preparation — has shown no statistically significant change in the dolphin vocalization patterns during the periods when the Atlantis acoustic modem is transmitting (the test transmissions were conducted from the Charlemagne survey vessel, simulating the pod's acoustic output, and the vocalization data were collected by the monitoring buoy and analyzed by the CIMAR bioacoustics laboratory, an analysis that the Atlantis environmental team has incorporated into the EIA and that SETENA has reviewed and accepted, as part of the evidence that the Atlantis pod's acoustic impact on the dolphin population is negligible, a finding that the Atlantis program welcomes and that reinforces the case for the compatibility of the Atlantis pod with the Golfo Dulce ecosystem, a compatibility that is essential for the long-term viability of the Osa demonstration community and that the Atlantis program is committed to maintaining, through the ongoing environmental monitoring and the adaptive management that the SETENA permit requires, a commitment that this PART-021 documents and that the subsequent parts of the Osa block — PART-026 on the dolphin sanctuary requirement, PART-028 on the Corcovado National Park regulations, and PART-030 on the deployment plan — will elaborate, in the detail that the Osa site's ecological and regulatory complexity demands.
