## 1.0 THE MACH REGIME OF CRUSTAL DISPLACEMENT WAVES: WHEN OCEAN WAVES EXCEED THE SPEED OF SOUND IN WATER

The concept of a Mach number — the ratio of a flow velocity to the local speed of sound in the fluid, Ma = u/c_s where c_s is the acoustic velocity, approximately 1,500 meters per second in seawater at the temperatures and pressures of the continental shelf (the speed of sound in seawater increases with increasing temperature, increasing salinity, and increasing pressure, with the dependence on pressure being approximately linear at 0.016 meters per second per meter of depth, so that at the Atlantis pod's depth of 45 meters, the speed of sound is approximately 1,500 + 0.016×45 ≈ 1,501 meters per second, a negligible correction for most purposes, but for the precise arrival-time calculations of the acoustic positioning system that guides the pod during its descent — a system that measures the time of flight of acoustic pulses between the pod and an array of seabed transponders, trilaterating the pod's position from the measured ranges to within approximately 0.1 meters, a precision that is essential for the pod's alignment with the pre-installed helical piles — the pressure correction is applied, along with corrections for temperature (derived from the CTD profile measured during the site survey) and salinity (from the same CTD cast), yielding a sound-speed profile that the acoustic positioning system's firmware uses to compute the range from the measured travel time, a computation that, if it ignored the sound-speed variations, would produce position errors of up to several meters, enough to prevent the pod's lifting lugs from engaging correctly with the pile anchor points, a misalignment that could require the pod to be lifted and repositioned — a costly and time-consuming operation that the precision of the acoustic system is designed to avoid — and that, for the purposes of the Mach number analysis, is typically taken as a constant 1,500 meters per second, a simplification that is justified because the flow velocities of interest — the wave orbital velocities at the surface, which range from approximately 100 meters per second (for the open-ocean wave, far from the shoaling amplification that increases the velocity near the coast) to approximately 200 meters per second (for the shoaled wave at the California continental shelf) — are at most approximately 0.13 Mach (200/1,500), well below the transonic regime (Ma ≈ 0.8 to 1.2) where compressibility effects become significant and shock waves can form, meaning that the megatsunami wave, despite its enormous height and its devastating destructive power, is a subsonic flow from the perspective of compressible fluid dynamics, a classification that has important implications for the Atlantis pod design because it means that the flow can be treated as incompressible — the density of the water does not change significantly in response to the pressure variations associated with the wave (the fractional density change is approximately Δρ/ρ ≈ Ma² ≈ (0.13)² ≈ 0.017, or 1.7 percent, at the surface, and is even smaller at the pod's depth, where the orbital velocity is attenuated by 85 to 95 percent, reducing the Mach number to Ma_depth ≈ 0.007 to 0.020 and the density change to less than 0.04 percent, a value that is completely negligible and that justifies the incompressible-flow assumption that the Atlantis CFD simulations employ, an assumption that simplifies the Navier-Stokes equations — eliminating the energy equation, which couples the velocity and pressure fields to the temperature field through the thermodynamic equation of state, and reducing the computational cost of the simulations by a factor of approximately 3 to 5 relative to a compressible-flow simulation of the same resolution and duration — without introducing any significant error in the predicted forces on the pod, a simplification that the Atlantis design team has validated by performing a limited set of compressible-flow simulations (using the OpenFOAM compressibleInterFoam solver, which solves the compressible Navier-Stokes equations with a volume-of-fluid method for the free surface, capturing the effects of density variations on the flow) for a subset of the wave conditions and comparing the predicted pressures on the pod's hull to the incompressible predictions, a comparison that showed differences of less than 2 percent — within the uncertainty of the turbulence model and the wave parameters — confirming that the incompressible approximation is adequate for the Atlantis application and that the additional computational expense of the compressible simulations (which require smaller time steps to resolve the acoustic waves — pressure disturbances that propagate at the speed of sound — and which therefore increase the computational time by a factor of 10 to 20 relative to the incompressible simulations, because the time step must satisfy the acoustic Courant condition Δt < Δx/c_s, where Δx is the grid spacing, typically 0.1 to 0.5 meters near the pod's hull, giving Δt < 0.1/1,500 ≈ 7 × 10^-5 seconds, compared to the convective Courant condition Δt < Δx/u ≈ 0.1/20 ≈ 5 × 10^-3 seconds for the incompressible simulation, a factor of 70 difference that, over the 24-hour simulated duration of the wave passage, translates to approximately 1.2 × 10^9 time steps for the compressible simulation versus approximately 1.7 × 10^7 time steps for the incompressible simulation, a factor of 70 in computational cost that makes the compressible simulation impractical for routine design iterations — although the compressible simulations that were performed, on a reduced-resolution grid and for a shorter duration, served the valuable purpose of confirming that the incompressible assumption does not introduce systematic errors into the load predictions, a confirmation that gives the Atlantis design team high confidence in the CFD-based predictions of the wave forces on the pod, confidence that is reflected in the relatively modest aerodynamic — rather, hydrodynamic — uncertainty factors that the design applies to the CFD results, factors of 1.15 to 1.25 for the dynamic pressure and 1.05 to 1.10 for the mean pressure, reflecting the high fidelity of the CFD simulations and the validation against the physical model tests at Mojave, a validation that reduces the epistemic uncertainty — the uncertainty that arises from imperfect knowledge of the physical processes, as opposed to the aleatory uncertainty that arises from the inherent variability of the system — to levels that are acceptable for the safety-critical Atlantis application, a demonstration of "validation credibility" that the Aegis C technical review board has reviewed and endorsed as meeting the program's standards for computational simulation-based design, standards that are modeled on the ASME V&V 20-2009 standard for verification and validation in computational fluid dynamics and heat transfer, a standard that the Atlantis CFD team — which includes two PhD-level computational fluid dynamicists with extensive experience in the aerospace and offshore industries, where CFD-based design is routine and where the standards for validation are well-established — has followed throughout the Atlantis design process, ensuring that the CFD predictions that underpin the pod's structural design, anchoring system design, and survivability assessment are not merely plausible but are demonstrably accurate, within the quantified uncertainty bounds, a level of rigor that is unusual in the marine engineering industry — where CFD is often used qualitatively, to compare design alternatives rather than to predict absolute loads — but that the safety-critical nature of the Atlantis application demands, and that the Atlantis design team has delivered.

However, while the water itself remains subsonic — the wave orbital velocity never approaches the speed of sound in water — the ATMOSPHERIC effects of the crustal displacement wave ARE supersonic, a distinction that, while not directly relevant to the underwater pod design (since the pod is isolated from the atmosphere by 45 meters of water), is important for understanding the overall event environment and for confirming that the geological evidence of past events — which includes indicators of supersonic atmospheric phenomena, such as the deposition of wind-transported sediment in patterns that require wind velocities exceeding 100 meters per second, far above the maximum wind speeds of even the most intense hurricanes (Hurricane Patricia in 2015 had maximum sustained winds of 95 meters per second, the highest reliably measured wind speed in any tropical cyclone, and even the most intense tornadoes — the EF5 tornadoes that occur in the central United States — have wind speeds that are estimated to be at most 135 meters per second, so the geological evidence of 200 to 300 meters per second wind speeds — preserved in the size distribution of wind-blown particles, the orientation of toppled trees (which, in some fossil forests associated with past displacement events, are uniformly oriented in a direction that is consistent with winds from a specific direction, rather than being randomly oriented as would be expected from hurricane-force winds, which change direction as the storm passes), and the erosion features on exposed rock surfaces (which show striations and polishing that are characteristic of high-velocity particle-laden flow, similar to the features that are observed in wind tunnels at supersonic speeds and that have been reproduced in the Mojave facility's supersonic wind tunnel, where compressed air is accelerated through a converging-diverging nozzle to velocities exceeding Mach 1, and where the erosion patterns on test specimens — granite, sandstone, and the LBFRP-001 composite — match the patterns observed in the geological record, providing experimental confirmation of the wind-speed estimates derived from that record) — is consistent with the predictions of the atmospheric response to crustal displacement, a response that involves the displacement of the atmosphere by the wave itself (the 3,200-meter wave displaces the atmosphere above it, creating a pressure pulse that propagates horizontally at the speed of sound in air, approximately 340 meters per second, and that, when the wave's propagation speed — the speed at which the wave front advances across the ocean, which for the shallow-water wave is approximately 21 meters per second (c = √(gh) for 45 meters depth) at the Atlantis sites, but which, for the deep-ocean wave that has not yet shoaled, is approximately 190 meters per second (√(9.81 × 3,688)), still subsonic — exceeds the local speed of sound in air, creates a shock wave — a discontinuity in the pressure, density, and velocity of the air that propagates ahead of the wave front, a phenomenon that is analogous to the sonic boom produced by a supersonic aircraft but on a vastly larger scale, with the shock wave from a 3,200-meter megatsunami having a pressure amplitude of approximately 10 to 30 kilopascals — comparable to the overpressure from a large conventional explosion at a distance of several hundred meters — and propagating ahead of the wave at the speed of sound, providing a warning — a very short warning, since the wave itself is only 10 to 20 minutes behind the shock wave (for a deep-water wave speed of 190 m/s and a sound speed of 340 m/s, the shock wave travels 340 - 190 = 150 m/s faster than the wave, so for a distance of 100 kilometers between the wave front and the coast, the shock wave arrives approximately 100,000/150 ≈ 670 seconds, or 11 minutes, before the wave — a warning that could, in principle, allow some last-minute evacuation, although for a 3,200-meter wave, evacuation to a sufficient elevation — at least 3,500 meters above sea level — would be impossible for all but the very few people who happen to be near a high mountain and who have access to rapid transportation, a number that is negligibly small compared to the total exposed population, and so the warning, while physically real, is practically useless for the surface population — but for the Atlantis pods, positioned at 45 meters depth where the pressure pulse from the atmospheric shock wave is attenuated by the water column (the acoustic impedance of water — the product of the density and the speed of sound — is approximately 1.5 × 10^6 rayls, compared to approximately 430 rayls for air, a ratio of approximately 3,500, so that only about 0.06 percent of the acoustic energy incident on the water surface from the atmospheric shock wave is transmitted into the water, the rest being reflected back into the atmosphere, an enormous impedance mismatch that effectively isolates the underwater environment from atmospheric acoustic disturbances, a principle that is exploited by submarines — which are essentially immune to atmospheric explosions, however powerful — and
