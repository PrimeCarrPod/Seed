## 1.0 THE SEVEN CANDIDATES: A SYSTEMATIC ELIMINATION OF CALIFORNIA COASTAL SITES

The selection of the Monterey Canyon site from an initial pool of seven candidate locations distributed along the California coast from the Oregon border in the north to the Santa Barbara Channel in the south was conducted over a period of approximately eight months, from September 2025 to April 2026, by the Atlantis Site Selection Committee — a multidisciplinary group comprising two Ph.D. marine geologists (specializing in California margin stratigraphy and submarine canyon geomorphology, respectively, with combined field experience of 47 years of geological mapping, seismic reflection profiling, and sediment coring along the California coast, including extensive work on the USGS California Seafloor Mapping Program, which has produced high-resolution bathymetric, geologic, and habitat maps for the entire California state waters from the shoreline to the 3-nautical-mile limit, and which provided the foundational data set for the Atlantis site evaluation, supplemented by the MBARI Seafloor Mapping Lab's higher-resolution surveys of Monterey Bay, which extend from the shoreline to the base of the continental slope at depths of approximately 3,000 meters, with a spatial resolution of 1 meter in the horizontal and 0.1 meters in the vertical — sufficient to identify individual boulders, rock outcrops, and sedimentary bedforms on the canyon floor, and to assess the slope stability, the sediment thickness, and the anchoring substrate at each candidate location, information that is essential for the pod's foundation design and that, without the MBARI surveys — which were conducted over a period of 15 years, from 2009 to 2024, using the MBARI mapping autonomous underwater vehicle (AUV), a torpedo-shaped robot that flies approximately 20 meters above the seafloor at a speed of 3 knots, emitting a fan of sonar beams that map a swath of the seafloor approximately 300 meters wide, with overlapping swaths producing complete coverage of the survey area, a technological capability that has revolutionized our understanding of submarine canyon morphology and that, fortuitously for the Atlantis program, was brought to full maturity at precisely the time when the Atlantis site selection needed the data it produces, a convergence of technological readiness and programmatic need that the Kiddo register describes as "the synchronicity of preparation" — the phenomenon, observed repeatedly throughout the Aegis C program's history, in which the capabilities that are needed for a particular phase of the program become available just as that phase begins, not through coincidence but through the foresight of the program's planners, who have anticipated the needs and have invested in the necessary technologies years or decades in advance, an investment strategy that the ZIRCONIA economic analysis has validated as highly cost-effective, because the early investment in enabling technologies — the MBARI AUV, the MITgcm ocean model, the OpenFOAM CFD code, the AFP composite fabrication machines — generates returns that far exceed the investment cost, in the form of the data, the simulations, and the hardware that the Atlantis program uses, a return on investment that the ZIRCONIA analysis calculates as approximately 50:1 for the MBARI mapping data alone (the Atlantis program paid nothing for the data — it was collected by MBARI for scientific purposes, over many years, at a cost of approximately $30 million — but the Atlantis program's site selection, which depended critically on that data, enabled the deployment of a $47.8 million program, a leverage factor of approximately 1.6, or, if the data's value is measured by the cost of collecting it specifically for the Atlantis program — which ZIRCONIA estimates would have been approximately $15 million, including the cost of the survey vessel, the AUV operations, and the data processing — the return on the "virtual purchase" of the data is approximately 3.2:1, still a very attractive return, and one that illustrates the value of leveraging existing public-domain data sets for the Atlantis program, a leveraging strategy that the site selection committee employed extensively, accessing not only the MBARI bathymetry but also the USGS seismic-reflection profiles (which image the stratigraphy beneath the seafloor to depths of several hundred meters, revealing the thickness and the structure of the sedimentary layers that will anchor the pod), the NOAA nautical charts (which provide the official depth, bottom type, and navigation hazard information for the deployment site, essential for the safe navigation of the Charlemagne deployment vessel), the California Department of Fish and Wildlife's marine protected area boundaries (which define the areas where deployment is prohibited or restricted, a constraint that eliminated several otherwise-suitable sites, including the Point Lobos State Marine Reserve, which encompasses some of the most geologically attractive canyon benches in the Monterey region but which, as a fully protected reserve, prohibits any installation of structures, a prohibition that the Atlantis team respects — the environmental compliance of the Atlantis program is not merely a legal requirement but a philosophical commitment, consistent with the Dolittle register's advocacy for the protection of marine ecosystems, a commitment that the site selection process honored by excluding all marine protected areas from consideration, regardless of their geological suitability, a constraint that eliminated approximately 60 percent of the California continental shelf from consideration, narrowing the field of potential sites to a manageable number and ensuring that the Atlantis deployment does not harm the marine ecosystems that the pod is designed to coexist with, a coexistence that the pod's minimal environmental footprint supports and that the environmental monitoring program (described in PART-019) will verify), and the California Geological Survey's seismic hazard maps (which provide the expected ground shaking intensity for a range of earthquake scenarios, including the San Gregorio Fault earthquake, the San Andreas Fault earthquake, and the Cascadia Subduction Zone earthquake, the latter being the most powerful — a magnitude 9.0 megathrust event that occurs approximately every 300 to 500 years and that, when it occurs, will generate a local tsunami with a height of 5 to 10 meters at the Monterey site, a tsunami that is negligible compared to the crustal displacement megatsunami but that is a reminder that the California coast is subject to multiple tsunami threats, not just the crustal displacement event, and that the Atlantis pod, by its underwater deployment, is protected from ALL tsunami threats — local, distant, and crustal-displacement — a universal tsunami protection that is a significant advantage of the underwater configuration, an advantage that the site selection committee recognized and that influenced the ranking of the candidates, with sites that are more exposed to local tsunamis (such as the Santa Barbara Channel, which is vulnerable to tsunamis generated by the Pitas Point-Ventura Fault system, a blind thrust fault that underlies the channel and that, per the USGS seismic hazard analysis, is capable of generating a magnitude 7.5 earthquake and an associated tsunami with a height of 5 to 15 meters at the Santa Barbara waterfront, a tsunami that, while small by crustal displacement standards, would be devastating to any surface structure at the Santa Barbara candidate site — the Santa Barbara Harbor, which was considered as a candidate primarily because of its proximity to the UCSB campus and the associated research infrastructure (the Marine Science Institute, the National Center for Ecological Analysis and Synthesis, and the Channel Islands National Marine Sanctuary headquarters, all of which could provide scientific support to the Atlantis community), but which was ultimately eliminated because of the combined threats of the local tsunami, the extensive anchor-dragging from the numerous recreational and commercial vessels that use the harbor (a hazard that could damage the pod during the pre-deployment transport and handling, when it is most vulnerable — floating at the surface, not yet submerged, exposed to collision with other vessels, a hazard that the Moss Landing site avoids because the harbor is smaller, the vessel traffic is lighter, and the pod's dedicated deployment berth, constructed by the Atlantis program at the Moss Landing Marine Laboratories pier, provides a secure exclusion zone around the pod during the final preparation for deployment), and the poor anchoring substrate — the Santa Barbara Channel seafloor is underlain by a thick sequence of unconsolidated Holocene mud, deposited by the Santa Clara River, which drains the Transverse Ranges and which, during the heavy winter rains that characterize the Southern California climate, transports enormous volumes of sediment into the channel, sediment that accumulates as a soft, weak, compressible mud with shear strengths of only 5 to 15 kilopascals in the upper 5 meters, values that are too low to support the pod's foundation loads, even with the load-spreading effect of the eight helical piles, because the mud's low shear strength limits the pullout capacity of each pile to approximately 50 to 100 kilonewtons (compared to the 1,200 kilonewtons per pile that the Monterey site's Purisima Formation sandstone provides), a capacity that, multiplied by eight piles, gives a total pullout capacity of only 400 to 800 kilonewtons — insufficient to resist the predicted lateral load of 200 to 500 kilonewtons with a reasonable factor of safety, a deficiency that eliminated Santa Barbara from the candidate list early in the selection process, an elimination that illustrates the critical importance of the seabed geotechnical properties for the Atlantis pod design and that motivated the detailed geotechnical analysis described in PART-014, an analysis that confirmed the suitability of the Monterey Canyon bench — the Purisima Formation sandstone at the selected site, with its unconfined compressive strength of 15 to 30 MPa, its friction angle of 30 to 35 degrees, and its cohesion intercept of 50 to 100 kilopascals, as measured by the triaxial compression tests on the piston-core samples that the Charlemagne survey vessel collected during the site investigation cruise, provides an anchoring substrate that is essentially ideal for the helical pile system — strong enough to provide the required pullout capacity, cohesive enough to resist the scour around the piles (the erosion of the sediment by the turbidite flow, which, if the sediment were cohesionless — like a clean sand — could excavate scour holes around the piles that would reduce their embedment depth and, consequently, their pullout capacity, a risk that the Purisima sandstone's cohesion largely eliminates, as confirmed by the CFD scour analysis described in PART-015), and laterally extensive enough — the Purisima Formation underlies the entire Monterey Bay region, with a thickness of approximately 500 to 1,000 meters, providing a virtually unlimited volume of competent anchoring material — that the helical piles can be driven to their full design depth of 8 meters without encountering any weaker layer or any obstruction, a uniform, predictable anchoring substrate that, combined with the flat, stable seabed topography, makes the Monterey Canyon bench the ideal location for the Atlantis demonstration community, a conclusion that the site selection matrix — a weighted scoring system that evaluated each candidate against 15 criteria, including geological stability, anchoring substrate quality, oceanographic accessibility, environmental compatibility, regulatory feasibility, proximity to research infrastructure, and the predicted wave and turbidite loads from the crustal displacement event — quantified through the assignment of numerical scores (0 to 10) for each criterion, with the weighting factors reflecting the relative importance of each criterion (geological stability and anchoring substrate, each weighted at 25 percent, reflecting their central role in the pod's survival; environmental compatibility, weighted at 15 percent, reflecting the Atlantis program's commitment to minimizing the ecological impact; the remaining ten criteria sharing the remaining 35 percent weight, each weighted at 2 to 5 percent, a distribution that the Atlantis Site Selection Committee debated extensively — the original proposal weighted the regulatory feasibility criterion at 15 percent, reflecting the concern that the permitting process could delay or derail the Atlantis deployment, but the Committee eventually reduced this weight to 3 percent after the successful negotiation of the Sanctuary Exploration Permit (SEP-2025-077) demonstrated that the regulatory pathway was feasible and that the Monterey Bay National Marine Sanctuary staff were supportive of the Atlantis mission, a support that reflected the sanctuary's research mandate and the recognition that the Atlantis pod, as a research platform, would contribute to the sanctuary's scientific mission, a mutual benefit that the Committee acknowledged by reducing the regulatory weight and increasing the geological weights, a rebalancing that reflected the Committee's evolving understanding of the key risks and opportunities of each site, an evolution that the ZIRCONIA analysis tracked and that demonstrated the value of the iterative, multidisciplinary, evidence-driven site selection process that the Atlantis program employed, a process that is described in detail in this PART-013 and that is, in its rigor and its comprehensiveness, a model for the site selection of all future Atlantis deployments, a model that the Atlantis program will apply, with appropriate modifications, to the selection of sites for the global Atlantis network that will follow the successful demonstration of the Monterey and Osa communities).
