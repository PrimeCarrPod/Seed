## 1.0 THE AIRY WAVE THEORY — LINEAR WAVE KINEMATICS FOR FINITE-AMPLITUDE DEEP-WATER WAVES

The classical Airy wave theory, developed by Sir George Biddell Airy in his 1845 treatise "Tides and Waves" and subsequently refined through the contributions of Stokes, Rayleigh, Lamb, and numerous twentieth-century hydrodynamists, provides the foundational mathematical framework for understanding the attenuation of wave-induced fluid motion with depth beneath a propagating surface gravity wave, a framework that, despite its simplifying assumptions — most critically, the assumptions that the wave amplitude is small compared to both the wavelength and the water depth, that the fluid is inviscid and incompressible, that the flow is irrotational (allowing the velocity to be expressed as the gradient of a scalar velocity potential), and that the free-surface boundary condition can be linearized by neglecting the nonlinear terms that involve products of the wave amplitude and its derivatives — yields predictions for the depth-dependence of the orbital velocity that are in remarkably good agreement with experimental measurements and with the results of fully nonlinear computational fluid dynamics simulations for wave steepness values (the ratio of wave height H to wavelength λ) up to approximately 0.01 to 0.03, a range that encompasses the crustal displacement megatsunami, for which the wave height-to-wavelength ratio, as estimated from the MITgcm simulations and the geological evidence described in PART-001, is approximately 3,200 meters divided by 300,000 meters, or approximately 0.011, placing the Airy theory at the margin of its formal range of validity but still providing a useful first-order approximation of the orbital velocity attenuation that is the physical basis for the Atlantis community's survival strategy, an approximation that is then refined by the Stokes fifth-order theory, which extends the linear Airy solution to include the nonlinear terms that become significant at higher wave steepness and that modify the depth-attenuation function, the wave profile (changing it from the sinusoidal shape predicted by Airy theory to the peaked-crest, flattened-trough shape that is characteristic of nonlinear deep-water waves and that can be observed in wind-generated ocean waves under storm conditions), and the wave celerity — the speed at which the wave form propagates — which increases with wave steepness because the nonlinear terms effectively add a correction to the linear dispersion relation that governs the relationship between wave frequency and wave number, a correction that is proportional to the square of the wave steepness and that, for the megatsunami parameters, increases the wave celerity by approximately 3 to 8 percent relative to the linear prediction, a modest but non-negligible effect that the Atlantis design team has incorporated into the OpenFOAM CFD models to ensure that the predicted arrival times of the wave at the Monterey and Osa sites — and hence the predicted duration of the dynamic loading phase that determines the fatigue cycling discussed in PART-005 — are as accurate as the available computational resources and the uncertainties in the wave generation parameters permit.

The derivation of the Airy wave solution begins with the specification of the governing equations for an inviscid, incompressible, irrotational fluid in a gravitational field, which are the Laplace equation for the velocity potential φ (a scalar function whose gradient gives the fluid velocity vector, such that the velocity components in the x (horizontal, in the direction of wave propagation) and z (vertical, positive upward from the mean sea surface) directions are given by u = ∂φ/∂x and w = ∂φ/∂z, respectively, and which must satisfy ∇²φ = ∂²φ/∂x² + ∂²φ/∂z² = 0 everywhere in the fluid domain, the condition of irrotationality being a consequence of the assumption that the fluid is initially at rest and that the wave motion is generated by conservative forces (gravity and pressure gradients) that do not introduce vorticity into the fluid), the kinematic boundary condition at the seafloor (which requires that the vertical component of the fluid velocity at the seafloor — at z = -h, where h is the water depth, assumed constant in the simplest derivation — be zero, since the fluid cannot penetrate the solid bottom, yielding the condition w = ∂φ/∂z = 0 at z = -h, a condition that is exact for an inviscid fluid and that is approximately satisfied for a real fluid, in which the no-slip condition at the seafloor creates a thin boundary layer where the velocity decreases rapidly to zero but the inviscid solution remains valid outside this boundary layer, which for megatsunami parameters has a thickness of approximately 1 to 10 meters, small compared to the water depth of 30 to 45 meters at the Atlantis sites), the kinematic free-surface boundary condition (which requires that a fluid particle on the free surface remain on the free surface — that is, the free surface is a material surface that moves with the fluid — yielding the condition ∂η/∂t + u(∂η/∂x) = w at z = η, where η(x,t) is the elevation of the free surface above the mean sea level, and where the nonlinear term u(∂η/∂x) represents the horizontal advection of the surface elevation by the horizontal velocity, a term that is neglected in the linear Airy theory but retained in the Stokes expansion), and the dynamic free-surface boundary condition (which requires that the pressure at the free surface equal the atmospheric pressure — assumed constant, which is a reasonable approximation for the crustal displacement scenario because the atmospheric pressure variations associated with the wave are minuscule compared to the hydrostatic pressure variations — and which, through the Bernoulli equation for unsteady, irrotational flow, yields the condition ∂φ/∂t + (1/2)(u² + w²) + gη = 0 at z = η, where g is the gravitational acceleration and where the term (1/2)(u² + w²) is the kinetic energy per unit mass, a term that is nonlinear (quadratic in the velocity components) and that is therefore neglected in the linear Airy theory, a neglect that is justified when the wave amplitude is small — specifically, when the Froude number based on the wave amplitude, Fr = u/√(gη), is much less than unity, which for the megatsunami, with surface orbital velocities of approximately 100 to 200 meters per second (estimated from the conservation of wave energy flux, which requires that the product of the wave energy density per unit area, (1/8)ρgH², and the wave group velocity c_g — the speed at which wave energy propagates — remain constant as the wave shoals, implying that as the group velocity decreases in shallow water, the wave height must increase to maintain constant energy flux, and this increased wave height implies increased orbital velocities, which are proportional to the wave height divided by the wave period), the Froude number is approximately 0.1 to 0.3, indicating that the nonlinear terms are not negligible and that the Stokes expansion is necessary for quantitative accuracy, although the linear Airy theory still provides the correct functional form of the depth-attenuation relationship, which is an exponential decay governed by the wave number k, a parameter that is determined by the wave frequency (or equivalently, the wave period) through the linear dispersion relation ω² = gk·tanh(kh), where ω = 2π/T is the angular frequency, T is the wave period, and the hyperbolic tangent function tanh(kh) accounts for the effect of finite water depth on the wave dispersion, approaching unity when the water depth is large compared to the wavelength (the deep-water limit, kh >> 1) and approaching kh when the water depth is small compared to the wavelength (the shallow-water limit, kh << 1), with the crustal displacement megatsunami, having a wavelength of approximately 300 kilometers and a water depth at the Atlantis sites of 30 to 45 meters, satisfying kh ≈ 0.0006 to 0.0009 for the Monterey site (45 meters depth) and kh ≈ 0.0004 to 0.0006 for the Osa site (30 meters depth), values that are firmly in the shallow-water regime where tanh(kh) ≈ kh, and where the wave celerity is approximately independent of the wavelength — all wavelength components travel at approximately the same speed, c = √(gh), which for h = 45 meters is approximately 21 meters per second, or 76 kilometers per hour — a property that has important consequences for the wave arrival sequence at the Atlantis sites, because the absence of dispersion (the dependence of wave speed on wavelength) means that the wave energy arrives as a relatively coherent pulse rather than being dispersed over time, concentrating the dynamic loading into a shorter interval and potentially increasing the peak loads on the pod's hull and anchoring system relative to the dispersed-loading case, an effect that the Atlantis design team has accounted for by applying a conservatism factor of 1.5 to the dynamic pressure estimates derived from the linear wave theory, ensuring that the hull's structural margins — already generous at the design hydrostatic pressure of 10.5 MPa versus the predicted dynamic pressure of 33 to 75 kPa — are sufficient to accommodate the uncertainties in the wave prediction without compromising the fundamental safety case that the physics of depth attenuation provides.

The solution of the Laplace equation subject to the linearized boundary conditions described above yields the velocity potential for a sinusoidal wave propagating in the positive x-direction: φ(x,z,t) = (a·g/ω) · [cosh(k(z+h)) / cosh(kh)] · sin(kx - ωt), where a is the wave amplitude (half the wave height, H/2, so that the surface elevation is given by η(x,t) = a·cos(kx - ωt), the familiar sinusoidal wave profile of linear theory), g is the gravitational acceleration, ω is the angular frequency, k is the wave number, h is the water depth, and the hyperbolic cosine ratio [cosh(k(z+h)) / cosh(kh)] represents the depth-dependence of the velocity potential — and hence of the fluid velocity — with the hyperbolic cosine function cosh(k(z+h)) decreasing from cosh(kh) at the surface (z = 0, so that the ratio equals unity, as required by the kinematic surface boundary condition) to cosh(0) = 1 at the seafloor (z = -h, so that the ratio equals 1/cosh(kh), a value that is less than unity for any finite water depth, indicating that the velocity at the seafloor is less than the velocity at the surface, and that approaches zero — the deep-water limit — when kh is large, since cosh(kh) grows exponentially with kh, and approaches unity — the shallow-water limit — when kh is small, since cosh(kh) ≈ 1 for kh << 1, indicating that in shallow water, the horizontal velocity is approximately uniform with depth, a result that is well-known in coastal engineering and that explains why the depth-attenuation of orbital velocity is minimal in the shallow-water regime, a fact that might at first glance appear to undermine the Atlantis community's survival strategy, since if the velocity is uniform with depth, then the pod experiences the same dynamic pressure as a surface structure, and there is no benefit to the underwater location — but this apparent contradiction is resolved by recognizing that the shallow-water approximation (kh << 1) applies to the relationship between the horizontal velocity at depth and the horizontal velocity at the surface for a GIVEN wave height at the surface, but the wave height at the surface, as discussed in PART-001, is itself determined by the shoaling amplification that occurs as the wave enters shallow water, and this amplification is driven by the conservation of wave energy flux, which requires that the wave height increase as the water depth decreases, a relationship that is given by H ∝ h^(-1/4) in the shallow-water limit (derived from the relationships E ∝ H², c_g ∝ √h, and the constancy of the product E·c_g ∝ H²·√h, where E is the wave energy density per unit area), such that as the water depth decreases, the wave height increases, but this increase occurs at the expense of the wave's horizontal extent — the wave becomes shorter and steeper as it shoals, and the orbital velocities, which are proportional to H/T (wave height divided by wave period) in the shallow-water limit, increase correspondingly, so that the pod, at a fixed depth of 45 meters beneath the mean sea surface, experiences orbital velocities that are proportional to the local wave height at the surface ABOVE the pod, a wave height that is substantially less than the wave height at the shoreline (where the water depth approaches zero and the wave height reaches its maximum through the shoaling amplification) because the pod is located 45 meters offshore — in water that, while shallow by open-ocean standards, is deep enough that the shoaling amplification has not yet reached its maximum — a subtlety that the Atlantis design team has analyzed in detail through the OpenFOAM CFD simulations of the Monterey and Osa sites, which account for the full nonlinear interaction between the incident wave, the local bathymetry, and the pod's presence, and which confirm that the orbital velocities at the pod's depth are attenuated by 85 to 95 percent relative to the surface orbital velocities at the same offshore location, an attenuation that is sufficient — when combined with the pod's streamlined teardrop geometry, which reduces the drag coefficient by a factor of approximately 8 relative to a cylindrical geometry as described in the parent document — to reduce the dynamic pressure on the pod to values that are well within the hull's structural capacity, a conclusion that is robust to the uncertainties in the wave parameters because the exponential nature of the depth-attenuation function means that even if the wave height were twice the predicted value (6,400 meters instead of 3,200 meters), the orbital velocity at 45 meters depth would increase by only approximately 40 percent (since the velocity scales as the square root of the energy, and the energy scales as the square of the wave height, so doubling the wave height quadruples the energy but increases the velocity by a factor of 2), a sensitivity that, while significant, does not approach the factor-of-100 safety margin that the pod's hull provides, and that can be further accommodated by increasing the design depth — if the wave height were indeed 6,400 meters, deploying the pods at 90 meters instead of 45 meters would restore the original safety margin, at an additional cost that, per ZIRCONIA's cost-benefit analysis, is estimated to be approximately 15 to 20 percent of the pod fabrication cost (primarily due to the increased pressure rating of the hull laminate and the larger ballast tanks required for the deeper descent), a cost that the Aegis C contingency reserve can accommodate and that would be incorporated into the final deployment plan once the wave height uncertainty is reduced through the ongoing refinement of the MITgcm simulations and the geological site characterization described in PART-013 and PART-023.

The horizontal and vertical components of the fluid velocity, derived from the velocity potential through the relationships u = ∂φ/∂x and w = ∂φ/∂z, are given, for the linear Airy wave, by the expressions: u(x,z,t) = a·ω · [cosh(k(z+h)) / sinh(kh)] · cos(kx - ωt) and w(x,z,t) = a·ω · [sinh(k(z+h)) / sinh(kh)] · sin(kx - ωt), where the hyperbolic sine and cosine functions have their usual definitions — sinh(x) = (e^x - e^(-x))/2, cosh(x) = (e^x + e^(-x))/2 — and where the depth-dependence is now contained in the ratios [cosh(k(z+h)) / sinh(kh)] for the horizontal velocity and [sinh(k(z+h)) / sinh(kh)] for the vertical velocity, ratios that depend on the parameter kh and on the dimensionless depth (z+h)/h, which ranges from 0 at the seafloor to 1 at the surface, and that can be evaluated for the specific values of kh at the Atlantis sites — approximately 0.0006 to 0.0009 for Monterey (45 meters) and approximately 0.0004 to 0.0006 for Osa (30 meters) — to obtain numerical estimates of the velocity attenuation. For kh = 0.001 (a representative value that is within the range of the Atlantis sites), the hyperbolic functions can be approximated by their power-series expansions for small argument: sinh(kh) ≈ kh + (kh)³/6 + ... ≈ kh (since kh is small, the cubic term is negligible), cosh(kh) ≈ 1 + (kh)²/2 + ... ≈ 1 (since (kh)² is on the order of 10^-6, entirely negligible), and the depth-dependent functions become cosh(k(z+h)) ≈ 1 + (k(z+h))²/2 and sinh(k(z+h)) ≈ k(z+h) + (k(z+h))³/6, yielding the approximate velocity attenuation factors: u(z)/u(0) ≈ cosh(k(z+h)) / cosh(kh) ≈ 1 + (k(z+h))²/2 (since cosh(kh) ≈ 1), which for a pod at depth D = 45 meters (z = -45 meters, z+h = 0 meters, so that u(-45)/u(0) ≈ 1, confirming the shallow-water result that the horizontal velocity is approximately uniform with depth), and w(z)/w(0) ≈ sinh(k(z+h)) / sinh(kh) ≈ (z+h)/h (using the small-argument approximations for the hyperbolic sine in both numerator and denominator), which for a pod at depth D = 45 meters (z = -45 meters, z+h = 0) gives w(-45)/w(0) ≈ 0, indicating that the vertical velocity at the seafloor is zero — as required by the bottom boundary condition — and that the vertical velocity decreases linearly with depth from its surface value to zero at the seafloor, a result that is a direct consequence of the shallow-water approximation and that, when combined with the uniform horizontal velocity profile, means that the fluid particles at the seafloor move back and forth horizontally but not vertically, tracing a degenerate elliptical orbit whose vertical axis is zero — essentially, a line — rather than the circular orbit of a deep-water wave or the elliptical orbit of an intermediate-depth wave, a pattern that has been confirmed by the CFD simulations of the Monterey and Osa sites and that has the important practical consequence that the dynamic pressure on the pod's hull, which depends on the square of the total velocity magnitude (u² + w²)^(1/2), is dominated by the horizontal component and is therefore approximately uniform over the pod's height (the pod's vertical dimension of approximately 8 meters is small compared to the water depth of 30 to 45 meters, so that the variation of the horizontal velocity over the pod's height, as given by the hyperbolic cosine depth function, is less than 5 percent, a variation that is negligible for engineering purposes), simplifying the structural analysis of the pod's hull loading and the anchoring system's lateral load distribution, and confirming that the shallow-water approximation, despite its apparent contradiction with the depth-attenuation that is the Atlantis community's primary survival mechanism — a contradiction that is resolved by recognizing that the attenuation is not of the velocity WITHIN the water column but of the velocity AT the pod's offshore location relative to the velocity AT the shoreline, as discussed above — is the appropriate theoretical framework for the Atlantis design, provided that it is applied correctly to the offshore, non-shoaling region where the pods are deployed rather than to the shoreline region where the wave height is maximal.

## 2.0 THE STOKES FIFTH-ORDER WAVE THEORY — NONLINEAR CORRECTIONS TO WAVE KINEMATICS AND THE CREST-HEIGHT ENHANCEMENT

The Stokes wave theory, developed by Sir George Gabriel Stokes in a series of papers published between 1847 and 1880 and subsequently extended to fifth order in the wave steepness parameter ε = ka (the product of the wave number k and the wave amplitude a, which is proportional to the ratio of the wave height to the wavelength, H/λ = (2a)/(2π/k) = ka/π ≈ ε/3, so that ε ≈ π·H/λ ≈ 3.14 × 0.011 ≈ 0.035 for the megatsunami) by the Norwegian hydrodynamicist J. E. Skjelbreia and the American coastal engineer J. A. Hendrickson in their 1960 monograph "Fifth Order Gravity Wave Theory," provides a systematic perturbation expansion of the governing equations in powers of the small parameter ε, with each successive order introducing corrections to the wave profile, the velocity potential, the dispersion relation, and the particle kinematics that become significant as the wave steepness increases, and with the fifth-order solution — which includes terms up to ε⁵ — providing accuracy that is sufficient for engineering applications involving wave steepness values up to approximately 0.1 to 0.15, a range that comfortably encompasses the megatsunami steepness of approximately 0.011, placing the Stokes fifth-order theory well within its domain of validity and providing a high-confidence prediction of the nonlinear modifications to the Airy wave kinematics that the Atlantis design team has incorporated into the structural load calculations, the anchoring system design, and the fatigue analysis. The Stokes expansion represents the velocity potential, the surface elevation, and the wave celerity as power series in ε: φ = ε·φ^(1) + ε²·φ^(2) + ε³·φ^(3) + ε⁴·φ^(4) + ε⁵·φ^(5) + O(ε⁶), where φ^(1) is the linear Airy solution described in the preceding section, φ^(2) is the second-order correction (proportional to the square of the wave amplitude), and so on, with each successive term being determined by substituting the expansion into the governing equations and the boundary conditions — the Laplace equation (which is linear and therefore applies to each term individually), the bottom boundary condition, the kinematic free-surface condition, and the dynamic free-surface condition (which, because they involve products of the unknown variables, introduce nonlinear coupling between terms of different orders) — and solving the resulting hierarchy of linear partial differential equations, a procedure that, while mathematically intricate and requiring careful algebraic manipulation to avoid secular terms — terms that grow without bound as time progresses and that would invalidate the perturbation expansion — has been standardized through the Skjelbreia-Hendrickson formulation and implemented in the computational tools that the Atlantis design team uses for wave kinematics prediction, including the open-source WaveKinematics library and the commercial OrcaFlex software package for offshore structure analysis.

The primary nonlinear corrections that the Stokes fifth-order theory introduces, relative to the linear Airy theory, and that are relevant to the Atlantis pod design, are as follows: first, the wave profile is no longer sinusoidal — the crest is higher and sharper (the crest elevation above mean sea level is increased by a factor that depends on ε², ε⁴, and higher even-order terms, which are positive, meaning that the crest is higher than the linear prediction by an amount that, for ε = 0.035, is approximately 2 to 4 percent of the wave height, or approximately 65 to 130 meters for a 3,200-meter wave, a correction that, while small in relative terms, is large in absolute terms and must be included in the surface wave height specification that the Attenuation calculation uses as its input, since an error of 100 meters in the surface wave height could translate, through the shoaling amplification described in PART-001, to an error of several hundred meters in the predicted wave height at the shoreline, an error that could affect the inundation zone mapping and the placement of the surface relay buoy's anchor point, which must be located landward of the predicted maximum inundation) and the trough is lower and flatter (the trough depression below mean sea level is decreased — made less negative — relative to the linear prediction, by an amount that is also proportional to ε² and ε⁴, so that the wave is not symmetric about the mean sea level, with more of the wave volume above the mean level than below it, a property that has implications for the total volume of water that is transported landward during the inundation phase and that therefore affects the turbidite flow volume and velocity during the recession phase, parameters that the Atlantis turbidite defense architecture — the bubble curtain, the auxetic cladding, and the CNT-polyurea coating — must be designed to accommodate); second, the horizontal particle velocity is increased at the crest and decreased at the trough, with the second-order correction being proportional to ε and having the form of a "Stokes drift" — a net forward (wave-propagation-direction) transport of fluid, superimposed on the oscillatory orbital motion, that results from the fact that the particle velocity is slightly greater under the crest (where the particle is moving forward) than under the trough (where the particle is moving backward), so that over a complete wave cycle, the particle does not return to its starting position but is displaced forward by a small amount, a drift velocity that, for the megatsunami, is approximately 0.5 to 2 meters per second (proportional to ε² times the wave celerity, or approximately (0.035)² × 21 meters per second ≈ 0.026 meters per second in the shallow-water approximation, but the shallow-water approximation underestimates the Stokes drift because the drift velocity is proportional to the square of the orbital velocity, and the orbital velocity — as discussed above — is not uniform with depth in the full Stokes solution, being larger near the surface and smaller near the bottom, so that the vertically averaged drift velocity is somewhat larger than the shallow-water estimate, reaching values of 0.1 to 0.3 meters per second for the megatsunami parameters, a net current that, while small, must be accounted for in the anchoring system design because it imposes a sustained — not oscillatory — lateral load on the pod for the duration of the wave passage, a load that is additional to the oscillatory dynamic pressure load and that, integrated over the 10 to 30 day duration of the event, could contribute to the anchoring system's creep deformation or to the seabed soil's consolidation settlement if not adequately resisted); and third, the dispersion relation — the relationship between the wave frequency ω and the wave number k — is modified by the nonlinear terms, with the corrected relation being ω² = gk·tanh(kh) · [1 + ε²·C_1 + ε⁴·C_2 + ...] where C_1 and C_2 are numerical coefficients that depend on the dimensionless water depth kh and that have been tabulated by Skjelbreia and Hendrickson for the full range of kh from deep water to shallow water, with the shallow-water values being C_1 = 9/8 and C_2 = 0 (the fourth-order correction vanishes in the shallow-water limit), yielding the modified dispersion relation ω² = gk·tanh(kh) · [1 + (9/8)ε²] for shallow water, which, for ε = 0.035, gives a correction factor of 1 + (9/8)·(0.035)² ≈ 1.0014, a 0.14 percent increase in ω for a given k, or equivalently, a 0.14 percent increase in the wave celerity for a given wavelength, a correction that is negligibly small for the Atlantis design purposes and that can be safely ignored, although the general principle — that nonlinearity increases the wave celerity, causing the crest to travel faster than the trough and contributing to the wave's asymmetric profile — remains important for understanding the wave's evolution as it propagates across the ocean basin, particularly the development of the steep front that precedes the main body of the wave and that, because of its shorter wavelength and higher frequency, may experience different depth-attenuation characteristics than the main wave, a phenomenon that the CFD simulations have captured and that the Atlantis design team has accounted for by using the full nonlinear wave kinematics — not the linear Airy approximation — for all structural load calculations.

The depth-dependence of the orbital velocity in the Stokes fifth-order theory is more complex than the simple hyperbolic cosine ratio of the Airy theory, because each order of the perturbation expansion introduces a different depth function — the first-order term has the cosh(k(z+h))/cosh(kh) dependence familiar from Airy theory, the second-order term has a cosh(2k(z+h))/cosh(2kh) dependence (because the second-order solution involves the second harmonic, with wave number 2k, which decays more rapidly with depth than the fundamental harmonic because the argument of the hyperbolic cosine is doubled, so that cosh(2k(z+h))/cosh(2kh) ≈ 1/cosh(2kh) at the seafloor, a value that is smaller than the first-order attenuation by a factor of approximately cosh(kh)/cosh(2kh), which for kh = 0.001 is approximately 1/2 (since cosh(0.001) ≈ 1 and cosh(0.002) ≈ 1, the shallow-water limit where the attenuation is negligible for both harmonics, but for intermediate-depth waves with kh ≈ 1, the attenuation of the second harmonic is substantially greater than the attenuation of the first harmonic, by a factor of approximately cosh(1)/cosh(2) ≈ 1.54/3.76 ≈ 0.41, meaning that the higher-harmonic velocity components — which are associated with the sharper crest and flatter trough of the nonlinear wave profile — decay more rapidly with depth than the fundamental component, so that at sufficient depth, the wave motion becomes approximately sinusoidal even though the surface profile is highly nonlinear, a result that is favorable for the Atlantis communities because it means that the nonlinear wave profile — with its peaked crest that concentrates the wave energy into a shorter time interval and increases the peak dynamic pressure — is largely "filtered out" by the depth attenuation, leaving only the smoother, more nearly sinusoidal fundamental component at the pod's depth, a component whose dynamic pressure is easier to resist and whose pressure cycling (the subject of PART-005) is more uniform and predictable, less likely to produce unexpected fatigue damage than the sharp pressure spikes that would be associated with the passage of the nonlinear crests at shallower depths), and the higher-order terms (third, fourth, fifth) have depth functions with arguments of 3k, 4k, and 5k, respectively, all of which decay increasingly rapidly with depth, so that at the Atlantis pod's depth — where the fundamental (first-order) attenuation is already 85 to 95 percent, as established by the Airy theory and confirmed by the CFD simulations — the higher-order corrections are attenuated to the point of being completely negligible, a conclusion that can be quantified by evaluating the second-order velocity at z = -45 meters (Monterey depth) for a wave with kh = 0.001 and ε = 0.035: the fundamental horizontal velocity amplitude at the surface is u_1(0) = a·ω·cosh(kh)/sinh(kh) ≈ a·ω/(kh) = (1,600 meters)·(2π/1800 seconds) / (0.001) ≈ 5.6 meters per second (using ω = 2π/T with T ≈ 30 minutes = 1,800 seconds, a typical megatsunami period, and noting that the Airy shallow-water approximation gives u ≈ a·ω/(kh) = a·c/h = (1,600 m)·(21 m/s)/(45 m) ≈ 750 m/s, which is clearly incorrect — the error arises from using the shallow-water approximation with the full wave amplitude, which violates the small-amplitude assumption of linear theory, and the correct approach is to calculate the orbital velocity from the energy flux conservation, which gives u_surface ≈ 100 to 200 m/s as estimated in PART-001, and to then apply the exponential attenuation to find u_at_pod ≈ (0.05 to 0.15)·u_surface ≈ 5 to 30 m/s, a range that is consistent with the CFD results and that the Atlantis design uses), and the second-order velocity at the surface is u_2(0) ≈ (3/8)·ε·u_1(0)·cosh(2kh)/sinh(2kh) ≈ (3/8)·(0.035)·(100 m/s)·(1)/(2·0.001) ≈ 660 m/s, which is, again, an artifact of the small-kh approximation and is not physically realistic — the lesson being that the Stokes perturbation expansion, while formally valid for small ε, loses accuracy when kh is also small because the individual terms, although formally of higher order in ε, become large when divided by sinh(kh) or sinh(2kh), which are themselves small (of order kh), such that the expansion is not a simple power series in ε but rather a series in ε/kh (at least for the shallow-water limit), and the convergence of this series requires not only ε << 1 but also ε/kh << 1, which for the megatsunami — with ε ≈ 0.035 and kh ≈ 0.001 — gives ε/kh ≈ 35, indicating that the Stokes expansion DIVERGES in the shallow-water limit for megatsunami parameters, a result that is well-known in the wave theory literature (the Stokes expansion is a "deep-water" expansion, valid when kh is not too small, and breaks down in shallow water because the nonlinear terms, which involve derivatives of the velocity potential that become large near the seafloor when the wavelength is long compared to the depth, overwhelm the linear terms) and that necessitates the use of alternative wave theories for shallow-water waves — specifically, the cnoidal wave theory, which is based on the Jacobian elliptic function cn (hence the name) and which reduces to the solitary wave (a single, non-periodic wave with all of its mass above the mean sea level, propagating without change of form) in the limit of infinite wavelength, and which is the appropriate theoretical framework for the megatsunami in the shallow-water regime, a framework that the Atlantis design team has adopted and that is described in the following section.

The breakdown of the Stokes expansion in the shallow-water limit — a breakdown that is not a failure of the underlying physics but rather a failure of the perturbation method that was chosen to approximate the physics, indicating that a different method, one that does not rely on the assumption that the wave amplitude is small compared to the water depth, is required — leads naturally to the cnoidal wave theory, which was developed by the Dutch mathematician Diederik Korteweg and his student Gustav de Vries in 1895 (the KdV equation, which governs the propagation of weakly nonlinear, weakly dispersive shallow-water waves and which admits the cnoidal wave as its periodic solution and the solitary wave as its limiting case of infinite period, is one of the most celebrated partial differential equations in mathematical physics, giving rise to the entire field of soliton theory and integrable systems that has transformed our understanding of nonlinear wave phenomena in disciplines ranging from fluid dynamics to nonlinear optics to quantum field theory) and which provides the correct description of the megatsunami wave kinematics in the shallow-water regime that ## 3.0 ORBITAL VELOCITY DECAY WITH DEPTH — THE ENGINEERING PARAMETERIZATION FOR ATLANTIS POD DESIGN

The engineering parameterization of the orbital velocity decay with depth that the Atlantis design team uses for structural load calculations, anchoring system design, and fatigue analysis is based not on any single analytical wave theory — which, as the preceding sections have demonstrated, breaks down for the extreme wave parameters of crustal displacement in shallow water — but on a hybrid approach that combines the functional form of the depth-attenuation relationship from linear wave theory (which, despite its quantitative inaccuracies for extreme waves, correctly captures the qualitative dependence of velocity on depth — specifically, the fact that the velocity decreases monotonically with increasing depth, approaching zero at the seafloor for the vertical component and approaching a non-zero but substantially reduced value for the horizontal component, with the reduction factor depending on the wave number k and the water depth h through the hyperbolic cosine ratio cosh(k(z+h))/cosh(kh) for the horizontal velocity and the hyperbolic sine ratio sinh(k(z+h))/sinh(kh) for the vertical velocity) with empirical calibration coefficients derived from the CFD simulations and the physical model tests, coefficients that adjust the analytical predictions to match the high-fidelity numerical and experimental results, a methodology that is standard practice in offshore engineering — where the design of platforms, pipelines, and subsea structures routinely relies on a combination of analytical models for preliminary design and CFD/physical testing for final verification — and that has been validated by the excellent agreement (within 10 to 15 percent for the orbital velocity magnitude) between the CFD predictions and the 1:50 scale physical model tests parameterization that is derived from them.

The calibrated depth-attenuation function for the horizontal orbital velocity at the Atlantis pod locations is given by: u(z) = u_surface · exp(-α · (|z|/h)^β), where u_surface is the horizontal orbital velocity at the surface at the pod's offshore location (estimated as described above, with values of 100 to 200 meters per second for the Monterey site and 40 to 80 meters per second for the Osa site), z is the vertical coordinate (negative downward from the mean sea surface, so that |z| is the depth below the surface), h is the local water depth, and α and β are empirical calibration coefficients that have been determined by fitting the function to the CFD results: for the Monterey site, α_monterey = 2.8 and β_monterey = 0.65, yielding an attenuation factor at z = -45 meters (|z|/h = 1) of exp(-2.8) = 0.061, or approximately 6.1 percent of the surface velocity, consistent with the "85 to 95 percent attenuation" cited in the parent document — the attenuation is 93.9 percent, meaning that 6.1 percent of the surface velocity reaches the pod's depth — and for the Osa site, α_osa = 3.2 and β_osa = 0.58 (the larger α and smaller β for Osa reflect the fact that the Osa wave, being smaller and having a shorter period — a consequence of its generation at a location closer to the rotation axis, as described in PART-001 — has a higher effective wave number k, leading to more rapid attenuation with depth, a result that is consistent with the linear theory's prediction that the attenuation is governed by cosh(k(z+h))/cosh(kh), which for larger k (shorter wavelength or smaller depth) decreases more rapidly with |z|), yielding an attenuation factor at z = -30 meters (|z|/h = 1) of exp(-3.2) = 0.041, or approximately 4.1 percent of the surface velocity, corresponding to 95.9 percent attenuation, which is even more favorable for survival than the Monterey attenuation, consistent with Costa Rica's status as the lower-risk deployment location.
