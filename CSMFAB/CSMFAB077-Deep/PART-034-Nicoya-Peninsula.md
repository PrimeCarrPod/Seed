# CSMFAB077-DEEP-034 — NICOYA PENINSULA: PAPAGAYO WIND-JET REGIME, SHALLOW BASEMENT ROCK, NORTHERN COSTA RICA BOUNDARY CONDITIONS

**Document ID:** CSMFAB077-DEEP-034 | **Parent:** CSMFAB000000000077 | **Version:** 2.0 | **Part:** 034 of 160 — Block 4

The Nicoya Peninsula, Costa Rica's largest peninsula, extending approximately 120 kilometers from its northern base near the town of Liberia to its southern tip at Cabo Blanco, represents the northern boundary of the Costa Rica Atlantis network, a boundary that is defined by the transition, at approximately 10°N latitude, from the low-wave pivot-axis regime of southern Costa Rica to the increasingly energetic wave regime that characterizes the Pacific coast of Nicaragua, El Salvador, and Guatemala — the Central American states that lie farther from the pivot axis and that, per the MITgcm simulations, will experience wave heights in the range of 500 to 1,500 meters during a crustal displacement event, heights that, while still substantially less than the 3,200 meters of California (because of the continuing, although diminishing, protective effect of the pivot-axis proximity, and because of the Coriolis attenuation at low latitudes, which reduces the wave energy that is trapped at the coast), are high enough that the Atlantis pod design, as it currently exists (with the 10.5 MPa pressure rating, the 45-meter design depth, and the 200-kilonewton anchoring capacity), would not be adequate — the dynamic pressure at a 45-meter depth beneath a 1,000-meter wave, using the depth-attenuation function from PART-002 with the Nicoya-specific calibration (α = 2.2, β = 0.70, reflecting the slightly lower attenuation at the Nicoya site, because the wave period is longer — approximately 15 to 25 minutes, versus 5 to 10 minutes at Osa — and the longer period corresponds to a smaller wave number k and, hence, to less rapid attenuation with depth), is approximately 15 to 30 percent of the surface dynamic pressure, or approximately 1.5 to 3 megapascals, a pressure that, while still within the hull's 10.5 MPa test pressure, approaches the pressure that would cause the hull to buckle (the buckling critical pressure, calculated in PART-003, is 15 to 25 megapascals, a factor of safety of only 5 to 17, lower than the factor of 20 to 50 that characterizes the Osa and Monterey designs, a reduction that the Aegis C safety analysis considers acceptable only if the pod's deployment depth is increased — which is the approach that the Atlantis design team has adopted for the Nicoya site, specifying a deployment depth of 60 meters (instead of 45 meters) and a corresponding increase in the hull laminate thickness, from 40 millimeters to 55 millimeters, a modification that increases the pod's pressure rating to 14 megapascals (test pressure) and the buckling critical pressure to 30 to 50 megapascals, restoring the factor of safety to the 20-to-50 range that the Atlantis design philosophy demands, a restoration that ZIRCONIA's cost analysis estimates will add approximately $350,000 to the pod's fabrication cost, an amount that the Atlantis program has approved, recognizing that the Nicoya site, as the northern anchor of the Costa Rica network, must be as survivable as the southern sites, a survivability that this PART-034, by describing the Nicoya wave regime, the Papagayo wind-jet effects (the Papagayo wind, a strong, offshore wind that blows from the Caribbean across the Nicaraguan and Costa Rican lowlands during the winter months, funneling through the topographic gaps in the Central American cordillera and generating intense upwelling in the Gulf of Papagayo, an upwelling that the Nicoya pod's oceanographic sensors will monitor, contributing to the Atlantis program's understanding of the Central American oceanography, a understanding that is essential for the prediction of the post-event environmental conditions, because the upwelling — which depends on the wind, which depends on the atmospheric circulation, which depends on the climate, which will be disrupted by the crustal displacement event — will be altered, and the Atlantis pod's sensors, by measuring the pre-event upwelling, will establish the baseline against which the post-event changes can be assessed), and the shallow basement rock of the Nicoya Complex (the same obducted ophiolite that underlies the Quepos site, but at a shallower depth — the Nicoya Complex outcrops extensively on the Nicoya Peninsula, and the seabed at the deployment site consists of the weathered surface of the ophiolite, a surface that the ROV surveys have shown to be irregular, with local relief of 1 to 3 meters, a irregularity that the Atlantis site preparation team has addressed by fabricating a custom seabed adapter — a steel framework that rests on four adjustable legs, each of which can be extended or retracted by the ROV to accommodate the local seabed topography, a framework that the pod's lifting lugs attach to, transferring the pod's weight to the seabed through the legs, a design that is more complex than the simple helical-pile anchoring of the flat seabed sites, but that is necessary to accommodate the Nicoya site's irregular topography, a necessity that the Atlantis design team has accepted, as the price of deploying at a site whose wave hazards require the additional protection that the increased depth and the stronger anchoring provide.
