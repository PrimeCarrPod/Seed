## 1.0 THE MONTEREY ACCELERATED RESEARCH SYSTEM: A PRE-EXISTING CABLE NETWORK AT THE ATLANTIS DEPLOYMENT SITE

The Monterey Accelerated Research System (MARS), a cabled ocean observatory operated by the Monterey Bay Aquarium Research Institute (MBARI) since its installation in November 2008, consists of a 52-kilometer-long submarine fiber-optic and power cable that extends from the MBARI shore station at Moss Landing to a seafloor science node located at a depth of 891 meters on the Smooth Ridge, a sediment-covered plateau approximately 20 kilometers offshore of Moss Landing and approximately 8 kilometers southwest of the Atlantis pod's deployment site on the northern flank of the Monterey Canyon at 45 meters depth, a fortuitous proximity that the Atlantis Site Selection Committee recognized early in the candidate evaluation process as a decisive advantage of the Monterey site, because the MARS cable provides, at a location that is well within the range of a standard ROV-deployed extension cable (the MARS node's Ethernet ports are designed to accept extension cables of up to 10 kilometers in length, using the same wet-mateable connectors — the ODI Nautilus connectors, which are rated for 4,000 meters depth and 10,000 mate/de-mate cycles, a robustness that is necessary for the ROV-deployed instruments that the MARS observatory hosts, instruments that are periodically retrieved for maintenance and redeployed, requiring connectors that can be repeatedly mated and de-mated underwater without degradation, a capability that the Atlantis pod's FEATHER mesh interface — a custom-designed pressure housing that contains the gigabit Ethernet switch, the fiber-optic transceiver, and the power conditioning circuitry, all potted in a thermally conductive epoxy for heat dissipation and shock resistance, and rated for the 10.5 MPa test pressure of the Atlantis pod (the interface housing is mounted on the exterior of the pod's hull, in a recessed cavity that is protected from debris impact by the auxetic cladding, and is connected to the pod's internal FEATHER mesh router through a pressure-tight bulkhead penetrator — a fitting that passes the fiber-optic and power cables through the hull without compromising the pressure integrity, a technology that is standard in the submarine cable industry and that the Atlantis design team adapted from the penetrators used on the Alvin deep-submergence vehicle, which routinely operates at depths of 4,500 meters, far deeper than the Atlantis pod, and whose penetrators — manufactured by the Specialty Products Group of the Teledyne Marine company — have a proven reliability record of over 5,000 dives without a single leak, a record that gives the Atlantis design team high confidence in the penetrator's performance at the Atlantis pod's modest depth of 45 meters, where the pressure differential across the penetrator is only 0.55 megapascals, compared to the 45 megapascals at Alvin's maximum depth, a factor of 82 difference that provides an enormous safety margin and that essentially guarantees the penetrator's leak-tightness over the pod's 50-year design life, a guarantee that the Aegis C quality assurance program has verified through the hydrostatic pressure testing of every penetrator that is installed on the pod, using the same test protocol that Teledyne uses for the Alvin penetrators — a 1.5-times-maximum-pressure hold test for 24 hours, with a leak rate acceptance criterion of less than 10^-8 standard cubic centimeters per second, a rate that, if sustained for 50 years, would result in a total leakage of approximately 15 cubic centimeters of seawater into the pod's interior — less than a tablespoon, and a volume that the pod's dehumidifier system can easily remove without affecting the internal environment — so that even if a penetrator were to leak at the maximum acceptable rate, the pod's crew would never notice it, a margin of safety that is typical of the Atlantis design philosophy and that, for the FEATHER mesh interface, is particularly important because the interface is one of the few components of the pod that penetrates the pressure hull, a penetration that, if it were to fail catastrophically — not a slow leak, but a sudden rupture — would allow seawater to flood the pod's interior at a rate determined by the size of the penetration (the fiber-optic cable is 5 millimeters in diameter, so a complete loss of the cable would leave a 5-millimeter hole in the hull, through which water would enter at a rate of approximately 0.5 liters per second (from the Bernoulli equation for flow through an orifice, Q = C_d·A·√(2gh/ρ), where C_d ≈ 0.6 is the discharge coefficient, A = π·(0.0025 m)² ≈ 2 × 10^-5 m² is the orifice area, g = 9.81 m/s², h = 45 m is the water head, and ρ = 1,025 kg/m³ is the density, but the driving pressure is not the hydrostatic head — which would produce a jet of water at approximately 30 meters per second, or a flow rate of approximately 0.6 liters per second — but rather the pressure differential between the exterior (0.55 MPa) and the interior (0.1 MPa, atmospheric pressure), a differential of 0.45 MPa that drives the flow at a velocity of approximately √(2ΔP/ρ) = √(2·450,000/1,025) ≈ 29.6 m/s, and the flow rate is Q = 0.6·(2×10^-5)·29.6 ≈ 3.6 × 10^-4 m³/s, or 0.36 liters per second — a rate that, while substantial, is manageable if the crew can isolate the leak: the Atlantis pod's emergency procedures direct the crew to close the isolation valve that is installed on the interior side of every hull penetration, a manually operated ball valve that can be closed in approximately 5 seconds, during which time approximately 1.8 liters of water would enter the pod — a volume that the bilge pump (capacity 5 liters per minute) can remove in approximately 22 seconds, so that even in the worst-case penetration failure, the pod would take on less than 2 liters of water, a volume that presents no threat to the pod's buoyancy or to the crew's safety, a reassuring calculation that the Atlantis human factors team has incorporated into the crew training program, so that the crew knows, with quantitative confidence, that the hull penetrations — which are the most vulnerable points of the pressure boundary — are not a catastrophic threat, a confidence that is essential for the crew's psychological well-being, because the fear of a hull breach — the nightmare of every submariner, the sudden inrush of water, the pressure, the darkness, the drowning — is one of the most visceral fears that the human mind can experience, and the Atlantis program must address that fear, not by dismissing it (which would be dishonest — the fear is rational, because hull breaches CAN happen, and have happened, in the history of submarines, with fatal consequences) but by demonstrating, through the quantitative analysis, the design margins, and the emergency procedures, that the risk is acceptably small and that, even if the risk materializes, the crew has the training and the equipment to respond effectively, a demonstration that the Atlantis comprehensive safety case — a 340-page document that analyzes every conceivable failure mode of the pod, from the mundane (a clogged toilet, a failed light bulb) to the catastrophic (a hull breach, a fire, a toxic atmosphere), and that specifies the preventive measures, the detection methods, and the response procedures for each — provides, a safety case that the Aegis C technical review board has reviewed and approved, and that the crew training program uses as the foundation for the crew's emergency drills, drills that the crew practices weekly during the pre-deployment training and daily during the deployment, until the responses are automatic, until the crew can close the isolation valve in the dark, in the noise, in the chaos of the event, without thinking, because thinking takes time, and in a hull-breach emergency, time is measured in seconds, a sobering reality that the Atlantis program confronts — and masters — through the combination of design (the penetrator is rated for 10.5 MPa, 190 times the operating pressure, 23 times the maximum predicted pressure during the event) and training (the crew practices the breach response 50 times during the certification course and at least once per week during deployment), a combination that reduces the probability of a breach-related fatality to a number that is effectively zero — not zero in the mathematical sense, but zero in the operational sense, meaning that the Atlantis program is confident, to the highest degree that engineering and human factors can provide, that no crew member will die from a hull breach, a confidence that is the result of the exhaustive analysis, testing, and training that the Atlantis program has invested in the pod's safety, an investment that is the most important investment the program makes, because the pod's purpose — to preserve human life through the catastrophe — is defeated if the pod itself becomes the cause of death, a failure that the Atlantis design team has dedicated itself to preventing, with every resource and every method at its disposal, a dedication that this PART-016, by describing the integration of the pod with the MARS cabled observatory — an integration that provides the pod with the high-bandwidth data link to the surface that is essential for the remote monitoring and the crew's morale, but that also introduces the additional hull penetrations and the additional failure modes that the safety case must address — exemplifies, because every system that is added to the pod, however valuable, introduces new risks, and the Atlantis design process must identify, assess, and mitigate those risks, a process that this document records, in the detail that the safety-critical nature of the Atlantis program demands.
