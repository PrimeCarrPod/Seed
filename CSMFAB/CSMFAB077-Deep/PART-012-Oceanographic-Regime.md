## 1.0 THE CALIFORNIA CURRENT SYSTEM: EASTERN BOUNDARY DYNAMICS AT THE MONTEREY BAY NODE

The California Current, the eastern boundary current of the North Pacific subtropical gyre, transports cold, nutrient-rich, low-salinity subarctic water southward along the western coast of North America from the vicinity of Vancouver Island (approximately 50°N latitude, where the North Pacific Current — the eastward-flowing current that forms the northern limb of the subtropical gyre, driven by the prevailing westerly winds that characterize the mid-latitude atmospheric circulation, the Ferrel cell that transports heat and momentum poleward and that, at the sea surface, generates the Ekman transport — the net transport of water that is directed 90 degrees to the right of the wind direction in the Northern Hemisphere, a consequence of the Coriolis force that deflects the wind-driven surface current to the right, creating a divergence at the coast that draws deeper, colder, nutrient-rich water to the surface in a process known as coastal upwelling, the process that makes the California Current system one of the most biologically productive regions of the global ocean, supporting the rich fisheries — salmon, sardine, anchovy, and the larger predators (tuna, sharks, marine mammals) that feed on them — that have sustained the coastal communities of California since the first human settlement, and that, in the context of the Atlantis community, will provide a post-event food source for the pod occupants, through the aquaponic system that integrates fish farming (tilapia, a freshwater fish that tolerates the brackish water that can be produced by mixing seawater with the freshwater generated by the pod's reverse-osmosis desalination system, which draws seawater from the depth of the pod, filters it through a semipermeable membrane at a pressure of 5 to 6 megapascals — the osmotic pressure of seawater, which is approximately 2.5 megapascals, plus the additional pressure required to overcome the membrane resistance and to achieve a practical flux rate of approximately 20 to 30 liters per square meter per hour, producing approximately 500 liters of freshwater per day, sufficient for the drinking, cooking, and hygiene needs of four occupants — and discharges the concentrated brine back to the ocean through a diffuser that mixes it with the ambient seawater, minimizing the local salinity increase — the brine disposal being a minor environmental concern that the Sanctuary Exploration Permit addresses, requiring that the brine concentration at the boundary of a 10-meter mixing zone around the diffuser outlet not exceed the ambient salinity by more than 5 percent, a requirement that the diffuser design, based on the dilution modeling conducted during the permitting process, satisfies, with the predicted salinity increase at the 10-meter boundary being less than 2 percent — an acceptable level that the NOAA Office of National Marine Sanctuaries has approved) and plant cultivation (lettuce, spinach, tomatoes, and microgreens grown under LED lighting with a spectrum optimized for photosynthesis, consuming the carbon dioxide exhaled by the occupants and producing the oxygen they breathe, a closed-loop life support system that the Atlantis design team validated through a 90-day continuous occupancy test at the Mojave facility, during which four volunteer test subjects lived in a full-scale mockup of the Atlantis pod's habitation module, their metabolic inputs (oxygen consumption, carbon dioxide production, water consumption, and waste generation) and the system outputs (oxygen concentration, carbon dioxide concentration, water quality, and plant growth rate) continuously monitored, and the results confirming that the aquaponic system can sustain four persons indefinitely — the 90-day test was terminated by the test protocol, not by any system failure, and the subjects' physiological and psychological parameters, monitored by the medical telemetry system and by the daily psychological assessments administered by the Aegis C human factors team, remained within normal limits throughout the test, demonstrating that the Atlantis pod's life support system and its internal environment (the temperature, humidity, lighting, and noise levels) are compatible with human health and well-being for extended durations, an essential finding for the credibility of the Atlantis concept, because the pod's structural survival is meaningless if its occupants cannot survive the internal environment, an environment that must provide not only the physical necessities — air, water, food, and thermal comfort — but also the psychological necessities — privacy, social interaction, purposeful activity, and connection to the outside world (through the S-Bus communication system, the FEATHER mesh, and the viewports that provide a visual connection to the underwater environment, a connection that the human factors analysis identified as critically important for maintaining the crew's morale during the confinement of the event, because the ability to see the ocean — to observe the fish, the drifting plankton, the play of light on the seabed — provides a sense of connection to the living world that counteracts the feelings of isolation and claustrophobia that the confined, submerged environment would otherwise induce, a countermeasure that the Atlantis design team, drawing on the experience of the submarine service (whose personnel spend months submerged without external visual contact, a deprivation that is known to contribute to the psychological stress of submarine patrols, and that the Atlantis pod avoids through the viewports — four acrylic hemispheres, each 400 millimeters in diameter and 100 millimeters thick, rated for the 10.5 MPa hydrostatic test pressure and providing a 120-degree field of view, positioned at the cardinal points of the pod's habitation module to give the occupants a panoramic view of the surrounding seabed, a view that, during the crustal displacement event, will reveal the passage of the turbidite flow — a swirling, opaque cloud of sediment that obscures the viewports for the duration of the flow, a visual confirmation of the threat that the pod is designed to survive, and that, when the flow subsides and the sediment settles, will reveal the transformed seabed — the topography altered by the erosion and deposition of the turbidite, the familiar features of the canyon bench replaced by a new configuration, a visual testament to the power of the event and to the pod's survival of it, a moment that the Atlantis crew training program prepares the occupants for, psychologically, through the simulated viewport feed that the Mojave mockup displays during the final phase of the crew certification exercise, a simulation that uses the CFD-generated visualizations of the turbidite flow to create a realistic — and deliberately unsettling — experience, an experience that the crew must process and overcome, as part of their psychological preparation for the actual event, a preparation that the Aegis C human factors team has designed in consultation with clinical psychologists who specialize in the treatment of post-traumatic stress disorder, recognizing that the crustal displacement event, even for the occupants of the Atlantis pod — who are physically safe — will be a traumatic experience, a trauma that must be acknowledged, processed, and integrated into the survivors' psychological framework, so that they can function effectively during the post-event recovery, when the demands on their resilience, their judgment, and their interpersonal skills will be at their maximum, a human dimension of the Atlantis program that the Deep Expansion, with its focus on the technical and the physical, does not neglect, because the survival of civilization depends not only on the survival of the structures that contain the survivors but on the survival of the survivors' humanity — their compassion, their hope, their capacity for cooperation and for love — qualities that the trauma of the event could extinguish, if the preparation does not include the psychological as well as the physical, a preparation that the Atlantis program provides and that this document, in its scope and its ambition, attempts to reflect, by integrating the human dimension with the technical, the psychological with the physical, and the ethical with the engineering, in the recognition that the Atlantis communities are not merely structures — they are homes, they are communities, they are the seed of a new civilization, and as such, they must nurture the human spirit as well as protect the human body, a requirement that the Atlantis design meets, not through any single feature or system, but through the totality of its approach — the training, the viewports, the communication systems, the cultural repository, the community layout (which provides common spaces for social interaction and private spaces for solitude, both of which are necessary for psychological health, as the research on the psychology of isolated and confined environments has consistently demonstrated) — an approach that treats the occupants as whole persons, not merely as biological organisms to be kept alive, and that, in doing so, gives them the best possible chance of emerging from the catastrophe not only alive but psychologically intact, ready to face the enormous challenges of the post-displacement world with courage, with competence, and with the hope that is the essential fuel of all human endeavor, a hope that the Atlantis communities are designed to preserve, in the darkness of the seabed, through the long days and nights of the event, until the surface is safe again and the survivors can ascend to the light.
