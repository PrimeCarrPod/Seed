# CSMFAB077-DEEP-027 — OSA SITE CFD MODELING: 170M WAVE MECHANICS AT 8.5°N LATITUDE, CORIOLIS PARAMETER EFFECTS

**Document ID:** CSMFAB077-DEEP-027 | **Parent:** CSMFAB000000000077 | **Version:** 2.0 | **Part:** 027 of 160 — Block 3

The computational fluid dynamics simulations of the wave passage at the Osa Peninsula site, conducted using the same OpenFOAM-based methodology that was developed for the Monterey simulations (PART-017) but adapted to the specific bathymetry, wave parameters, and Coriolis forcing of the 8.5°N latitude, reveal a fundamentally different wave regime than the Monterey case — a regime characterized by wave heights at the pod's 30-meter depth that are approximately 15 to 25 percent of the Monterey wave heights (not simply because the surface wave is smaller — 170 meters versus 3,200 meters, a ratio of approximately 0.053 — but because the wave PERIOD is shorter, approximately 5 to 10 minutes for the Costa Rica wave versus 20 to 40 minutes for the California wave, a difference that arises from the different generation mechanisms: the California wave is a basin-scale response, involving the entire Pacific Ocean, with a wavelength of approximately 300 to 500 kilometers and a period of 20 to 40 minutes — the classical shallow-water wave with c = √(gh) ≈ 190 m/s in the deep ocean, slowing to approximately 21 m/s as it shoals on the continental shelf, a deceleration that stretches the wave and increases its period, whereas the Costa Rica wave is a LOCAL response, generated by the displacement of the Cocos Plate and the associated deformation of the Middle America Trench, a displacement that is smaller — because Costa Rica is near the pivot axis of the lithospheric rotation, as described in PART-008 — and that produces a wave with a wavelength of approximately 50 to 100 kilometers and a period of 5 to 10 minutes, a wave that is more akin to a conventional earthquake-generated tsunami than to the planetary-scale megatsunami of the California case, a distinction that the Osa CFD simulations capture and that has important implications for the Atlantis pod design: the shorter wave period means that the wave number k = 2π/λ is larger (by a factor of approximately 3 to 6), and, as the depth-attenuation analysis in PART-002 established, the attenuation of the orbital velocity with depth is governed by the hyperbolic cosine function cosh(k(z+h))/cosh(kh), which, for larger k, decreases more rapidly with depth — the attenuation is STRONGER for the shorter-period Costa Rica wave, a beneficial effect that the Osa CFD simulations quantify: at the pod's depth of 30 meters, the orbital velocity is attenuated by approximately 96 to 98 percent relative to the surface (compared to 85 to 95 percent at Monterey), a difference that, when combined with the smaller surface orbital velocity (which is, approximately, proportional to the wave height divided by the wave period, H/T, and the ratio H/T for Osa — 170/600 ≈ 0.28 meters per second per meter of wave height — is about 5 times smaller than for Monterey — 3,200/1,800 ≈ 1.8 m/s per meter), yields a pod-level orbital velocity of only 1 to 4 meters per second, a velocity that produces a dynamic pressure of approximately 0.5 to 8 kilopascals — less than 2 percent of the Monterey dynamic pressure, and essentially negligible from a structural perspective, a result that confirms the Osa site's status as the ultra-low-hazard deployment and that validates the Atlantis strategy of deploying the primary survival nucleus at Costa Rica, where the physics of wave generation and attenuation conspire to provide a degree of protection that approaches the ideal — the pod at Osa, during the crustal displacement event, will barely feel the wave, a wave that, at the surface, is 170 meters high and is devastating the coastal communities, but that, at 30 meters depth, is merely a gentle surge, a surge that the pod's anchoring system resists with a factor of safety of greater than 30, a surge that the pod's hull experiences as a pressure fluctuation of less than 1 percent of the hydrostatic baseline, a surge that, far from being a threat, is simply the background noise of a dynamic ocean, a noise that the pod — designed to survive the worst that the planet can produce — treats as routine, a routine that the Osa CFD simulations have characterized with the precision that the Atlantis design requires and that this PART-027 reports.
