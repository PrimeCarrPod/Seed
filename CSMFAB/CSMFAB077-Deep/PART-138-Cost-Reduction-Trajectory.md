# CSMFAB077 — PART 138: COST REDUCTION TRAJECTORY

**Parent:** CSMFAB077 | **Part:** 138 of 160 | **Block:** 12 | **Aegis C** | **July 2026**

The 85 percent learning curve — a well-established empirical relationship in manufacturing economics, first documented by Theodore Paul Wright in his 1936 study of airframe production costs, in which he observed that the labor hours required to produce each successive airframe decreased by a constant percentage (approximately 15 percent) with each doubling of the cumulative production quantity, a phenomenon that reflects the accumulated experience of the production workforce (workers become more efficient as they repeat the same tasks, discovering shortcuts, refining techniques, and reducing errors), the improvements in the production process (tooling modifications, workflow optimizations, supply chain integration), and the incremental design refinements that reduce the material waste, the assembly time, and the rework rate — predicts that the Atlantis pod fabrication cost will decline from the initial $2.04 million per Monterey-spec pod (the cost of Pod #1, the prototype that absorbed the one-time engineering costs of the production tooling development, the assembly procedure validation, and the workforce training, costs that the Zirconia analysis has separated from the "recurring" fabrication cost — the cost of producing Pod #2 through Pod #6 at Monterey and Pod #1 through Pod #4 at Osa — to establish the "theoretical first unit cost" of $1.74 million, which is the cost that Pod #1 would have had if the one-time costs of $300,000 per pod (for a total of $1.8 million in non-recurring engineering distributed across the 10-pod production run at $180,000 per pod) were excluded, a standard cost-estimating technique that the program's cost engineers use to forecast the cost of future pods and to evaluate the competitiveness of the Atlantis design against alternative underwater habitat concepts) to approximately $1.38 million at Pod #20 (the 4th doubling from Pod #1's 1.74 to Pod #2's 1.48 (85 percent of 1.74), Pod #4's 1.26, Pod #8's 1.07, and Pod #16's 0.91 — the unit cost at each doubling being 85 percent of the cost at the previous doubling, a progression that, when plotted on log-log axes, produces a straight line with a slope of log(0.85)/log(2) ≈ -0.234, the "learning curve slope" that characterizes the rate of cost reduction and that the Atlantis program's cost engineers have validated against the actual cost data from the fabrication of Pods #1 through #10, which closely followed the 85 percent curve — the actual cost of Pod #10 was $1.10 million, compared to the predicted cost of $1.07 million, an error of 2.8 percent that is within the typical forecasting accuracy of the learning curve methodology (which generally predicts unit costs within ±10 percent at the 4th doubling, with the accuracy improving at higher production quantities as the statistical noise in the early production data is averaged out), providing the program's financial analysts with the confidence to project a pod fabrication cost of approximately $620,000 at Pod #100 (the 7th doubling from Pod #1), a cost that would make the Atlantis habitat economically competitive with conventional surface construction for disaster-resistant community shelters — at $620,000 per pod accommodating 4 persons, the per-capita construction cost would be $155,000, approximately one-third of the $400,000-per-capita cost of the San Francisco Dome and within the range of the per-capita construction cost of FEMA's conventional disaster-resistant community shelters (the "safe rooms" designed to the FEMA P-361 standard for tornado and hurricane protection, which cost approximately $120,000 to $180,000 per person at the scale of a 200-person community shelter), a cost parity that the Atlantis program's advocates within FEMA have identified as the threshold at which the Atlantis pod transitions from an experimental demonstration project to a production-line disaster resilience product that could be deployed at scale, funded by the same FEMA Hazard Mitigation Grant Program that currently funds the construction of conventional safe rooms, and integrated into the national disaster preparedness framework alongside the tornado shelters, the earthquake-resistant building codes, and the tsunami evacuation towers that protect coastal communities against the hazards that we can predict and prepare for — a framework that the crustal displacement threat, improbable as it is, demands we extend to the hazards that we cannot predict, the hazards whose probability we cannot calculate, the hazards whose consequences we cannot comprehend, except through the stark language of the geological record: the wave was 3,200 meters high, it traveled faster than sound, it scoured the continents and drowned the coastal plains and carried the bodies of every living thing that had not found shelter into the abyssal depths from which the wave had risen, and when the waters finally receded, the world was remade, the species that had survived were the species that had been, by luck or by preparation, beneath the wave when it passed, in the deep where the water was still, where the pressure was constant, where the darkness was eternal, and where the community of humans who had built their pods — at Monterey, at Osa, at the global network of PART-147 — waited, breathing their HELIOX mixture, monitoring their CO₂ scrubbers, checking their decompression schedules, and listening, through the acoustic modems, for the signal from the surface that would tell them the wave had passed, the waters were receding, and the time had come — after 90 days, after 6.25 days of decompression, after 50 years of preparation — to return to the surface, to see what had survived, to begin the rebuilding, and to carry forward, into the transformed world that the wave had left behind, the knowledge, the culture, the memory, and the hope of the human species, preserved in the pods beneath the sea.
