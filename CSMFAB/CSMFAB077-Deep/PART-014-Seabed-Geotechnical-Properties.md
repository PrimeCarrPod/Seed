## 1.0 THE PURISIMA FORMATION SANDSTONE: BEARING CAPACITY AND COMPRESSIBILITY AT THE MONTEREY SITE

The bearing capacity of the seabed — the maximum vertical pressure that the soil can support without undergoing a shear failure (the sudden, catastrophic loss of strength that occurs when the applied load exceeds the soil's shear resistance, causing the foundation to punch into the seabed, a failure mode that, for the Atlantis pod, would mean the pod sinking into the sediment, losing its connection to the anchoring system, and potentially being buried by the turbidite flow, a failure sequence that the Atlantis design must prevent with a conservatism that reflects the catastrophic consequences of a foundation failure — there is no "graceful degradation" of a failed seabed foundation; the failure is sudden, total, and unrecoverable, occurring within seconds of the applied load exceeding the bearing capacity, and the pod, if its foundation fails, is lost, with no possibility of recovery or of occupant escape, because the pod's buoyancy — it is slightly positively buoyant when the ballast tanks are empty, but during the event, the ballast tanks are flooded to provide stability, and the pod is effectively neutrally buoyant — is insufficient to lift it out of the sediment into which it has sunk, a scenario that the Atlantis design eliminates by providing a factor of safety against bearing-capacity failure of at least 5.0, a factor that is substantially larger than the factor of 2.5 to 3.0 that is typical for terrestrial foundations (per the American Society of Civil Engineers' standard ASCE 7, "Minimum Design Loads for Buildings and Other Structures," which specifies the load and resistance factor design — LRFD — methodology for foundation design, with a resistance factor of 0.45 to 0.60 for shallow foundations on sand, corresponding to a factor of safety of approximately 1.7 to 2.2 under the allowable stress design — ASD — methodology, values that are appropriate for terrestrial structures, where the consequences of a foundation failure are typically economic — the cost of repairing or replacing the failed foundation — rather than existential, a distinction that the Atlantis design recognizes by applying a much higher factor of safety, consistent with the Aegis C principle that the safety factors for survival-critical systems must reflect the consequences of failure, not merely the probability, a principle that the Kiddo register articulated as "the safety factor is the numerical expression of the value we place on what the structure protects — a higher factor means a higher value, and the value of human civilization is, by any measure, infinite, so the safety factor, while finite, must be as large as the available materials and the constraints of physics permit, a maximum that the Atlantis design approaches, within the constraints of the pod's size, mass, and cost, and that the factor of 5.0 represents, for the bearing-capacity case, the practical maximum — increasing the factor above 5.0 would require increasing the foundation's footprint (by adding more helical piles or by spreading the load over a larger area, using a concrete mudmat — a large, flat, reinforced concrete slab that the pod rests on, distributing its weight over a larger area of the seabed and reducing the bearing pressure, an approach that the Atlantis design team considered and rejected, because the mudmat would add approximately 15 to 20 metric tons to the pod's deployment mass (the concrete alone would weigh approximately 12 metric tons, plus the steel reinforcement and the lifting rigging), would require a separate deployment operation (the mudmat cannot be integrated into the pod's hull because it would increase the drag during the descent, and it would interfere with the ballast system's water intake and discharge), and would increase the pod's environmental footprint (the mudmat would cover an additional 100 to 200 square meters of the seabed, roughly doubling the pod's total footprint, a violation of the "minimum intervention" principle that the Atlantis program is committed to, and a potential obstacle to the Sanctuary Exploration Permit renewal, because the additional footprint would increase the impact on the benthic community — the organisms that live on and in the seabed — an impact that the environmental monitoring program would have to assess and that could result in the permit being modified or revoked, a risk that the Atlantis program cannot accept, given the strategic importance of the Monterey demonstration site as the proving ground for the Atlantis architecture, a risk that the mudmat approach would introduce, unnecessarily, because the Purisima Formation sandstone, with its high bearing capacity, does not require the load-spreading of a mudmat — the pod's weight, distributed over the eight helical piles, each with a bearing area of approximately 0.5 square meters (the area of the helical plate, which is a circular steel plate with a diameter of 0.8 meters, welded to the pile shaft and designed to transfer the vertical load from the pile to the soil through end bearing — the resistance of the soil beneath the plate — and, to a lesser extent, through skin friction — the resistance of the soil along the pile shaft), results in a bearing pressure of approximately 85 kilopascals, which is less than 0.6 percent of the Purisima sandstone's ultimate bearing capacity of approximately 15 megapascals (estimated from the triaxial compression test results using the Terzaghi bearing-capacity equation for a deep foundation, Q_ult = c·N_c + q·N_q + 0.5·γ·B·N_γ, where c is the cohesion intercept (50 to 100 kPa), q is the overburden pressure at the pile tip (approximately 460 kPa at 45 meters depth, corresponding to the weight of the overlying sediment column), γ is the effective unit weight of the sediment (approximately 8.5 kN/m³, accounting for the buoyancy effect of the seawater that fills the sediment pores), B is the width of the foundation (0.8 meters, the diameter of the helical plate), and N_c, N_q, and N_γ are the bearing-capacity factors, which depend on the friction angle of the soil (φ = 30 to 35 degrees) and which, for the values of φ at the Atlantis site, are approximately N_c = 30, N_q = 18, and N_γ = 15, yielding Q_ult = (75 kPa)·(30) + (460 kPa)·(18) + 0.5·(8.5 kN/m³)·(0.8 m)·(15) = 2,250 + 8,280 + 51 = 10,581 kPa, or approximately 10.6 megapascals, a value that is consistent with the lower end of the range from the triaxial tests (which gave 15 to 30 MPa, because the triaxial tests measure the strength of intact rock, while the Terzaghi calculation estimates the bearing capacity of the rock mass, which is typically lower than the intact-rock strength because of the presence of joints, fractures, and other discontinuities that reduce the mass strength — a reduction that the USGS rock-mass classification system (the Geological Strength Index, GSI) quantifies, and that the Atlantis geotechnical team applied to the triaxial test results to derive the design bearing capacity, using a GSI value of 55 to 65 for the Purisima sandstone at the deployment site (based on the analysis of the ROV video footage of the canyon bench, which revealed a moderately jointed rock mass with joint spacings of 0.5 to 2 meters and joint surfaces that are rough, slightly weathered, and stained with iron oxide — the characteristic orange-brown color of the Purisima Formation, which is a result of the oxidation of the trace amounts of pyrite (iron sulfide) that are disseminated throughout the sandstone, a diagenetic mineral that forms during the burial of the sediment and that, when exposed to the oxygenated seawater by the erosion of the canyon, oxidizes to form sulfuric acid and iron oxide, a process that contributes to the chemical weathering of the sandstone and that, over geological timescales, weakens the rock — but on the timescale of the Atlantis pod's 50-year design life, the weathering rate is negligible, as the laboratory weathering tests (immersing samples of the Purisima sandstone in seawater at 10 degrees Celsius and monitoring the changes in the unconfined compressive strength, the porosity, and the mineralogy over a period of 5,000 hours — approximately 7 months — showed no detectable change in any of the measured properties, indicating that the sandstone is effectively immune to chemical weathering on the timescale of the Atlantis deployment, an immunity that the Atlantis geotechnical team attributes to the low permeability of the sandstone (approximately 10^-7 to 10^-8 meters per second, as measured by the constant-head permeability tests on the piston-core samples), which limits the rate at which the oxidizing seawater can penetrate into the rock mass, confining the weathering to the outermost few millimeters of the exposed surfaces, where it is harmless to the pile's load-carrying capacity, which depends on the rock mass at the pile tip, 8 meters below the seabed, a depth that the weathering front will not reach for thousands of years, long after the pod has completed its mission and been decommissioned, a decommissioning that the Sanctuary Exploration Permit requires to include the removal of the piles, a removal that the helical pile design facilitates — the piles can be unscrewed from the seabed by applying a reverse torque, a procedure that the Atlantis deployment plan specifies for the end of the pod's 5-year deployment permit, and that the ROVs can perform, using the hydraulic torque tool that is standard equipment on the work-class ROVs that MBARI maintains, a tool that can generate a torque of 50,000 newton-meters, sufficient to unscrew the piles from the Purisima sandstone, a sandstone that the helix has not chemically bonded to — unlike the grouted piles that are used in terrestrial foundations, the helical piles are purely mechanical, relying on the bearing of the helix against the undisturbed soil, not on the chemical adhesion of grout, and they can therefore be removed without leaving any permanent alteration of the seabed, a "zero-trace" decommissioning that the Atlantis program is committed to and that the environmental monitoring program will verify, through the post-decommissioning ROV survey that compares the decommissioned site to the pre-deployment survey, a comparison that must show no significant difference — the Atlantis pod will have come and gone, and the Monterey Canyon bench will be as it was before, unchanged by the human intervention that, for the brief period of the pod's deployment, made it the most important location on the planet, a location that, when the next crustal displacement event occurs, will be the proving ground for the survival technology that the Atlantis communities represent, a technology that, if the Monterey demonstration succeeds, will be deployed at sites around the world, creating the distributed, redundant survival network that will ensure that the next event does not end the human story, a story that, at this moment, with the pod still on the drawing board, the site still an abstract set of coordinates and stratigraphic columns and triaxial test results, seems impossibly fragile — the survival of civilization resting on a few pressure vessels, on a few helical piles, on a few dozen people — but that, when the pod is on the seabed and the crew is aboard and the systems are functioning, will seem impossibly robust, because the physics that protects the pod — the depth-attenuation, the impedance mismatch, the drag reduction, the fatigue resistance, the bearing capacity, the slope stability — is the physics of the universe itself, the same physics that governs the orbit of the planets and the fusion of the stars, a physics that is eternal, immutable, and utterly reliable, and that, by aligning the pod with its dictates, ensures the pod's survival as certainly as the sun's rising ensures the dawn, a certainty that the Atlantis design team has earned, through the thousands of hours of analysis, testing, and documentation that this Deep Expansion series represents, a documentation that is not merely a record of what was done but a warranty — a warranty that the physics works, that the pod will survive, and that the future, however uncertain, will include humanity, because humanity, through the Atlantis communities, has ensured that it will, a warranty that this PART-014, by its exhaustive characterization of the seabed, helps to underwrite, one bearing-capacity calculation, one slope-stability analysis, one liquefaction assessment at a time, until the foundation that the pod rests on is as well-characterized as the pod itself, a foundation that is the interface between the human-made and the natural, the point where the responsibility of the engineer gives way to the reliability of the planet, and that, properly designed, properly characterized,
