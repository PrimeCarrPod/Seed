# CSMFAB077 — PART 136: FUNDING MODEL — PUBLIC-PRIVATE PARTNERSHIP, FEMA/DHS GRANTS, COMMERCIAL LICENSING

**Parent:** CSMFAB077 | **Part:** 136 of 160 | **Block:** 12 | **Aegis C** | **July 2026**

The Atlantis program's $47.8 million initial capital requirement is funded through a four-component financial structure: (1) $18.2 million (38 percent) from Carrington Storm Motors' internal capital allocation — the Aegis C materials development budget, which funds the CSMFAB program across its multiple initiatives (the Lonsdaleite Outskirt Communities, the San Francisco Dome design, and the Atlantis program) and which is replenished by the commercial licensing revenue from the Aegis C intellectual property portfolio (the LBFRP-001 composite patent, licensed to three manufacturers in the US, Germany, and Japan for applications ranging from deep-sea mining equipment to ballistic armor to hydrogen fuel tank manufacturing, with annual royalty payments totaling approximately $4.2 million, representing approximately 3 percent of the combined annual sales of the licensees' LBFRP-001-enhanced products — a royalty rate that the program's licensing director negotiated to maximize the revenue from the patent while maintaining the incentive for the licensees to invest in the manufacturing infrastructure and the marketing necessary to grow the LBFRP-001 market, a strategic balancing act that the licensing director describes as "the art of technology transfer — you want to charge enough to fund the research that created the technology, but not so much that you strangle the industrial ecosystem that will deploy it at scale, because the ultimate goal of the Aegis C program is not to collect royalties but to get the technology out into the world, into the products that will protect people, and that requires the licensees to be profitable, to be motivated, and to see the licensing relationship as a partnership, not a tax, a philosophy that has guided the Aegis C licensing strategy from the beginning and that has resulted in the stable, growing royalty stream that provides approximately 40 percent of the internal funding for the CSMFAB initiatives." (2) $14.3 million (30 percent) from a FEMA Building Resilient Infrastructure and Communities (BRIC) grant, awarded through a competitive process that evaluated the Atlantis proposal against the program's criteria of "innovative, community-driven, risk-informed resilience investments that reduce disaster suffering and enhance community recovery," with the Atlantis proposal receiving the highest technical score of any BRIC application in the 2026 funding cycle — a score of 97 out of 100, with the three-point deduction attributed to the proposal's reliance on the crustal displacement hypothesis, which the BRIC review panel acknowledged is a "scientifically credible but unvalidated" hazard whose probability cannot be quantified with the precision that the BRIC program typically requires for its traditional hazard mitigation investments (flood walls, seismic retrofits, wildfire fuel reduction), a limitation that the Atlantis proposal addressed by emphasizing the program's dual-use value — the pods serve as research platforms, educational facilities, and disaster response assets (the pods' medical capability, communications infrastructure, and autonomous life support make them ideal forward operating bases for the FEMA Urban Search and Rescue (US&R) task forces responding to a major California earthquake, a scenario that is far more probable than a crustal displacement event and that the BRIC review panel recognized as providing "immediate, tangible resilience benefits independent of the crustal displacement hazard," a recognition that the Atlantis program's government relations team successfully leveraged in their Congressional outreach, resulting in a bipartisan letter of support signed by the entire California Congressional delegation (53 Representatives and 2 Senators), urging the FEMA Administrator to approve the BRIC grant on the grounds that "the Atlantis program represents a transformative investment in American technological leadership, disaster resilience, and international cooperation, and its benefits extend far beyond the improbable scenario of crustal displacement to the all-too-probable scenarios of earthquakes, tsunamis, and other coastal hazards that threaten California's communities and economy"). (3) $9.6 million (20 percent) from commercial licensing of the Atlantis pod technology for non-catastrophic applications — the program has licensed the pod design, the ECLSS technology, and the hyperbaric safety protocols to a consortium of three offshore aquaculture companies (in Norway, Chile, and Japan) who are developing subsea fish farms — large, automated aquaculture enclosures at depths of 30 to 50 meters, where the constant temperature, the protection from surface storms, and the proximity to the nutrient-rich deep water create ideal conditions for the cultivation of Atlantic salmon, yellowtail amberjack, and bluefin tuna, species that are increasingly threatened by the warming and acidification of the surface ocean and that the subsea farms can grow more efficiently, with lower environmental impact, and at a lower cost than the conventional surface net-pen farms that currently dominate the aquaculture industry, a convergence of survival technology and commercial opportunity that the Atlantis program's business development team describes as "the unexpected second act of the Atlantis story — we designed these pods to survive a catastrophe that may never occur, and in the process we created a technology that is revolutionizing the way humanity feeds itself from the sea, a technology whose commercial value dwarfs the government grants and the internal funding combined, and that will, over the next decade, generate more revenue for Carrington Storm Motors than the entire $47.8 million Atlantis project cost, a return on investment that will fund the next generation of Atlantis pods, the expansion to the global network of PART-147, and the realization of the ultimate vision: a world where thousands of underwater communities, distributed across every ocean, provide humanity with the redundant, resilient, survivable presence that the crustal displacement threat demands, and that the commercial success of the subsea aquaculture industry, which is built on the Atlantis technology, is the economic engine that makes that vision possible, transforming the Atlantis program from a government-funded survival project into a self-sustaining, market-driven, exponentially growing enterprise that earns its existence every day, not by asking for grants but by delivering value — to the fish farmers who produce healthier, more affordable seafood, to the consumers who eat it, to the environment that benefits from the farms' lower carbon footprint and reduced pressure on wild fish stocks, and to the human species, which benefits from the distributed, resilient infrastructure that the farms, operated and maintained by the same crews who would occupy them during a crustal displacement event, provide as a permanent, operational, profitable network of survival nodes that will be there, ready, maintained, and crewed, when the wave finally comes.
