## 1.0 THE RECESSION PHASE: WHEN THE OCEAN RECLAIMS ITS BASIN AND THE REAL THREAT TO SUBMERGED STRUCTURES EMERGES

The recession phase of a crustal displacement event — the period, lasting from approximately 10 to 30 days after the initial inundation, during which the estimated 10^12 to 10^14 cubic meters of water that was displaced onto the continents by the mega-tsunami drains back into the ocean basins, a volume of water that is approximately 1,000 to 100,000 times the volume of Lake Superior (12,000 cubic kilometers, or 1.2 × 10^13 cubic meters, the largest of the North American Great Lakes by volume, and a convenient reference for visualizing the scale of the crustal displacement flood — imagine the entire Great Lakes system, all five lakes combined (approximately 22,000 cubic kilometers), emptying onto the continents, and then draining back to the ocean, carrying with it the eroded remains of the continental surface, a mixture of water, sediment, rock, and organic debris that, in its density and its flow characteristics, is not water but a hyperconcentrated debris flow — a fluid with a sediment concentration of 10 to 50 percent by volume, a density of 1,100 to 1,800 kilograms per cubic meter (compared to 1,025 for clear seawater), and a viscosity that, because of the particle-particle interactions that dominate the rheology of concentrated suspensions, is 10 to 1,000 times greater than the viscosity of water, a non-Newtonian rheology — meaning that the shear stress is not linearly proportional to the shear rate, as it is for a Newtonian fluid like water, but follows a more complex relationship, typically a Bingham plastic model (in which the fluid behaves as a rigid solid at low shear stresses — below a yield stress τ_y, which for a debris flow with 30 percent sediment concentration is on the order of 100 to 1,000 pascals — and as a viscous fluid at higher shear stresses, with an effective viscosity — the Bingham viscosity — of 0.1 to 10 pascal-seconds, 100 to 10,000 times the viscosity of water) or a Herschel-Bulkley model (which generalizes the Bingham model by allowing the shear stress to vary as a power law of the shear rate above the yield stress, a model that better captures the shear-thinning behavior — the reduction of the effective viscosity with increasing shear rate — that is characteristic of concentrated suspensions, where the alignment of the particles in the flow direction reduces the interparticle friction and the effective viscosity, a behavior that is favorable for the Atlantis pod because it means that the turbidite flow, at the high shear rates that characterize the flow near the pod's hull (shear rates of 10^2 to 10^4 per second, corresponding to velocity gradients of 10 to 100 meters per second over a distance of 0.01 to 1 meter, the thickness of the boundary layer where the flow velocity transitions from zero at the hull surface to the free-stream velocity), has a lower effective viscosity than it would have at lower shear rates, a reduction that decreases the shear stress on the hull — the skin friction drag — and the thickness of the boundary layer — the region of reduced velocity near the hull surface — both of which are beneficial effects that the Atlantis design team has incorporated into the CFD simulations by using the Herschel-Bulkley model with parameters calibrated from the rheological measurements of sediment-water mixtures that were collected during the site survey phase (using a rotational rheometer — a laboratory instrument that measures the torque required to rotate a spindle immersed in a fluid sample at a controlled speed, from which the shear stress and the shear rate are calculated — with the sediment samples that were retrieved from the Monterey and Osa sites by the Charlemagne survey vessel's box corer and piston corer, which extracted sediment cores from depths of 1 to 5 meters below the seabed, providing samples of the material that the turbidite flow will entrain and transport, samples that the Atlantis materials laboratory at Mojave analyzed to determine their particle size distribution (using laser diffraction granulometry, which measures the angular distribution of light scattered by a suspension of particles and infers the particle size distribution from the Mie scattering theory, with a size range of 0.01 to 3,500 micrometers, covering the full range from clay to coarse sand), their mineral composition (using X-ray diffraction, which identifies the crystalline phases present in the sediment based on their characteristic patterns of X-ray scattering, revealing a mineralogy that is dominated by quartz, feldspar, and clay minerals — smectite, illite, and kaolinite — at Monterey, and by carbonate minerals — calcite and aragonite — at Osa, reflecting the different geological provenances of the two sites), and their rheological properties — the yield stress, the Bingham viscosity, and the Herschel-Bulkley consistency and flow-behavior indices — as functions of the sediment concentration, the particle size distribution, and the salinity of the interstitial water (which influences the electrochemical interactions between the clay particles, affecting the flocculation — the aggregation of clay particles into larger, more easily settled flocs — and the yield stress of the debris flow, a complex interplay that the Atlantis rheology team characterized through a systematic experimental program that measured the flow curves (shear stress versus shear rate) of 120 sediment-water mixtures spanning the full range of sediment concentrations, particle sizes, and salinities that are expected at the Atlantis sites, an experimental program that consumed approximately 4 months and that produced the rheological database that the CFD simulations use as their constitutive model, ensuring that the simulated turbidite flow accurately represents the physical flow that will occur during the event, an accuracy that is essential because the turbidite flow — not the wave itself — is the primary threat to the submerged pod, as described in Section 6 of the parent document, a threat that includes not only the drag force (which, as calculated from the CFD simulations with the Herschel-Bulkley rheology, is approximately 200 to 500 kilonewtons for the Monterey pod under the 90th-percentile turbidite flow conditions (flow velocity of 20 meters per second, sediment concentration of 25 percent, flow direction aligned with the pod's major axis — the worst-case orientation, which the pod's azimuthal positioning during deployment is designed to avoid, by orienting the pod such that the major axis is parallel to the predicted turbidite flow direction, reducing the drag coefficient by a factor of approximately 3, a factor that the anchoring system's factor of safety of 20 to 50 comfortably accommodates, even for the worst-case orientation), a drag force that is substantial but manageable, as the anchoring system analysis in PART-015 demonstrates), but also the debris impact hazard — the boulders, ranging from 0.5 to 3 meters in diameter, with masses of 500 to 50,000 kilograms, that are transported as bedload, rolling, sliding, and saltating (bouncing) along the seabed under the influence of the turbidite flow's shear stress, and that, if one of these boulders strikes the pod's hull, would deliver an impact energy of 10^5 to 10^8 joules (equivalent to 0.025 to 25 kilograms of TNT), an energy that, concentrated on an impact area of approximately 0.01 to 0.1 square meters (the contact area between the boulder and the hull), would produce instantaneous contact pressures of 10^6 to 10^9 pascals — pressures that exceed the compressive strength of even the highest-performance concretes and that would pulverize a conventional steel hull, a threat that the Atlantis pod's layered turbidite defense (the auxetic cladding, the acoustic cavitation bubble curtain, the CNT-reinforced polyurea coating, and the teardrop hull geometry that reduces the probability of a direct impact, as described in Section 6 of the parent document) is designed to mitigate, with the CFD-based impact analysis predicting that the probability of a boulder of sufficient size (diameter greater than 0.5 meters, the threshold below which the CNT coating can absorb the impact energy without damage to the underlying pressure hull) striking the pod during the entire turbidite flow phase is approximately 0.03 to 0.08 per pod — i.e., there is a 3 to 8 percent chance that any given pod will experience a significant boulder impact during the recession phase, a probability that, while low, is not negligible, and that the Atlantis design addresses by providing the auxetic cladding — a sacrificial layer that fractures during the impact, absorbing the energy and preventing it from reaching the pressure hull — and, in the event that the cladding is breached, the CNT-polyurea coating provides a second line of defense, protecting the hull from the abrasion of the suspended sediment and the smaller debris particles that bypass the bubble curtain, a defense that the testing program at Mojave has validated through full-scale impact tests, in which a 500-kilogram granite boulder (equivalent to a prototype boulder of 3.5 meters diameter and 90,000 kilograms mass at the 1:6 scale used for the impact testing, which was conducted at a scale that was larger than the 1:50 scale used for the hydraulic modeling, because the impact dynamics — which depend on the material properties of the boulder and the target, not on the fluid mechanics of the flow — are not subject to the Froude similarity constraints that govern the hydraulic modeling, and can therefore be tested at a larger scale, reducing the scale effects and increasing the confidence in the results) was propelled at the test article — a full-scale section of the Atlantis pod's hull, complete with the auxetic cladding, the CNT-polyurea coating, and the LBFRP-001 laminate — at velocities of 15 to 25 meters per second (the range predicted by the CFD simulations for the boulder impact velocity at the pod's depth, accounting for the reduction in the flow velocity near the seabed due to the bottom boundary layer — the reduction that the Atlantis location strategy exploits, by positioning the pod at a depth (45 meters) where the boundary layer has reduced the flow velocity by approximately 30 to 50 percent relative to the velocity in the main body of the turbidite, a reduction that is additional to the depth-attenuation of the wave orbital velocity and that further decreases the threat to the pod), and the results confirmed that the auxetic cladding absorbs the impact energy without transferring a damaging load to the pressure hull (the maximum strain measured on the inner surface of the hull laminate, using strain gauges bonded to the laminate at the impact location, was less than 0.1 percent — well below the laminate's design strain limit of approximately 0.5 percent for impact loading, and far below the ultimate tensile strain of approximately 2.5 percent, confirming that the hull experiences only elastic deformation during the impact, with no permanent damage and no risk of delamination or fiber breakage), and that the CNT-polyurea coating, after 10 consecutive impacts at the same location (representing the worst case of repeated impacts, which could occur if the pod is located at a site where the turbidite flow is concentrated — such as a narrow channel, which the site selection process specifically avoids, as described in PART-013), showed only superficial erosion — the loss of approximately 0.5 millimeters of the 8-millimeter coating thickness — without any degradation of the underlying laminate, confirming that the coating can sustain multiple impacts without requiring replacement, a capability that is important because, during the recession phase, the pod will be inaccessible — the surface will still be too dangerous for the Charlemagne fleet to approach, because the turbidite flows and the atmospheric disturbances (the dust, the shock waves, the wind) will persist for weeks or months after the main wave passage — and the pod must survive, autonomously, without external repair or resupply, for the entire duration of the hazardous period, a period that the Atlantis design assumes to be 90 days (the standard life-support endurance), and that the turbidite defense architecture is designed to survive without degradation, through the combination of the redundant defense layers — if the auxetic cladding is destroyed on one side of the pod, the CNT coating on that side may be exposed to continuous abrasion, but the pod can be rotated during the event (using the ballast system, which can adjust the pod's trim, causing it to rotate around its vertical axis as the ballast water is transferred between tanks, presenting a fresh, undamaged side of the hull to the flow, a defensive maneuver that the Atlantis crew training program includes as a standard procedure for turbidite flow passage, and that the pod's control system can execute autonomously if the crew is incapacitated or if the event occurs when the pod is unoccupied — during the "standby mode," when the pod is on the seabed, its systems maintained by the automated S-Bus controller, awaiting the arrival of the crew or the event), a maneuver that, by distributing the turbidite wear over the entire hull surface, extends the life of the turbidite defense system and increases the probability that the pod will survive the entire hazardous period without requiring repair, a probability that the Atlantis fault tree analysis estimates as greater than 0.995 for the Monterey site (where the turbidite flow is most intense) and greater than 0.999 for the Osa site (where the turbidite flow is less intense, because the inundation — and hence the volume of water that drains back to the ocean — is smaller, and because the Golfo Dulce, with its sheltered geometry, attenuates the flow), probabilities that, combined with the structural survival probability of the pressure hull (greater than 0.9999, as discussed in PART-005), the anchoring system survival probability (greater than 0.999, as discussed in PART-015), and the life support system reliability (greater than 0.995, as discussed in the parent document's Section 10), yield an overall pod survival probability — the probability that a given pod, with its crew, survives the crustal displacement event — of approximately 0.990 to 0.998, depending on the site and the crew's training and performance, a probability that the Atlantis design team considers acceptable for the demonstration phase (the goals of which are to validate the Atlantis architecture, to collect operational data, and to provide the nucleus of the post-event recovery — goals that a survival probability of 99 percent supports, since, out of ten pods (six at Monterey and four at Osa), the expected number of surviving pods is 9.9 — essentially, all ten pods are expected to survive, with the loss of one pod being a possibility but not a probability, a possibility that the Atlantis program accepts as the irreducible risk of deploying a new technology in an environment that cannot be fully characterized before the event occurs, a risk that is far lower — by a factor of approximately 100 — than the risk of NOT deploying the pods (which is the risk that, when the event occurs, there will be NO survivors with the knowledge, the organization, and the resources to rebuild), and that is therefore justified, not as an acceptable cost (the loss of a pod and its crew is a tragedy, not a cost, and the Atlantis program does not treat it as such — the crew members are volunteers, fully informed of the risks, and the program's commitment to their safety is absolute, as expressed in the exhaustive analysis, the generous margins, and the redundant safety systems that characterize the design, a commitment that the Aegis C ethical review board has affirmed as consistent with the program's values and with the principles of informed consent and risk minimization that govern human subject research in extreme environments) but as an unavoidable consequence of the fact that no human enterprise can be made perfectly safe, and that the pursuit of safety, when carried to its logical extreme, would prevent any action — including the deployment of the Atlantis communities — that carries any risk, however small, a paralysis that would ensure the extinction of the species, because the risk of extinction from crustal displacement is not zero (it is approximately 10^-5 per year, as estimated in PART-001), and the only way to reduce that risk to an acceptable level is to accept the much smaller risks associated with the preparation, a trade-off that the Aegis C architecture embraces, a trade-off that the Atlantis communities embody, and a trade-off that this Deep Expansion, through its exhaustive documentation of the risks and the mitigations, seeks to make transparent, so that the decision-makers — the Aegis C program managers, the funding organizations, and ultimately the public whose survival is at stake — can make an informed choice, a choice that the Atlantis design team, by its work, has made easier, because the analysis shows, with quantitative rigor and with conservative assumptions, that the Atlantis communities will survive, that the risks are manageable, and that the alternative — doing nothing — is unacceptable, a conclusion that Parts 001 through 010 have established through the physics foundation they provide, and that Parts 011 through 160 will build upon, through the detailed site-specific analysis, the engineering design documentation, and the deployment planning that transforms the Atlantis concept from a theoretical possibility into a practical reality, a reality that, when the next crustal displacement event occurs, will be the
