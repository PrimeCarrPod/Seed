## 1.0 DRAKE BAY: THE PIVOT-AXIS SITE WHERE CRUSTAL DISPLACEMENT PHYSICS COLLAPSES THE WAVE FIELD

Drake Bay (Bahía Drake), located at approximately 8°41'N, 83°40'W on the northwestern coast of the Osa Peninsula, occupies a unique position in the crustal displacement wave field — a position that the Atlantis geophysical analysis, as described in PART-001 and PART-008, identifies as being within approximately 50 to 80 kilometers of the "pivot axis" of the lithospheric rotation, the line on the Earth's surface along which the displacement velocity of the lithosphere relative to the geoid is zero (or, more precisely, is minimized, because the lithosphere, as a spherical shell rotating about an axis that passes through the Earth's center, has a velocity that varies as the sine of the angular distance from the rotation axis, with the velocity being zero at the two points where the rotation axis intersects the Earth's surface — the rotation poles — and being maximal at the equator of the rotation, 90 degrees from the poles, a pattern that is analogous to the variation of the Earth's rotational velocity with latitude, where the velocity is zero at the geographic poles and maximal — approximately 465 meters per second — at the equator, except that, for the crustal displacement rotation, the axis is not the Earth's spin axis but a different axis, determined by the mechanics of the lithospheric decoupling, and the velocity of interest is not the rotational velocity of the lithosphere but the displacement velocity of the ocean floor, the forcing that generates the wave, such that locations near the rotation poles — the pivot axis — experience minimal forcing and, consequently, minimal wave heights, a physical protection that the Atlantis site selection strategy exploits, by positioning the Costa Rica sites — all of which are within 200 kilometers of the pivot axis, as estimated from the MITgcm simulations and the paleomagnetic reconstruction of the last event's rotation geometry — in the region where the crustal displacement wave is inherently weakest, a region that, by the accident of geography, coincides with one of the most beautiful, most ecologically intact, and most welcoming coastlines on Earth, a coincidence that the Atlantis program considers fortunate, but not accidental — the same geological forces that produced the Osa Peninsula's uplift, its Oxisol preservation, and its low-latitude wave attenuation also produced the stunning biodiversity and the renowned natural beauty that make the Osa Peninsula a world-class ecotourism destination, attracting approximately 100,000 visitors per year, who come to hike the Corcovado trails, to surf the Pavones break, to kayak the mangrove estuaries of Drake Bay, and to observe the whales and the dolphins that inhabit the waters off the peninsula, a tourism that the Atlantis program supports, not only because it provides the economic base of the local community but because it fosters the global appreciation of the natural world that the Dolittle register considers essential for the long-term preservation of the planet's biodiversity, a preservation that the Atlantis communities, by their example of coexistence with the marine environment, promote, a promotion that this PART-031, by describing the Drake Bay site, advances.

The Drake Bay site itself, at a depth of 25 meters on a gently sloping sandy seabed approximately 3 kilometers offshore of the village of Agujitas, is the shallowest and the most benign of the seven Costa Rica sites that the Atlantis program has evaluated, a benignity that is expressed in the predicted wave parameters: the surface wave height, from the MITgcm pivot-axis simulation, is estimated at 30 to 60 meters, a reduction of 65 to 82 percent relative to the Osa site's 170 meters and a reduction of 98 to 99 percent relative to the Monterey site's 3,200 meters, a reduction that, when propagated to the pod's depth of 25 meters through the depth-attenuation function described in PART-002, yields an orbital velocity of approximately 0.3 to 1.0 meters per second and a dynamic pressure of approximately 0.05 to 0.5 kilopascals — values that are, for all practical purposes, zero, meaning that the Drake Bay pod, during the crustal displacement event, will experience essentially no wave loading, a condition that the Atlantis design team refers to as "the survivability ideal" — a pod that is deep enough to avoid the surface turbulence, shallow enough to permit easy deployment and egress, positioned at a site where the wave physics dictates minimal disturbance, and connected to the Aegis C command network through the satellite link and the FEATHER mesh, a pod that is, in effect, a fully functional, fully survivable, and fully comfortable underwater home, a home that the Atlantis program intends to deploy at Drake Bay as the first of the "expansion" Atlantis communities — the communities that follow the successful demonstration of the Monterey and Osa sites and that extend the Atlantis network to the additional Costa Rica locations described in this Block 4, a network that will provide, for the first time in human history, a distributed, redundant, and passively survivable refuge from a planetary catastrophe, a refuge that the Atlantis communities, by their design and their deployment, realize.
