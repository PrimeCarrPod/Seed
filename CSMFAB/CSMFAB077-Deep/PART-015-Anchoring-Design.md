## 1.0 HELICAL PILE ANCHORING: THE MECHANICS OF SCREW-ANCHOR EMBEDMENT IN COMPETENT SEDIMENTARY ROCK

The anchoring system for the Atlantis-class subsea habitation module — a critical subsystem whose failure would result in the immediate and catastrophic loss of the pod, because an unanchored pod at 45 meters depth, subjected to the lateral forces of the turbidite flow (200 to 500 kilonewtons, as estimated in PART-010) and to the oscillatory forces of the wave orbital velocity (5 to 30 meters per second, as estimated in PART-002), would be swept off the canyon bench and transported down the canyon axis into the abyssal depths, where the increasing hydrostatic pressure would eventually exceed the hull's design limit of 10.5 megapascals (at a depth of approximately 1,025 meters, the pressure at the pod's depth would reach 10.5 MPa, and beyond that depth, the hull would implode — a catastrophic, instantaneous collapse that would kill the occupants before they could react, a failure that the anchoring system is designed to prevent with a reliability that, as the fault tree analysis described in PART-005 specifies, must exceed 0.999 for the Monterey site and 0.9999 for the Osa site, values that are achievable because the helical pile anchoring concept — which is adapted from the terrestrial foundation engineering practice of using screw-piles for the foundations of transmission towers, wind turbines, and other structures that must resist large lateral and uplift loads in a variety of soil conditions, a practice that has been standardized by the International Building Code (IBC Section 1810) and by the Deep Foundations Institute's guidelines for helical pile design (DFI-IM-2018), and that the Atlantis anchoring team — led by a Ph.D. geotechnical engineer with 22 years of experience in offshore foundation design, including the design of the suction-caisson anchors for the Hywind Scotland floating wind farm (the world's first commercial floating wind farm, which uses suction caissons — large, open-bottomed steel cylinders that are embedded in the seabed by pumping water out of the interior, creating a pressure differential that forces the caisson into the seabed, a concept that the Atlantis team considered but rejected, because the suction caisson requires a fine-grained, low-permeability soil (clay or silt) to maintain the pressure differential, and the Purisima Formation sandstone, with its relatively high permeability (10^-7 to 10^-8 meters per second), does not provide the necessary seal, a deficiency that the helical pile — which relies on mechanical bearing, not on fluid pressure — does not suffer, making it the superior choice for the Atlantis application, a choice that the anchoring team validated through a series of pullout tests conducted at the Mojave facility, where a full-scale helical pile (8 meters long, 200 millimeters shaft diameter, 800 millimeters helix diameter, fabricated from ASTM A572 Grade 50 steel with a yield strength of 345 MPa and an ultimate tensile strength of 450 MPa, the same steel that is used for the structural beams of bridges and buildings, selected for its combination of high strength, good weldability, and corrosion resistance — the latter being provided by a 3-millimeter thickness of the MXene coating, applied by the same electrophoretic deposition process that coats the pod's hull, providing both corrosion protection and an antifouling surface that discourages the attachment of marine organisms, a surface that the pullout tests confirmed remains effective after the equivalent of 50 years of immersion — the test piles were pre-conditioned in seawater for 5,000 hours at 15 degrees Celsius before testing, and the pullout capacity was measured and compared to the capacity of un-conditioned control piles, showing no significant difference (less than 3 percent variation, within the statistical uncertainty of the test, which had a coefficient of variation of approximately 8 percent, based on the results of 12 pullout tests at each of three embedment depths — 4 meters, 6 meters, and 8 meters — a test matrix that provided the data for the calibration of the pullout capacity model, a model that relates the pullout capacity Q_pullout to the embedment depth D, the helix diameter B, the soil's undrained shear strength s_u (for cohesive soils) or the friction angle φ and the effective overburden pressure (for cohesionless soils), and the number of helical plates (the Atlantis design uses a single plate, because the Purisima sandstone's strength is sufficient to achieve the required capacity with a single plate, reducing the installation torque — the twisting force required to screw the pile into the seabed, which is proportional to the number of plates and to the square of the helix diameter — and simplifying the installation procedure, which is performed by the ROV's hydraulic torque tool, a tool that the MBARI ROV Ventana, which is based at Moss Landing and which is available for the Atlantis deployment, routinely uses for the installation of the MARS cabled observatory's instruments, an observatory that the Atlantis pod will connect to for its high-bandwidth data link, as described in PART-016, a synergistic relationship between the Atlantis pod and the MARS observatory that benefits both — the pod gains a reliable, high-speed data connection to the surface (the MARS cable, which was installed in 2008 and which provides 1 gigabit per second of Ethernet connectivity between the observatory node at 891 meters depth and the shore station at Moss Landing, a connectivity that the Atlantis pod's FEATHER mesh interface — a gigabit Ethernet switch that converts the pod's internal FEATHER mesh traffic to the MARS Ethernet protocol — uses to transmit the pod's telemetry, the crew's communications, and the video from the viewports and the external cameras to the Aegis C command network, a data stream that is essential for the remote monitoring of the pod's status during the event and for the post-event assessment of the pod's condition, and the MARS observatory gains an additional instrument platform — the Atlantis pod — that expands the observatory's sensor network and that provides a unique opportunity to study the effects of the crustal displacement event on the Monterey Canyon environment, a scientific opportunity that the MARS science team has enthusiastically endorsed, as documented in the Memorandum of Understanding between the Atlantis program and the Monterey Bay Aquarium Research Institute (MOU-ATL-MBARI-2026-001), which governs the sharing of the MARS cable bandwidth (the Atlantis pod is allocated 100 megabits per second of the 1 gigabit total, sufficient for the telemetry and the communications, with the remaining bandwidth reserved for the MARS science instruments, a sharing arrangement that the MOU specifies will be renegotiated if the Atlantis pod's data requirements exceed the allocated bandwidth, a flexibility that the MARS network architecture — which uses standard Ethernet switching, allowing dynamic bandwidth allocation — supports, and that the Atlantis program appreciates, because the post-event data stream, which will include the high-resolution video of the turbidite flow, the acoustic recordings of the wave passage, and the detailed telemetry of every system on the pod, will be far larger than the pre-event data stream, and the Atlantis program needs the assurance that the MARS cable can accommodate this increase, an assurance that the MARS network engineers have provided, based on their analysis of the network's capacity and the Atlantis pod's expected data rates, an analysis that the Atlantis telecommunications team has reviewed and accepted, confirming that the MARS cable provides the high-bandwidth, low-latency data connection that is essential for the pod's integration into the Aegis C command network, a connection that the Osa site — which lacks a cabled observatory — cannot match, and that partially offsets the Osa site's inherent advantages (lower wave threat, simpler regulatory environment) in the overall comparison of the two demonstration sites, a comparison that the ZIRCONIA analysis has formalized and that the Aegis C program management has used to allocate the resources between the two sites, with Monterey receiving the larger share of the communications infrastructure investment (the FEATHER mesh integration, the satellite backup link, the MARS cable interface) and Osa receiving the larger share of the life-support and habitability investment (the larger aquaponic system, the more extensive crew training facilities, the additional escape modules — reflecting the Osa site's role as the primary post-event recovery headquarters, a role that requires the Osa pods to support a larger crew (the Osa site can accommodate up to 24 persons at surge capacity, compared to Monterey's 36, but the Osa site's crew is expected to be the nucleus of the post-event recovery organization, and as such, they require additional training, additional equipment, and additional redundancy, all of which increase the per-pod cost at Osa relative to Monterey, an increase that the ZIRCONIA analysis estimates at approximately $1.2 million per pod, or approximately $4.8 million for the four-pod Osa community, an increase that the Aegis C program has approved, recognizing that the Osa site's strategic importance as the high-confidence survival nucleus justifies the additional investment, an investment that the helical pile anchoring system — which is identical at both sites, because the Osa carbonate platform, with its bearing capacity of 200 to 400 kilopascals (lower than the Purisima sandstone's 15 MPa, but still more than adequate for the pod's foundation loads of 85 kPa), provides a similar embedment performance, with the pullout capacity at the 8-meter design depth being approximately 800 to 1,000 kilonewtons per pile (compared to 1,200 at Monterey, a reduction that reflects the lower strength of the carbonate platform but that still provides a total lateral capacity of 6,400 to 8,000 kilonewtons for the eight-pile array, a factor of safety of 32 to 40 relative to the predicted lateral load of 200 kilonewtons at the Osa site, a factor that is more than adequate and that confirms that the helical pile anchoring concept, originally developed for the more demanding Monterey conditions, is easily adapted to the less demanding Osa conditions, a confirmation that the anchoring team validated through the pullout tests on the carbonate platform samples that the Charlemagne survey vessel collected from the Osa site, tests that showed the expected reduction in capacity but that also showed a more ductile pullout response — the Osa piles, when tested to failure, exhibited a gradual loss of capacity, with the load-displacement curve showing a long post-peak plateau rather than the abrupt drop that characterized the Monterey piles' failure (which was a brittle failure, with the sandstone fracturing suddenly at the peak load, a behavior that is characteristic of rock-socketed piles and that the anchoring team accounted for by applying a higher factor of safety to the Monterey design — 5.0 versus 3.5 for Osa — a differential that reflects the different failure modes and the different consequences of a failure at the two sites (a Monterey failure, while unlikely, would be more consequential because the Monterey pods are at the frontline of the wave, and their failure would deprive the Aegis C program of the data that the Monterey pods are designed to collect — the "experimental" function of the Monterey community, as distinct from the "operational" function of the Osa community, a distinction that the Aegis C program management emphasizes: Monterey is where the Atlantis architecture proves itself, Osa is where the architecture delivers on its promise, and both are necessary for the overall success of the mission).
