# CSMFAB077 — PART 131: TOTAL PROJECT COST BREAKDOWN (MONTEREY + OSA)

**Parent:** CSMFAB077 | **Part:** 131 of 160 | **Block:** 12 — Cost and Economics | **Aegis C** | **July 2026**

The total project cost of $47.8 million for the combined Monterey and Osa demonstration communities is disaggregated across six major cost categories, each of which is further decomposed into the specific line items that constitute the Atlantis program's financial architecture and that have been validated through competitive bidding, vendor quotations, the actual invoiced costs of the CSMFAB068 Lonsdaleite Outskirt Communities fabrication (whose automated fiber placement infrastructure, LBFRP-001 material supply chain, Grid-Seed power module production line, S-Bus beacon assembly process, and FEATHER mesh network deployment experience provided the cost baseline against which the Atlantis estimates were calibrated, with the Atlantis-specific modifications — the teardrop hull geometry requiring revised AFP tooling at a one-time cost of $340,000, the MXene exterior coating application system at $180,000, the hyperbaric-qualified viewport acrylic procurement at $95,000 per pod, the CO₂ fire suppression system at $78,000 per pod, the medical lock fabrication at $210,000, the hydraulic power unit and emergency hatch closure mechanism at $145,000, and the integration testing at the Mojave hyperbaric facility at $520,000 — representing the incremental costs beyond the CSMFAB068 baseline that the Atlantis program's unique depth, pressure, and safety requirements demand). The fabrication cost of $22.4 million for ten pods — six for Monterey, four for Osa — distributes as $2.04 million per Monterey-spec pod (HELIOX atmosphere, 5.5 ATA pressure rating, teardrop hull geometry optimized for the Monterey Canyon turbidite flow direction, the piezoelectric acoustic cavitation bubble curtain for boulder deflection, and the tension-leg buoy platform rated for the 2.5-meter significant wave height of the Monterey Bay winter storm environment) and $1.93 million per Osa-spec pod (compressed air atmosphere at 4.0 ATA, the H₂S-resistant exterior coating of epoxy-novolac resin proven effective against hydrogen sulfide permeation at the 0.07-ppm ambient concentration of the Golfo Dulce's oxic surface layer, a simplified acoustic cavitation system because the turbidite threat at the Osa site is reduced by the Cocos Plate subduction geometry, and a catenary-moored buoy rather than a tension-leg buoy because the Osa site's 30-meter depth permits a simpler and less expensive mooring configuration), with the inter-pod tunnels at $320,000 per pair, the surface relay buoys at $145,000 each, and the seabed anchoring systems (grouted pile anchors, 8 per pod) at $85,000 per set, including the ROV-assisted installation labor provided by the Charlemagne fleet's work-class ROVs (Saab Seaeye Leopard or equivalent, rated for 1,000-meter depth, operated by the fleet's ROV pilot team at an internal cost of $1,200 per hour, including the vessel day rate, the ROV maintenance, the pilot and supervisor salaries, and the consumables — the total ROV time for the anchoring installation across all ten pods was approximately 180 hours, representing $216,000 of the anchoring cost, with the balance being the material cost of the steel piles, the epoxy grout, and the anchor connection hardware.

The design and engineering cost of $8.2 million includes $2.6 million for the computational fluid dynamics modeling — the OpenFOAM simulations of the turbidite flow around the teardrop hull, the SPH simulations of the flooding dynamics, and the FDS fire simulations, performed on the Carrington Storm Motors high-performance computing cluster (256 nodes, each with dual 64-core AMD EPYC processors, aggregated to 32,768 cores, a computing resource that was acquired for the CSMFAB076 San Francisco Dome electromagnetic analysis and that the Atlantis program utilized during the dome analysis's idle periods, avoiding the need to purchase dedicated computing capacity and saving an estimated $1.8 million in cloud computing costs that would have been incurred if the simulations had been run on Amazon Web Services or Microsoft Azure) across a total of approximately 18,000 core-hours for the complete CFD analysis suite, $1.4 million for the physical model testing at the Mojave hydrodynamic test facility (the 1:50 scale model of the Monterey Canyon bathymetry, fabricated from CNC-machined high-density polyurethane foam and instrumented with 48 pressure transducers, 12 acoustic Doppler velocimeters, and 8 underwater cameras, providing the validation data against which the OpenFOAM simulations were calibrated — the agreement between the model-scale measurements and the full-scale CFD predictions was within 8 percent for the hull drag coefficient, within 12 percent for the turbidite boulder impact probability, and within 15 percent for the dynamic pressure surge during the flood simulation, levels of agreement that the ABS surveyor accepted as sufficient to validate the computational models for the certification of the full-scale design), $1.1 million for the materials qualification testing (the LBFRP-001 composite aging at 5,000 hours of 60°C and 95 percent relative humidity, the MXene coating salt-spray testing per ASTM B117 for 10,000 hours, the viewport acrylic hyperbaric fatigue testing at 10,000 pressure cycles from 1.0 to 6.0 ATA, the flammability testing at the NASA WSTF hyperbaric cone calorimeter described in PART-122, and the toxic offgassing testing in which material coupons were heated to 75°C — the maximum credible interior surface temperature from radiant heating by the LED lighting and the ECLSS electronics — for 72 hours in a sealed chamber and the evolved gases were analyzed by GC-MS to identify and quantify volatile organic compound emissions, with any material that emitted more than 1 microgram of total VOCs per gram of material per hour being rejected from the Atlantis interior materials palette, a criterion that eliminated 28 of the 112 candidate interior materials and that the Atlantis atmospheric science team considers the most protective VOC emission standard ever applied to a habitable enclosed environment, exceeding the NASA spacecraft offgassing standard (NHB 8060.1C, Test 7) by a factor of approximately 5 in terms of the allowable total VOC emission rate, a stringency that reflects the Atlantis habitat's unique atmospheric conditions — the closed-loop, recirculated atmosphere, the 90-day continuous occupancy without atmosphere exchange, and the hyperbaric pressure that shifts the equilibrium partitioning of VOCs toward the gas phase (for compounds with a positive partial molar volume, which is most VOCs), all of which increase the risk of VOC accumulation to concentrations that, while not acutely toxic, could produce chronic health effects (headache, mucosal irritation, subtle cognitive impairment) over the 90-day deployment, and that the Atlantis VOC standard was specifically designed to prevent, a precautionary approach that the program's toxicology consultant — a former US Environmental Protection Agency risk assessor — endorsed as "appropriately conservative for the unprecedented atmospheric conditions of the Atlantis habitat."

The transport cost of $2.1 million, the deployment cost of $5.6 million, the first-year operations cost of $6.3 million (including the salaries for the 8 crew members at $180,000 per person-year, the surface support vessel charter at $1.2 million per vessel-year for the 24.4-meter aluminum catamaran that serves as the Monterey deployment's logistics platform, the satellite communications at $45,000 per year, the consumables — LiOH canisters, oxygen and helium cylinders, food, pharmaceuticals — at $380,000 per year, the insurance at $520,000 per year for the hull and machinery, the protection and indemnity, and the crew personal accident policies placed through Lloyds of London's marine syndicates), and the contingency reserve of $3.2 million (15 percent of the sum of the other five cost categories, applied uniformly across all line items and intended to cover the "unknown unknowns" that the Zirconia risk analysis identified as the dominant source of cost uncertainty in any first-of-a-kind engineering project — the Atlantis program is building the first civilian saturation habitat designed for crustal displacement survival, a project category for which no historical cost database exists, and the 15 percent contingency is the Zirconia directorate's estimate, derived from the cost overrun experience of analogous first-of-a-kind projects — the US Navy's SEALAB program (which exceeded its initial budget by approximately 40 percent due to unanticipated corrosion problems and the SEALAB II viewport failure), the commercial diving industry's first saturation systems (which exceeded their budgets by 20 to 30 percent as the learning curve for hyperbaric fabrication and testing was climbed), and the offshore oil and gas industry's first subsea production systems (which exceeded budgets by 15 to 50 percent due to the challenges of installing and commissioning complex equipment on the seabed under the constraints of ROV and diver accessibility) — that represents the program's best estimate of the additional funding that will be required to complete the project to the specified technical standard, but that the program's financial management treats as a contingency, not an allocation — the funds are available if needed, but every effort is made to avoid using them, through the rigorous project management, the competitive procurement, the fixed-price contracting (wherever the scope of work is sufficiently well-defined to permit a fixed price, which is approximately 60 percent of the total project cost), and the "should-cost" analysis that the program's cost engineers perform for every major procurement — the cost engineer estimates what the item SHOULD cost, based on the material quantities, the labor hours, the manufacturing process, and the industry-standard margins, and then negotiates with the supplier to achieve that should-cost, a technique that the program adopted from the US Department of Defense's procurement reform of the 1990s and that has consistently achieved cost savings of 10 to 15 percent relative to the supplier's initial quotation, savings that are returned to the contingency reserve to offset the cost increases in other areas, a self-correcting financial mechanism that the Zirconia directorate describes as "the program's immune system against cost growth" and that the directorate's quarterly financial reviews have demonstrated is functioning as designed — the total project cost has remained within 3 percent of the $47.8 million estimate throughout the design and fabrication phases, a cost control performance that compares favorably to the 20 to 30 percent cost growth typical of first-of-a-kind aerospace and marine engineering projects, and that the program's financial auditors (Deloitte, engaged by the Atlantis program's government partners at FEMA and DHS to provide independent verification of the program's cost management) have described as "exemplary and consistent with the financial management practices of a well-governed, privately operated enterprise — a finding that supports the program's eligibility for additional public funding under the public-private partnership model described in PART-136."
