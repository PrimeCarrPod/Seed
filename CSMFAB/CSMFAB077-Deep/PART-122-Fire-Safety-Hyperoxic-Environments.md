## 1. THE HYPEROXIC FIRE HAZARD: COMBUSTION PHYSICS AT ELEVATED OXYGEN PARTIAL PRESSURE AND TOTAL AMBIENT PRESSURE

Fire in a hyperoxic, hyperbaric environment is the most rapidly lethal non-biological hazard facing the occupants of the Atlantis-class subsea habitation modules, and it is not an exaggeration to state — as the Apollo 1 Accident Review Board stated in its 1967 report to the NASA Administrator, a document that fundamentally reshaped the fire safety philosophy of every subsequent crewed spacecraft and that serves, alongside the 2017 recollection of Apollo 7 astronaut Walter Cunningham who personally witnessed the aftermath of the Apollo 1 fire and spent the subsequent decades of his life advocating for spacecraft fire safety reforms that were partially but incompletely adopted, and the 2019 fire aboard the Russian AS-31 Losharik nuclear-powered deep-submergence vehicle in which 14 naval officers perished from combustion product inhalation within a hyperbaric chamber environment whose oxygen partial pressure accelerated the fire beyond any possibility of manual suppression, as the foundational catastrophic case studies that the Atlantis fire safety protocol was designed to prevent rather than to survive — that a fire in a 5.5-ATA environment enriched to a PiO₂ of 1.10 ATA will propagate at a rate that renders conventional fire suppression techniques, including hand-held extinguishers, fire blankets, and water-based sprinkler systems, completely ineffective, and that the only viable strategy for fire safety in such an environment is the exhaustive elimination of ignition sources, the specification of materials whose combustion characteristics have been characterized at the habitat's operating pressure and oxygen concentration, and the provision of an automated, whole-habitat fire suppression system that can extinguish a fire within seconds of detection, before the exponential growth phase of the combustion reaction has consumed so much of the habitat's oxygen supply that the occupants — even those in other compartments protected by closed hatches — asphyxiate before they can don emergency breathing apparatus and evacuate.

The physics of combustion in a hyperbaric, hyperoxic atmosphere differs from the familiar combustion behavior at sea level in three fundamental respects, each of which increases the fire hazard by orders of magnitude relative to the surface baseline and each of which demands specific engineering countermeasures that are integrated into the Atlantis pod's design. First, the rate of a heterogeneous combustion reaction — the oxidation of a solid fuel such as a polymer textile, a wooden structural element, or a hydrocarbon-based insulation material — is limited at normobaric oxygen concentrations primarily by the rate at which oxygen molecules can diffuse through the boundary layer of stagnant gas that surrounds every burning surface, a diffusion-limited regime in which the combustion rate is approximately proportional to the oxygen concentration in the ambient atmosphere raised to a power between 0.5 and 1.0 depending on the flow conditions (forced convection producing the higher exponent, natural convection producing the lower exponent, with the transition between regimes occurring at a Grashof number — the ratio of buoyancy forces to viscous forces in the boundary layer, Gr = gβΔTL³/ν², where g is gravitational acceleration, β is the coefficient of thermal expansion, ΔT is the temperature difference between the burning surface and the ambient gas, L is the characteristic length scale of the burning object, and ν is the kinematic viscosity of the gas — of approximately 10⁹, above which the flow transitions from laminar natural convection to turbulent natural convection with correspondingly higher mass transfer rates). At the Atlantis Monterey site's PiO₂ of 1.10 ATA — which is equivalent to an oxygen mole fraction of 0.20 in a total pressure of 5.5 ATA, but represents an oxygen DENSITY (mass per unit volume of oxygen) that is 5.5 times higher than the oxygen density in surface air at 1.0 ATA and 21 percent oxygen, because the density of an ideal gas is directly proportional to its partial pressure at constant temperature (ρ_O₂ = P_O₂ × M_O₂ / (R × T), where M_O₂ is the molar mass of oxygen, 32 g/mol, R is the universal gas constant, and T is the absolute temperature) — the mass flux of oxygen to the burning surface increases by a factor of approximately 5.5 relative to the surface baseline, and because the combustion rate is directly proportional to the oxygen mass flux in the diffusion-limited regime, the fire will consume fuel at approximately 5.5 times the rate observed in surface air, meaning that a fire that would take 55 seconds to propagate from ignition to flashover in a surface room will reach flashover — the point at which the radiant heat flux from the fire raises all exposed combustible surfaces in the compartment to their autoignition temperature simultaneously, producing a near-instantaneous transition from a localized fire to a fully involved compartment fire — in approximately 10 seconds in the Atlantis habitat, a timeline that is shorter than the human reaction time for even a trained, alert occupant to recognize the fire, locate an extinguisher, and begin suppression, and that is comparable to the response time of the automated fire detection and suppression system described in Section 4, which must detect the fire within 1 to 2 seconds of ignition and initiate suppression within 3 to 5 seconds to prevent flashover in a hyperbaric environment where the fire growth rate is accelerated by the five-fold increase in oxygen mass flux.

Second, the autoignition temperature of combustible materials — the minimum temperature at which a material will spontaneously ignite in the absence of an external ignition source, a property that depends on the chemical composition of the material, the oxygen concentration, and the total pressure, and that is measured by standardized test methods such as ASTM E659 ("Standard Test Method for Autoignition Temperature of Chemicals") in which a small sample of the material is injected into a heated flask containing the test atmosphere and the temperature at which ignition occurs within a specified observation period (typically 10 minutes) is recorded — decreases as oxygen concentration increases, because the activation energy for the initial oxidation reaction that initiates the combustion chain mechanism is more easily supplied at higher oxygen concentrations, where the frequency of reactive collisions between oxygen molecules and the fuel's surface molecules increases proportionally to the oxygen partial pressure. For typical organic polymers — the plastics, textiles, and elastomers that constitute the majority of the non-metallic materials in a habitation environment — the autoignition temperature in air (21 percent oxygen at 1.0 ATA) ranges from approximately 300°C for cellulosic materials such as cotton and paper, through 350 to 450°C for common thermoplastics such as polyethylene, polypropylene, and polystyrene, to 500 to 600°C for thermally stable engineering polymers such as polyether ether ketone (PEEK), polyimide (Kapton), and polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE, Teflon), and these values decrease by approximately 50 to 100°C when the oxygen concentration is increased to 30 percent at 1.0 ATA total pressure, a reduction that has been documented in the flame-retardant literature and that is the reason why the International Space Station's atmosphere — which is maintained at 1.0 ATA and 21 percent oxygen, identical to sea level, despite the mass penalty of carrying nitrogen as a diluent gas — is specifically designed to avoid the elevated oxygen concentration that the Apollo program used in its early capsule designs (100 percent oxygen at 0.34 ATA, which was selected to simplify the environmental control system by eliminating the need for a nitrogen supply and nitrogen partial pressure sensor, a design decision that, combined with the use of Velcro fastener strips, nylon netting, and polyurethane foam padding — materials that burned with explosive violence in the pure-oxygen atmosphere of the Apollo 1 command module during the January 27, 1967 plugs-out test, killing astronauts Gus Grissom, Ed White, and Roger Chaffee within approximately 30 seconds of the first report of fire — produced the worst outcome in the history of American spaceflight and prompted the comprehensive redesign that resulted in the Apollo command module's post-fire atmosphere specification of 60 percent oxygen and 40 percent nitrogen at launch, transitioning to 100 percent oxygen at 0.34 ATA only after reaching orbit when the reduced total pressure would reduce the fire hazard to acceptable levels despite the elevated oxygen concentration). At the Atlantis Monterey PiO₂ of 1.10 ATA — which, to reiterate, represents an oxygen concentration of 20 percent by volume at 5.5 ATA total pressure, not an elevated oxygen concentration but rather an elevated oxygen DENSITY — the autoignition temperature reduction effect is governed by the oxygen density rather than the oxygen mole fraction, because the combustion reaction rate depends on the number of oxygen molecules per unit volume available for reaction, not on the proportion of oxygen molecules in the gas mixture, and the five-fold increase in oxygen density at the Monterey site relative to surface air produces an autoignition temperature reduction that is comparable to that produced by an oxygen mole fraction of approximately 50 to 60 percent at 1.0 ATA (a concentration that would support near-explosive combustion of essentially all organic materials), although the quantitative prediction of autoignition temperature as a function of oxygen density and total pressure requires detailed chemical kinetic modeling of the specific fuel-oxygen reaction system — the Semenov thermal explosion theory, which models autoignition as the point at which the rate of heat generation by the exothermic oxidation reaction exceeds the rate of heat loss to the surroundings (dQ_gen/dt = V × ΔH_c × A × exp(-E_a/RT) × [O₂]ⁿ, where V is the volume of the reacting system, ΔH_c is the heat of combustion per mole of fuel, A is the pre-exponential factor of the Arrhenius rate equation, E_a is the activation energy, R is the gas constant, T is the absolute temperature, [O₂] is the oxygen concentration, and n is the reaction order with respect to oxygen, typically between 0.5 and 2.0 depending on the reaction mechanism; and dQ_loss/dt = h × S × (T - T_ambient), where h is the convective heat transfer coefficient, S is the surface area for heat loss, and T_ambient is the ambient temperature; the autoignition condition is dQ_gen/dt = dQ_loss/dt and d(dQ_gen/dt)/dT > d(dQ_loss/dt)/dT, the condition for thermal runaway) — provides a theoretical framework for predicting autoignition temperature as a function of oxygen concentration, with the critical insight that the oxygen concentration term appears in the heat generation rate equation and therefore directly influences the temperature at which the Semenov criterion for ignition is satisfied, such that doubling the oxygen concentration reduces the autoignition temperature by an amount that depends logarithmically on the oxygen concentration through the Arrhenius exponential temperature dependence, a relationship that the Atlantis materials qualification program has incorporated into its testing protocol: every material proposed for use in the habitat interior must undergo autoignition testing at the habitat's maximum design oxygen density (equivalent to 5.5 ATA total pressure with 20 percent oxygen, or any equivalent pressure-concentration combination that produces the same oxygen density, such as 2.75 ATA with 40 percent oxygen, which is more practical for the Mojave hyperbaric test facility's chamber pressure limitations) and must demonstrate an autoignition temperature that exceeds the maximum credible hot-surface temperature in the habitat (150°C for electronic component surfaces, 200°C for the galley cooking equipment surfaces, and 300°C for the lithium iron phosphate battery pack surfaces under worst-case thermal runaway conditions with the battery management system attempting to discharge the affected cell to prevent thermal propagation) by a safety margin of at least 100°C, a specification that effectively restricts the Atlantis interior materials to a palette of thermally stable, intrinsically flame-resistant polymers, inorganic fibers, and metal components that would be considered excessively conservative for surface construction but that are essential for hyperbaric fire safety.

Third, and most insidiously, the elevated total pressure in the Atlantis habitat increases the volumetric heat release rate of a fire — the rate at which the combustion reaction liberates thermal energy, measured in kilowatts per cubic meter of the fire compartment volume — by a factor approximately proportional to the total pressure raised to a power between 0.5 and 1.0, depending on whether the combustion is diffusion-limited (exponent closer to 0.5, because the buoyancy-driven entrainment of ambient gas into the fire plume is weaker at higher densities, partially offsetting the increased oxygen mass flux) or kinetically limited (exponent closer to 1.0, because the rate of the gas-phase oxidation reactions in the flame zone is directly proportional to the concentration of reactants, which increases linearly with total pressure). For a well-ventilated compartment fire in the diffusion-limited regime — the regime that would apply to a fire in the Atlantis habitat's main living compartment, which is ventilated at a rate of approximately 5 air changes per hour by the ECLSS circulation fans to maintain uniform temperature and gas composition — the volumetric heat release rate at 5.5 ATA total pressure is approximately 2.5 to 3.5 times higher than the heat release rate of an identical fuel package burning in surface air at 1.0 ATA, an acceleration that, combined with the five-fold increase in oxygen mass flux described previously, produces a fire that reaches a given heat release rate (and therefore a given compartment temperature) approximately 10 to 20 times faster than a surface fire in the same compartment geometry, a timeline compression that transforms fire from a manageable emergency with the possibility of manual suppression and orderly evacuation into an existential threat against which the ONLY effective defense is the combination of ignition source elimination (the primary prevention strategy) and automated, ultra-fast suppression (the secondary mitigation strategy), because the fire growth rate in the hyperbaric environment exceeds the human cognitive and physical response capabilities by such a margin that any dependence on human action is, as the Apollo 1 review board found in its analysis of the command module fire, "a lethal design philosophy incompatible with the demonstrated combustion characteristics of materials in the operational environment."

The implications of this combustion physics analysis for the Atlantis fire safety protocol are stark and unambiguous: (a) the habitat interior must be designed and maintained to eliminate ALL potential ignition sources, a requirement that extends beyond the obvious elimination of open flames (no smoking, no candles, no gas stoves — the galley uses an induction cooktop that heats cookware directly through electromagnetic induction at 20 to 50 kHz, producing no hot surface except the bottom of the cookware itself, which is regulated to a maximum temperature of 220°C by a failsafe thermostatic cutoff switch that disconnects the induction coil power if the cookware surface temperature exceeds 240°C, a safety margin that accounts for the autoignition temperature reduction at 5.5 ATA) to include the rigorous control of electrical energy — the Atlantis electrical system operates entirely at 24 volts DC for interior circuits, a voltage that is below the 30-volt threshold at which electrical arcing becomes capable of sustaining an arc across a gap of more than 0.1 millimeters (the Paschen minimum for air at 5.5 ATA occurs at a lower voltage than at 1.0 ATA because the increased gas density at higher pressure shifts the Paschen curve — which plots the breakdown voltage as a function of the product of pressure and electrode gap distance, with a characteristic minimum at the Paschen minimum where the electron mean free path is optimal for ionization multiplication — but even at 5.5 ATA, the minimum breakdown voltage for a 0.1-millimeter gap in air is approximately 300 volts, well above the Atlantis 24-volt distribution voltage, meaning that an electrical arc cannot be initiated by insulation failure or loose connection in the Atlantis interior electrical system, a design choice that eliminates the most common ignition source in terrestrial building fires — faulty electrical wiring — by design rather than by maintenance vigilance), and all electrical connectors, switches, relays, and circuit breakers are enclosed in hermetically sealed housings that are purged with nitrogen at 1.0 ATA relative to the habitat's ambient pressure (i.e., the enclosure interior is maintained at a pressure 1.0 ATA ABOVE the habitat's 5.5 ATA, for a total internal pressure of 6.5 ATA, with the overpressure supplied by a dedicated nitrogen supply from the habitat's gas storage and maintained by a pressure regulator that opens to admit nitrogen whenever the enclosure pressure falls below 6.3 ATA, the lower threshold that accounts for the slow leakage of nitrogen through the enclosure seals over time and ensures that any leak through the enclosure wall — such as a crack in a connector housing or a failed O-ring on a switch actuator — results in a flow of inert nitrogen FROM the enclosure INTO the habitat, rather than a flow of oxygen-rich habitat atmosphere INTO the enclosure where it could encounter a spark or hot spot and initiate combustion, a design principle known as "purge and pressurize" that is standard practice in the chemical process industry for electrical equipment in flammable gas atmospheres (IEC 60079-2, "Equipment protection by pressurized enclosure 'p'") and that has been adapted for the hyperbaric environment by increasing the enclosure overpressure to account for the elevated ambient pressure and the correspondingly higher leakage rate — the leakage rate through a given seal geometry is proportional to the square root of the differential pressure according to the orifice flow equation, so the nitrogen consumption rate for the purged enclosures at 5.5 ATA ambient pressure is approximately 1.5 times higher than it would be at 1.0 ATA for the same overpressure, a manageable increase that is accounted for in the habitat's nitrogen consumption budget (approximately 0.5 cubic meters of nitrogen at STP per day for enclosure purging, supplied from the same high-pressure nitrogen cylinders that provide the atmosphere diluent gas for the HELIOX mixture and the backup gas for the emergency breathing apparatus); (b) all materials in the habitat interior — structural elements, furnishings, textiles, insulation, cable sheathing, gaskets, sealants, adhesives, paints, and the clothing worn by the occupants — must be selected from materials whose combustion characteristics have been quantified at the habitat's operational pressure and oxygen density, using the test methods described in Section 3, and must meet flammability acceptance criteria that are substantially more stringent than those applied to terrestrial buildings, aircraft cabins, or even spacecraft — the NASA spacecraft materials flammability test (NASA-STD-6001, Test 1, "Upward Flame Propagation Test") is conducted at the maximum oxygen concentration expected during the mission (30 percent for the International Space Station, 100 percent for the Orion capsule during the in-orbit phase at reduced total pressure), but the Atlantis habitat's equivalent oxygen concentration — defined as the mole fraction of oxygen that would produce the same oxygen density at 1.0 ATA total pressure as the habitat's actual oxygen density — is approximately 110 percent (5.5 ATA × 21 percent O₂ = 115.5 percent O₂ equivalent at 1.0 ATA, although the increased total pressure also affects the combustion kinetics and the gas-phase heat transfer, so the "equivalent oxygen concentration" concept is an approximation rather than a rigorous equivalence), a concentration at which essentially all organic polymers will burn with near-explosive violence unless they are specifically formulated with flame-retardant additives (halogenated compounds such as brominated diphenyl ethers that interfere with the gas-phase radical chain reactions of combustion by releasing hydrogen bromide (HBr) and bromine radicals (Br•) that scavenge the hydroxyl radicals (OH•) and hydrogen radicals (H•) that propagate the combustion chain, or phosphorus-based compounds such as ammonium polyphosphate that promote char formation on the burning surface, creating a thermally insulating carbonaceous layer that reduces the heat flux from the flame to the underlying fuel and thereby reduces the rate of pyrolysis gas production that feeds the flame) at concentrations of 10 to 30 percent by weight, loadings that alter the material's mechanical properties, processing characteristics, and toxicological profile — halogenated flame retardants, in particular, have been restricted by the European Union's Restriction of Hazardous Substances (RoHS) Directive and the Registration, Evaluation, Authorisation and Restriction of Chemicals (REACH) Regulation due to their persistence in the environment, their bioaccumulation in the food chain, and their endocrine-disrupting and neurodevelopmental toxicity in humans, concerns that are amplified in the confined, recirculating atmosphere of an underwater habitat where any volatile compounds released from the materials — including the flame retardants themselves, their thermal degradation products, and the combustion products that would result from a fire despite the best efforts at prevention — will accumulate in the atmosphere and expose the occupants to chronic toxicological risk that, over a 90-day deployment, could produce adverse health effects ranging from endocrine disruption to carcinogenesis, a risk that the Atlantis material selection protocol addresses by preferring INTRINSICALLY flame-resistant materials — materials whose polymer backbone chemistry confers flame resistance without the need for additive flame retardants — over flame-retarded commodity polymers, and by specifying that any additive flame retardant must be non-halogenated (phosphorus-based, nitrogen-based, or inorganic — such as aluminum trihydroxide, Al(OH)₃, which decomposes endothermically at approximately 200°C, absorbing heat from the fire and releasing water vapor that dilutes the combustible gas mixture and cools the flame zone, a triple-action flame retardant mechanism — heat absorption, fuel dilution, and gas-phase cooling — that is effective, non-toxic, and compatible with the Atlantis habitat's closed-loop atmosphere because the water vapor released by the decomposition is simply condensed in the ECLSS humidity control system and returned to the habitat's potable water supply without introducing foreign contaminants into the closed-loop life support system); and (c) the automated fire suppression system must be capable of extinguishing a fire of any credible size and fuel type within 10 seconds of ignition — a suppression time that is achievable ONLY through a total-flooding gaseous fire suppression system that discharges an inert gas (carbon dioxide, nitrogen, argon, or a blend such as Inergen — 52 percent nitrogen, 40 percent argon, 8 percent carbon dioxide — into the entire habitat volume, reducing the oxygen concentration from the ambient 20 percent to below approximately 12 percent, the threshold at which most hydrocarbon-based combustion reactions become self-extinguishing because the heat released by the fraction of fuel molecules that successfully collide with oxygen molecules is insufficient to sustain the pyrolysis of additional fuel molecules, a threshold that varies with the specific fuel — cellulosic materials such as cotton and paper require an oxygen concentration below approximately 15 percent to self-extinguish, while hydrocarbon polymers such as polyethylene and polypropylene require below approximately 13 percent, and some oxygenated polymers such as polyoxymethylene (acetal, Delrin) can continue to smolder at oxygen concentrations as low as 8 percent because they contain oxygen in their polymer backbone that participates in the combustion reaction, a characteristic that makes acetal an unacceptable material for the Atlantis interior even though it is widely used in terrestrial engineering for its mechanical strength, dimensional stability, and machinability — and that the Atlantis materials specification explicitly prohibits for any interior application, favoring instead the inherently flame-resistant polymers described above, which self-extinguish at oxygen concentrations of 15 percent or higher and therefore provide a safety margin between the suppression system's target oxygen concentration and the concentration that would fail to extinguish the fire, ensuring that the suppression system — which is designed to reduce the habitat oxygen concentration to 10 percent within 10 seconds of activation and to hold it at or below 10 percent for 30 minutes to allow complete cooling of all hot surfaces that could reignite the fuel when the oxygen concentration is restored — will reliably extinguish any fire involving the approved interior materials without requiring the system to achieve an oxygen concentration so low that it would be immediately lethal to any occupants who are not wearing emergency breathing apparatus, a critical distinction because the suppression system MUST be survivable for the crew: the CO₂ flooding system described in Section 4 is designed to suppress the fire within seconds, after which the crew — who have donned their emergency breathing masks (full-face positive-pressure masks supplied with HELIOX breathing gas from a dedicated emergency gas supply that is isolated from the habitat's main gas storage and is activated automatically when the fire suppression system discharges, providing each occupant with approximately 30 minutes of breathing gas at the habitat's ambient pressure, sufficient time to don a full emergency egress suit — a one-piece garment fabricated from Nomex aramid fabric with an integrated hood, gloves, and booties, providing thermal protection against the residual heat from the extinguished fire and chemical protection against the acidic combustion products (hydrogen chloride from PVC cable insulation decomposition, hydrogen fluoride from PTFE decomposition, hydrogen cyanide from nitrogen-containing polymer decomposition such as polyurethane foam and nylon textiles, and carbon monoxide from incomplete combustion of all carbonaceous materials, with CO being the primary lethal agent in fires — CO binds to hemoglobin with an affinity approximately 240 times greater than oxygen, forming carboxyhemoglobin (COHb) that reduces the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity and shifts the hemoglobin-oxygen dissociation curve to the left, impairing oxygen unloading at the tissues, such that a COHb level of 30 to 40 percent produces severe headache, confusion, and loss of consciousness, and a level exceeding 50 percent is frequently fatal even with aggressive treatment including 100 percent oxygen breathing and hyperbaric oxygen therapy, which accelerates CO dissociation from hemoglobin by increasing the partial pressure gradient for CO elimination from the blood to the alveolar gas, reducing the COHb half-life from approximately 320 minutes breathing air at 1.0 ATA to approximately 80 minutes breathing 100 percent oxygen at 1.0 ATA, will have permeated the habitat atmosphere during the fire — and can then proceed to assess the damage, ventilate the habitat using the emergency purge system (the ECLSS is shut down during the fire suppression event to avoid recirculating the combustion products, and the emergency purge system floods the habitat with fresh HELIOX from the high-pressure storage banks while venting the contaminated atmosphere through the overpressure relief valve to the external seawater, a purge process that exchanges the habitat's entire gas volume — approximately 100 cubic meters at the ambient pressure of 5.5 ATA, containing approximately 550 cubic meters of gas at STP — in approximately 15 to 20 minutes at the maximum purge flow rate of 0.5 cubic meters per second at STP, a rate that is ## 2. IGNITION SOURCE INVENTORY AND CONTROL: THE ATLANTIS ELECTRICAL ISOLATION AND ENERGY MANAGEMENT PROTOCOL

The Atlantis ignition source control program begins with a systematic inventory of every device, system, and process within the habitat that could, under any credible failure scenario, produce a source of ignition — defined as any localized energy release exceeding approximately 0.1 millijoules (the minimum ignition energy for a stoichiometric hydrogen-air mixture at 1.0 ATA, which is the most easily ignited gas mixture encountered in the Atlantis environment because the hydrogen fuel cell produces and stores hydrogen gas at pressures up to 200 bar, and although the hydrogen storage and fuel cell are located in an externally mounted, pressure-isolated equipment bay that is separated from the main habitat volume by a water-filled isolation chamber — a 0.5-meter-thick annular water jacket that surrounds the fuel cell module and that absorbs any hydrogen leak by dissolving the hydrogen gas in water, where its concentration cannot exceed the saturation limit of approximately 1.6 milligrams per liter at 25°C and 5.5 ATA (Henry's Law constant for hydrogen in water at 25°C is approximately 7.8 × 10⁻⁶ mol/L·Pa, so at a hydrogen partial pressure of 1.0 ATA, the equilibrium dissolved hydrogen concentration is approximately 0.8 mg/L, or about 0.4 percent of the lower flammability limit of hydrogen in air, which is 4.0 percent by volume, meaning that even a catastrophic hydrogen leak into the water jacket would produce a dissolved hydrogen concentration far below the flammability limit and the hydrogen would slowly diffuse out of the water into the water jacket's vented headspace, which is purged with nitrogen at a rate of 0.1 cubic meters per hour to maintain a non-flammable atmosphere — any hydrogen that reaches the main habitat atmosphere would be at concentrations measured in parts per million, not percent, and would be detected by the solid-state hydrogen sensor in the ECLSS gas monitoring suite long before approaching the lower flammability limit, a multi-barrier defense that the Zirconia risk analysis rates as Category I (the highest level of confidence in risk mitigation, equivalent to the nuclear industry's "defense in depth" principle for reactor safety systems) because it requires the simultaneous failure of at least three independent barriers — the fuel cell module pressure boundary, the water jacket containment, and the nitrogen purge system — for a flammable hydrogen concentration to reach the habitat interior, a combination of failures whose probability, based on the documented failure rates of comparable pressure vessels and piping systems in the chemical process industry (approximately 10⁻⁶ to 10⁻⁷ per vessel-year for catastrophic rupture, and approximately 10⁻⁴ to 10⁻⁵ per flanged joint-year for significant leak, per the Center for Chemical Process Safety (CCPS) Process Equipment Reliability Database), is less than 10⁻¹² per year when the three barriers are modeled as independent failure events in a fault tree analysis, a level of reliability that is considered "incredible" in the probabilistic safety assessment framework and that therefore does not drive the overall fire risk calculation for the Atlantis habitat) and that could ignite a combustible material in the habitat interior. The ignition source inventory for the Atlantis pod is maintained as a living document, updated at the beginning and end of each 90-day deployment cycle, and reviewed by the Aegis C fire safety review board — composed of three Carrington Storm Motors senior engineers with expertise in hyperbaric fire protection, electrical systems safety, and materials flammability, plus an independent consultant from the NASA White Sands Test Facility (WSTF) hyperbaric materials flammability laboratory, which has been the primary test facility for spacecraft fire safety since the Apollo 1 fire and which possesses the specialized equipment — the "600 psia chamber," capable of testing materials and components at pressures up to 4.1 MPa (600 psia, equivalent to approximately 40 ATA) in oxygen concentrations from 0 to 100 percent, with instrumentation for measuring heat release rate, smoke production rate, toxic gas emission, and the minimum conditions for ignition, propagation, and self-extinguishment, providing the empirical data that underpin the NASA materials flammability database (NHB 8060.1, "Flammability, Odor, Offgassing, and Compatibility Requirements and Test Procedures for Materials in Environments that Support Combustion") and that serve as the benchmark against which the Atlantis materials qualification testing is calibrated — at each review cycle.

The specific ignition sources that the Atlantis design eliminates or controls include: (1) electrical arcs from circuit interruption — all switches, relays, and circuit breakers are of the hermetically sealed type, with contacts enclosed in a glass or ceramic envelope that is either evacuated (vacuum contactors for the high-voltage — 120 volts DC — circuits that power the ECLSS fans and the water pump motors, with vacuum being preferred over gas-filling for arc quenching in high-voltage DC circuits because a vacuum arc extinguishes when the current zero-crossing of the AC waveform would normally provide the interruption opportunity, a characteristic that does not exist in DC circuits where the current is continuous, requiring the vacuum interrupter to force the arc to extinguish by rapidly separating the contacts and relying on the vacuum's dielectric strength — approximately 20 kV/mm for a well-conditioned vacuum gap — to prevent the arc from restriking after the current is interrupted, a mechanism that is less reliable in DC than in AC but that is still the preferred technology for DC circuit interruption at voltages above approximately 50 volts because of its compact size, long operational life (typically 10,000 to 100,000 operations at rated current, compared to 1,000 to 5,000 operations for an air-break contactor of equivalent rating), and inherent containment of the arc plasma within the sealed vacuum envelope), or nitrogen-filled (for the 24-volt DC circuits, where the arc energy is too low to require vacuum interruption and the simpler, less expensive nitrogen-filled sealed relays — conforming to MIL-PRF-39016, "Relays, Electromagnetic, Established Reliability" — provide adequate arc suppression and complete isolation of the contact arc from the habitat atmosphere, with the nitrogen fill preventing contact oxidation and extending the relay's operational life to approximately 100,000 operations at rated current, which for the Atlantis application — where most relays cycle fewer than 10 times per day — corresponds to a service life exceeding the 50-year design life of the pod itself, a reliability margin that reflects the Aegis C principle of "design for the life of the facility, not for the maintenance interval"); (2) electrostatic discharge (ESD) — a spark produced by the sudden discharge of static electricity accumulated on an insulating surface or an electrically isolated conductor, with discharge energies ranging from approximately 0.1 millijoules for a discharge from a human finger after walking across a carpeted floor (the classic "doorknob shock" that is annoying but not dangerous in a normobaric, non-flammable atmosphere, but that could ignite a flammable gas mixture in a hyperbaric, oxygen-enriched environment because the minimum ignition energy for most hydrocarbon-air mixtures decreases as oxygen concentration increases, approximately as the inverse square of the oxygen mole fraction for the gas-phase chain-branching reactions that control ignition — a stoichiometric methane-air mixture has a minimum ignition energy of approximately 0.3 millijoules at 21 percent oxygen and 1.0 ATA, which decreases to approximately 0.1 millijoules at 30 percent oxygen and to less than 0.01 millijoules at 100 percent oxygen, a reduction of more than an order of magnitude that reflects the increased concentration of chain-carrying radicals (H•, O•, OH•) in the ignition kernel at higher oxygen concentrations, which accelerates the branching reactions that convert a localized spark into a self-sustaining flame) to several joules for a discharge from a large isolated conductor such as a metal cart or a piece of equipment that has been triboelectrically charged by the flow of an insulating fluid (hydraulic oil, for example) through a hose during system operation, the Atlantis protocol specifies that all interior surfaces — flooring, walls, furnishings, and equipment enclosures — must have a surface resistivity between 10⁶ and 10⁹ ohms per square (the "static-dissipative" range defined by ANSI/ESD S20.20, "Protection of Electrical and Electronic Parts, Assemblies and Equipment," which is sufficient to bleed static charge to ground at a rate that prevents accumulation beyond 100 volts — well below the 300 to 500 volts required to produce a perceptible spark ## 3. MATERIALS FLAMMABILITY QUALIFICATION: THE ATLANTIS FIRE TEST PROTOCOL AT 5.5 ATA AND 20 PERCENT OXYGEN

The materials flammability qualification program for the Atlantis habitat is based on a modified NASA-STD-6001 test protocol that has been adapted for the hyperbaric environment by the Carrington Storm Motors materials engineering team in collaboration with the NASA White Sands Test Facility (WSTF) hyperbaric materials flammability laboratory, and that incorporates four complementary test methods designed to characterize the ignition, flame spread, heat release, and combustion product toxicity of every material that will be exposed to the habitat atmosphere — the "interior atmosphere exposure" category, which includes all materials in the main habitat volume, the inter-pod tunnels, and the transfer module when it is pressurized to habitat pressure and not isolated for diver lock operations, and which excludes materials that are permanently immersed in seawater (the exterior hull surface, the anchoring system components, the ballast tank internals, the ROV docking interface, and the external lighting and sensor housings) and materials that are sealed within nitrogen-purged enclosures at all times (the electrical components within the purged enclosures described in Section 2, the battery cells within the BMS-monitored and cooled battery pack, and the interior of the medical lock when it is pressurized for DCS treatment and isolated from the main habitat atmosphere), because these excluded materials are not exposed to the habitat's oxygen-enriched atmosphere and therefore do not contribute to the fire hazard in the occupied volume.

Test Method 1 — the "Upward Flame Propagation Test" (NASA-STD-6001 Test 1, modified for hyperbaric operation) — evaluates the flammability of sheet, film, and textile materials in the configuration most likely to support upward flame spread: a vertically oriented specimen, 30.5 centimeters long by 6.4 centimeters wide (the standard NASA Test 1 specimen dimensions, which have been used for spacecraft materials flammability testing since the Apollo program and which provide a well-characterized baseline for comparing results across different materials and test facilities), exposed at its lower edge to a pilot flame — a 6.4-millimeter-diameter methane-air diffusion flame with a heat release rate of approximately 100 watts, which provides a standardized ignition source that is representative of a localized electrical fault, a hot surface contact, or an electrostatic discharge, the three ignition source categories that the Atlantis ignition source control program (Section 2) has identified as the most probable initiators of a hyperbaric fire — for a duration of 15 seconds, after which the pilot flame is removed and the specimen's self-extinguishment or continued burning is observed for a total test duration of 60 seconds. The test is conducted inside a pressure vessel capable of maintaining a total pressure of 5.5 ATA with an atmosphere of 20 percent oxygen and 80 percent helium (the HELIOX 8020 mixture used in the Monterey habitat, tested at the Monterey site's full operational pressure to account for the effects of total pressure on the combustion kinetics — the helium diluent, unlike the nitrogen diluent in the standard NASA Test 1 atmosphere (21 percent oxygen, 79 percent nitrogen at 1.0 ATA), has a higher thermal conductivity (0.152 W/m·K for helium versus 0.026 W/m·K for nitrogen at 1.0 ATA and 300 K) that increases the heat loss from the flame to the surrounding gas and thereby REDUCES the flame spread rate and the propensity for ignition relative to an equivalent nitrogen-diluted atmosphere, an effect that makes the Atlantis HELIOX atmosphere inherently safer than the Osa site's nitrox atmosphere (air at 4.0 ATA) for the same oxygen density, and one that the materials qualification program exploits by testing materials under the worst-case (nitrox) conditions rather than the HELIOX conditions, ensuring that a material that passes the nitrox test will also pass the HELIOX test by an additional margin — the testing conservatism is intentionally biased toward the more hazardous atmosphere to provide a safety margin that covers both the Monterey and Osa operational profiles with a single test protocol).

The acceptance criteria for Test 1 are derived from the NASA-STD-6001 requirements for materials used in habitable spacecraft compartments (Category 1 materials, the most stringent category, which requires that the material self-extinguish within 15 seconds of pilot flame removal, that the flame front not propagate more than 15.2 centimeters (6 inches) from the ignition point during the 60-second observation period, that no burning droplets fall from the specimen (a criterion that is particularly important in the microgravity spacecraft environment where burning droplets can float to other surfaces and ignite secondary fires, but that is also relevant to the Atlantis habitat because the habitat's 1-G gravitational environment — the pod is stationary on the seabed, not in free-fall — causes burning droplets to fall to the floor, where they can ignite accumulated debris, carpeting, or the occupant's clothing, a secondary ignition hazard that the materials qualification program addresses by requiring that no material in the habitat produce burning droplets during the Test 1 protocol, regardless of the material's orientation or the presence of a debris-collecting surface below the specimen), and that the total burn length — the distance from the specimen's lower edge to the farthest point of char, melting, or consumption — not exceed 15.2 centimeters (6 inches) from the ignition point, a criterion that ensures that even if a material does ignite, the fire will be contained to the immediate vicinity of the ignition source and will not spread along the material surface to involve a larger area, a containment strategy that works in concert with the automated fire suppression system to prevent the fire from exceeding the suppression system's design capacity (a fire involving more than approximately 1 square meter of burning surface area in the hyperbaric environment would release heat at a rate that the CO₂ flooding system — designed to extinguish a fire within a 100-cubic-meter compartment within 10 seconds — might not be able to suppress before the compartment reached flashover conditions, a limitation that the Aegis C fire safety analysis has accepted as the "maximum credible fire" and that the ignition source control and materials qualification programs are specifically designed to prevent from ever occurring, through the defense-in-depth strategy of ignition prevention, material non-combustibility, and rapid automated suppression, with no single barrier being relied upon as the sole defense against the maximum credible fire).

Test Method 2 — the "Oxygen Index Test" (ISO 4589-2, "Plastics — Determination of Burning Behaviour by Oxygen Index," modified for hyperbaric total pressure but conducted at the habitat's oxygen mole fraction of 20 percent) — measures the minimum concentration of oxygen, expressed as a volume percentage, that will support sustained combustion of a material specimen in a candle-like configuration (a vertically oriented rod or strip ignited at the top, with the flame propagating downward against the buoyant flow of hot combustion products, a configuration that minimizes the preheating of the unburned material by the rising hot gases and therefore provides the most conservative (lowest stable) measurement of the oxygen concentration required for sustained combustion). The standard oxygen index test is conducted at 1.0 ATA total pressure, with the oxygen concentration in the test atmosphere (typically a mixture of oxygen and nitrogen) varied to determine the limiting oxygen index (LOI) — the concentration at which the specimen burns for exactly 3 minutes or consumes exactly 5 centimeters of its length, whichever occurs first. The LOI value is a fundamental material property that characterizes the material's intrinsic flammability independent of the external heat flux, the specimen geometry, or the test scale, and that correlates well with the material's performance in the larger-scale flammability tests (Test 1, Test 3 — the "Cone Calorimeter Test" for heat release rate) as demonstrated by extensive inter-laboratory validation studies. For the Atlantis materials qualification, the LOI test is conducted at 5.5 ATA total pressure with an atmosphere of 20 percent oxygen in helium (or in nitrogen for the Osa site qualification — materials that will be used at both sites must pass the test in both atmospheres, because the nitrogen diluent produces a higher flame temperature and faster flame spread rate than the helium diluent due to the thermal conductivity difference described above, and materials that pass in nitrogen will pass in helium but NOT VICE VERSA, a directionality that the test protocol explicitly accounts for by requiring the nitrogen-diluent test for all materials that will be deployed at the Osa site, where the atmosphere is compressed air at 4.0 ATA with 79 percent nitrogen, a gas composition that is MORE hazardous from a fire propagation perspective than the Monterey HELIOX atmosphere despite the lower total pressure), and the LOI value is determined by varying the oxygen concentration in the test atmosphere (with the balance being helium or nitrogen) and identifying the concentration that just supports sustained combustion. The acceptance criterion is that the material's LOI must exceed 21 percent (the oxygen concentration in the habitat atmosphere) by a margin of at least 5 percentage points — an LOI of 26 percent or higher — at the tested total pressure, a margin that accounts for the uncertainties in the LOI measurement (±1 to 2 percent absolute oxygen concentration for inter-laboratory reproducibility), the potential for material aging and contamination to reduce the LOI over the habitat's 50-year design life (polymer degradation by hydrolysis, oxidation, and radiation — see PART-121 for the accelerated aging protocol — can reduce the LOI by 2 to 5 percentage points depending on the polymer chemistry and the degradation mechanism, a reduction that the qualification program addresses by testing aged specimens (5,000 hours at 60°C and 95 percent relative humidity, equivalent to 50 years at 10°C per the Arrhenius acceleration model with E_a = 45 kJ/mol) in addition to as-manufactured specimens, and requiring that the aged LOI still exceed the 26 percent threshold), and the effect of the specimen's thickness on the LOI (thinner specimens generally have lower LOI values because the heat loss from the flame zone to the underlying unburned material is greater for thin materials, reducing the flame temperature and making sustained combustion more difficult, a counterintuitive effect that means the most conservative LOI measurement is obtained on the THICKEST specimens, and the Atlantis test protocol specifies a specimen thickness of 3.2 millimeters — the maximum thickness for which the standard LOI test apparatus can accommodate the specimen — to ensure that the measured LOI is the lowest (most conservative) value for the material).

Test Method 3 — the "Cone Calorimeter Test" (ASTM E1354, "Standard Test Method for Heat and Visible Smoke Release Rates for Materials and Products Using an Oxygen Consumption Calorimeter," modified for hyperbaric pressure and for the Atlantis habitat's atmospheric composition) — measures the heat release rate, the total heat released, the effective heat of combustion, the mass loss rate, the time to ignition, the smoke production rate (expressed as the specific extinction area, a measure of the smoke's light-obscuring capacity per unit mass of material burned), and the production rates of carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide, and other combustion gases (hydrogen chloride, hydrogen cyanide, nitrogen oxides, sulfur dioxide, and hydrogen fluoride, for materials containing chlorine, nitrogen, sulfur, or fluorine atoms in their chemical structure) when a 100-millimeter by 100-millimeter specimen is exposed to a controlled radiant heat flux — typically 35 kW/m², which corresponds to the heat flux experienced by a surface exposed to a developing fire in an adjacent compartment, or 50 kW/m², which corresponds to the heat flux experienced by a surface in a fully developed compartment fire approaching flashover conditions — in the presence of a spark igniter to initiate combustion of the pyrolysis gases. The cone calorimeter is the primary tool for characterizing the fire performance of materials in terrestrial fire safety engineering, having been adopted by the International Maritime Organization (IMO) for the Fire Test Procedures (FTP) Code that governs materials used on passenger ships, and has been extensively validated in both the laboratory and full-scale fire tests, with the peak heat release rate (PHRR) measured in the cone calorimeter correlating well (correlation coefficient r² ≈ 0.8 to 0.9) with the time to flashover in room-corner fire tests. For the Atlantis qualification, the cone calorimeter test is conducted inside the hyperbaric pressure vessel at the full habitat pressure of 5.5 ATA (Monterey) or 4.0 ATA (Osa), with the test atmosphere consisting of the habitat's actual breathing mixture (HELIOX 8020 for Monterey, compressed air for Osa) and the radiant heat flux set to 50 kW/m² — the higher of the two standard heat fluxes, corresponding to the worst-case fire scenario — to ensure that any material that passes this test will not contribute significantly to fire growth or produce lethal concentrations of toxic combustion products in the confined habitat volume under any credible fire scenario. The acceptance criteria for Test 3 are: (a) the peak heat release rate (PHRR) must not exceed 100 kW/m², a threshold that is approximately one-fifth of the PHRR of a typical upholstered furniture item (PHRR ≈ 500 to 1,000 kW/m²) and that represents a material whose contribution to a compartment fire would be minimal even if multiple items of that material were burning simultaneously, because the total fire heat release rate is the sum of the burning items' individual heat release rates, and a compartment containing 20 items each producing 100 kW/m² over a burning area of 0.1 square meters would produce a total heat release rate of approximately 200 kW — a fire that is large enough to activate the smoke detection system and uncomfortable for the occupants but that is well within the suppression capacity of the CO₂ flooding system, which is designed to extinguish fires with heat release rates up to approximately 1,000 kW (the "design fire" for the suppression system, based on the worst-case ignition scenario: a localized electrical fire involving the insulation of a single power cable bundle, which contains approximately 2 kilograms of polyimide-insulated copper wire that, if ignited by a sustained electrical arc, would burn — despite polyimide's inherent flame resistance — with a peak heat release rate of approximately 800 kW, based on the cone calorimeter data for 3.2-millimeter-thick polyimide film (Kapton HN) tested at 50 kW/m² in a 20 percent oxygen, 80 percent helium atmosphere at 5.5 ATA, a test that was conducted specifically for the Atlantis program at the WSTF hyperbaric cone calorimeter facility and that represents, to the best of the authors' knowledge, the first published cone calorimeter data for any material at a total pressure exceeding 1.0 ATA — a research contribution that the WSTF fire scientists have submitted for publication in the journal Fire Safety Journal with the Atlantis engineering team as co-authors, in recognition of the novel test conditions and the potential applicability of the data to other hyperbaric environments, including future deep-sea mining habitats, submarine rescue chambers, and the pressurized crew compartments of deep-submergence rescue vehicles); (b) the total smoke production — quantified as the total smoke released per unit area of specimen, measured in square meters of smoke obscuration per square meter of specimen area — must not exceed 200 m²/m², a threshold that ensures that the smoke generated by a fire in the habitat, even if it involves multiple burning items, does not reduce the visibility in the habitat below approximately 1 meter (the distance at which an occupant can locate an exit, don a breathing mask, and navigate to the transfer module for evacuation), based on the correlation between the cone calorimeter smoke production and the full-scale compartment smoke density developed by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) through their fire research program, which has demonstrated that a smoke production rate of 200 m²/m² in the cone calorimeter corresponds to a smoke optical density of approximately 0.5 per meter in a room-corner fire test — a level at which a person with normal vision can see illuminated exit signs at a distance of approximately 2 to 3 meters, sufficient for wayfinding in a familiar environment such as the Atlantis pod interior that the crew knows intimately from their training and daily operations; (c) the carbon monoxide yield — the mass of CO produced per unit mass of material burned, expressed in grams of CO per gram of material — must not exceed 0.05 g/g, a value that is approximately one-tenth of the CO yield from burning polyurethane foam (0.5 to 0.7 g/g, depending on the foam density and the ventilation conditions), the material that has been responsible for the majority of fire fatalities in residential buildings because of its widespread use in upholstered furniture and bedding and its exceptionally high CO yield under smoldering and flaming combustion conditions, and that is banned from the Atlantis interior by the materials specification (all bedding, seating, and acoustic insulation materials must be fabricated from intrinsically flame-resistant materials — Nomex aramid fiber batt insulation, melamine foam acoustic panels, and silicone-impregnated fiberglass textiles for upholstery — that do not produce CO at yields exceeding 0.01 g/g under any combustion conditions, a specification that is verified by the cone calorimeter CO yield data at the habitat's operational pressure and atmosphere composition); and (d) the production of acid gases — hydrogen chloride (HCl), hydrogen fluoride (HF), hydrogen bromide (HBr), and hydrogen cyanide (HCN) — must not exceed 0.01 g/g for any individual acid gas, a threshold that ensures that even a fire involving 10 kilograms of material (a "large" fire by Atlantis standards, representing approximately one-tenth of the combustible mass in the main habitat compartment, which is dominated by non-combustible materials — the aluminum equipment racks, the steel pressure hull penetrations, the ceramic and glass laboratory equipment, and the water-filled tanks of the aquaculture system, representing approximately 80 percent of the total mass in the habitat, with the remaining 20 percent consisting of materials that are flammable only under the forced combustion conditions of the cone calorimeter and that are self-extinguishing under the ambient conditions of Test 1 — the upward flame propagation test — demonstrating that the Atlantis materials specification has successfully eliminated all materials that would support a self-sustaining fire in the habitat atmosphere, a claim that is supported by the complete absence of any Test 1 failure among the materials approved for the Atlantis interior, representing approximately 200 materials tested from an initial candidate pool of approximately 500 materials, of which approximately 300 were rejected for failure to meet one or more of the Test 1, Test 2, or Test 3 acceptance criteria, a rejection rate that the WSTF fire scientists have described as "the most rigorous materials flammability screening program in any industry, including aerospace, submarine, and offshore oil and gas, and one that should serve as a model for future hyperbaric habitation projects worldwide") would produce acid gas concentrations in the habitat atmosphere — at a ventilation rate of 5 air changes per hour, the steady-state concentration of a continuously emitted contaminant is C_ss = E / (Q × V), where E is the emission rate (grams per hour), Q is the ventilation rate (air changes per hour), and V is the compartment volume (cubic meters) — that remain below the Immediately Dangerous to Life or Health (IDLH) concentrations established by the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) for each compound: 50 ppm for HCl (IDLH), 30 ppm for HF, 30 ppm for HBr, and 50 ppm for HCN, ensuring that the crew — who will be wearing the emergency breathing masks described in Section 1 from the moment of fire detection — can survive in the habitat atmosphere for the 15 to 20 minutes required for the emergency purge system to reduce the contaminant concentrations to safe levels, and that even in the worst case of a crew member who is unable to don a breathing mask (because they are unconscious, trapped by debris, or otherwise incapacitated), the contaminant concentrations will not exceed the lethal concentration for 50 percent of the exposed population (LC₅₀) for the estimated exposure duration, a level of protection that the Zirconia risk analysis has modeled using the NIST Fire Dynamics Simulator (FDS) — a computational fluid dynamics code that solves the Navier-Stokes equations for low-speed, thermally driven flow with an emphasis on smoke and heat transport from fires — adapted for the hyperbaric environment by modifying the gas properties (density, thermal conductivity, specific heat capacity, and diffusivity) to reflect the HELIOX atmosphere at 5.5 ATA and by incorporating the increased combustion rate and the altered toxic gas yields from the cone calorimeter data at the habitat's operating conditions, producing a fire simulation that the Atlantis fire safety team has used to optimize the placement of the CO₂ discharge nozzles, the smoke detectors, and the emergency breathing mask storage locations to minimize the time from ignition to suppression and to maximize the survival probability of all occupants for every credible fire scenario.

## 4. THE CARBON DIOXIDE TOTAL-FLOODING FIRE SUPPRESSION SYSTEM: DESIGN, CAPACITY, AND OPERATION

The Atlantis automated fire suppression system is a total-flooding carbon dioxide (CO₂) system conforming to the design principles of NFPA 12, "Standard on Carbon Dioxide Extinguishing Systems" (2018 Edition), adapted for the hyperbaric environment and for the unique requirements of a sealed underwater habitat where the discharge of the suppression agent into the occupied volume presents an asphyxiation hazard that must be balanced against the greater and more immediate hazard of uncontrolled fire — a risk tradeoff that the NFPA 12 standard explicitly addresses for occupied spaces (Section 4.3.2 of NFPA 12 requires that CO₂ total-flooding systems protecting occupied spaces incorporate a pre-discharge alarm with a time delay sufficient for personnel evacuation, typically 30 to 60 seconds, and that the CO₂ concentration in the protected space after discharge not exceed the concentration that would be immediately lethal — approximately 17 percent CO₂ by volume at 1.0 ATA, corresponding to a partial pressure of 0.17 ATA — for a duration exceeding the time required for personnel to evacuate, which NFPA 12 specifies as approximately 1 minute for a person walking at a normal pace from the farthest point in the protected space to the nearest exit, a specification that is based on the inhalation toxicity data for CO₂, which at concentrations of 7 to 10 percent produces hyperventilation, headache, dizziness, and confusion within several minutes, at 10 to 15 percent produces loss of consciousness within approximately 1 minute, and at concentrations exceeding 17 percent produces loss of consciousness within several breaths and death from asphyxiation within minutes if not removed from the CO₂-enriched atmosphere). The Atlantis CO₂ system departs from the NFPA 12 pre-discharge delay requirement in one critical respect: in the hyperbaric environment, a 30-second pre-discharge delay would allow a fire to grow from a localized ignition to a fully developed compartment fire that is no longer suppressible by the CO₂ system — the fire growth rate calculation described in Section 1 demonstrates that the time from ignition to flashover in the Atlantis habitat is approximately 10 seconds, making a 30-second pre-discharge alarm WORSE than useless, because it would alarm the crew (useful) but then allow the fire to grow beyond the suppression system's capacity before the CO₂ discharged (catastrophically useless), resulting in the worst possible outcome: the crew is alerted to the emergency but cannot escape because the fire has already blocked their path to the transfer module, and the suppression system, when it finally discharges, is insufficient to extinguish the fully developed fire, leaving the crew trapped in a burning, oxygen-depleted habitat with no viable options for survival. The Atlantis fire suppression philosophy therefore REJECTS the NFPA 12 pre-discharge delay and instead specifies an instantaneous discharge upon fire detection — the CO₂ is released from the storage cylinders and floods the habitat within approximately 3 seconds of the fire detection system's alarm signal, a response time that is achievable because the CO₂ is stored as a liquid at ambient temperature (approximately 20°C) and at its saturation pressure of approximately 5.7 MPa (57 bar, or 830 psig) in six high-pressure cylinders, each with a water capacity of 50 liters and a CO₂ fill mass of 35 kilograms, for a total CO₂ storage of 210 kilograms, distributed through a piping network to 12 discharge nozzles strategically positioned throughout the habitat — four nozzles in the main living compartment (each discharging horizontally at floor level, where the CO₂ — which is approximately 1.5 times denser than the HELIOX atmosphere at 5.5 ATA, because the density of CO₂ at 5.5 ATA and 20°C is approximately 1.5 × (44 g/mol / (4 g/mol × 0.8 + 32 g/mol × 0.2)) = 1.5 × (44 / 9.6) ≈ 6.9 times the density of the HELIOX atmosphere, a density ratio that ensures the CO₂ will rapidly fill the compartment from the floor upward, displacing the lighter HELIOX atmosphere and extinguishing any fire at floor level, where most fires originate, within seconds of discharge), two nozzles in the galley compartment (discharging downward from the ceiling, where the CO₂ will flood the area around the induction cooktop and the galley appliances, the most probable location of a fire involving cooking oil, overheated food, or an electrical appliance malfunction), two nozzles in the sleeping quarters (discharging at floor level to flood the compartment while the occupants are sleeping — an automatic activation that is triggered by the smoke detection system's alarm signal, regardless of whether any occupant is awake to confirm the fire, because a fire in the sleeping quarters is the most lethal scenario due to the occupants' reduced state of alertness and the potential for smoke inhalation to incapacitate them before they can awaken and don breathing masks, and the CO₂ discharge itself — while an asphyxiation hazard — is a LESSER hazard than the smoke and toxic gases from a fire that is allowed to grow for even a few seconds in the hyperbaric environment, a risk tradeoff that the Zirconia safety analysis has quantified using a probabilistic model that assigns a survival probability of 0.99 to a sleeping occupant exposed to a CO₂ discharge (because the cold CO₂ gas — expanding from the liquid state at -20°C at the nozzle — produces an audible hiss and a thermal shock that will awaken even a deeply sleeping occupant, and the occupant can don their bedside emergency breathing mask — which is stored in a quick-release bracket directly above each bunk, accessible within 2 seconds of awakening — before the CO₂ concentration in the breathing zone reaches the incapacitating threshold of approximately 10 percent, which requires approximately 15 to 20 seconds at the Atlantis CO₂ discharge rate because the CO₂ must mix with the ambient atmosphere before reaching the breathing zone at bunk level, where the HELIOX atmosphere's stratification — CO₂ at the floor, HELIOX at the ceiling — provides a time buffer during which the breathing zone remains largely HELIOX as the CO₂ layer gradually deepens from the floor) versus a survival probability of approximately 0.05 for a sleeping occupant exposed to a fire that is allowed to burn for 30 seconds before CO₂ discharge (because the hyperoxic fire's rapid growth will fill the compartment with toxic smoke and deplete the oxygen to lethal levels within approximately 20 to 30 seconds of ignition, as predicted by the FDS simulations — the occupant would likely be incapacitated by smoke inhalation before the CO₂ discharged and, even if they survived the smoke, would then face the CO₂ asphyxiation hazard, with the combined probability of surviving both hazards being less than 5 percent due to the multiplicative effect of two consecutive life-threatening events), demonstrating that instantaneous CO₂ discharge, despite its own hazards, is the survival-maximizing strategy for the Atlantis hyperbaric fire scenario); two nozzles in the inter-pod tunnel segments (discharging at floor level along the tunnel's length, designed to create a bidirectional CO₂ flow that fills the tunnel from both ends simultaneously, extinguishing any fire in the tunnel — which could block the evacuation route between pods — within seconds of detection, and providing a survivable atmosphere in the tunnel for any crew member who is transiting the tunnel at the time of the fire, with the understanding that the tunnel's small cross-sectional area (approximately 3.1 square meters for a 2-meter-diameter circular cross-section) means that the CO₂ will fill the tunnel from floor to ceiling more rapidly than it fills the larger habitat compartments, and the crew member must therefore proceed immediately to the nearest pod — whichever is closest — and seal the tunnel hatch behind them, a procedure that is drilled during the emergency response training described in PART-127 and that is supported by the tunnel's emergency lighting system, which activates automatically upon fire detection and provides illuminated directional arrows pointing to the nearest pod hatch); and two nozzles in the transfer module (discharging at floor level, designed to flood the module during the worst-case scenario of a fire occurring while a diver is decompressing in the module — a scenario that the Atlantis fire safety analysis identifies as the most challenging to manage because the diver cannot immediately evacuate the module to the main habitat (the module is at a lower pressure than the habitat and the interconnecting hatch cannot be opened without equalizing pressure, a process that takes approximately 3 to 5 minutes for a pressure differential of more than 0.5 ATA, during which time a fire would destroy the module and kill its occupant) and cannot evacuate to the sea (because the decompression obligation would cause fatal DCS), leaving the diver trapped in a burning decompression chamber with no viable escape route — unless the CO₂ system extinguishes the fire within seconds, after which the diver remains in the module breathing from the BIBS mask (which supplies breathing gas from the habitat's gas storage, independent of the module's atmosphere) while the module's contaminated atmosphere is purged through the overboard vent, a post-fire purge that is automatically initiated by the fire suppression control system as soon as the CO₂ discharge is complete and the fire is confirmed extinguished (by the absence of a temperature rise in the module's thermocouple array and the return of the smoke detector signal to its normal background level), and that replaces the module's atmosphere with fresh HELIOX within approximately 10 minutes, after which the diver can remove the BIBS mask and resume normal decompression activities — an operational scenario that the Atlantis fire safety team has modeled using a combined FDS-hyperbaric decompression simulation that tracks the fire's growth, the CO₂ discharge and mixing, the diver's decompression status — the tissue inert gas tensions in each of the 16 Bühlmann compartments — and the post-fire purge and atmosphere restoration, confirming that the diver can survive the fire, the CO₂ discharge, and the post-fire atmosphere restoration without exceeding the M-values for any tissue compartment, because the BIBS mask provides a continuous supply of normoxic HELIOX at the module's ambient pressure, maintaining the diver's decompression profile exactly as if the fire had not occurred, a design feature that the Aegis C verification program identifies as one of the "hidden requirements" — requirements that are not obvious from the top-level specifications (the transfer module must serve as a decompression chamber, and the fire suppression system must protect all occupied volumes) but that emerge from the interaction of the subsystems and that, if not identified and resolved during design, would produce a catastrophic failure during operation, and that the Atlantis systems engineering team identified through a structured hazard analysis technique known as a "functional failure modes and effects analysis" (F-FMEA) in which each function of each subsystem is systematically examined for failure modes, and the effects of each failure mode are propagated through the system's functional architecture to identify the combinations of failures that could produce a hazardous or catastrophic outcome, a technique that the Aegis C program adopted from the aerospace industry's safety analysis methodology (SAE ARP4761, "Guidelines and Methods for Conducting the Safety Assessment Process on Civil Airborne Systems and Equipment") and that has been instrumental in identifying and resolving dozens of hidden requirements that would otherwise have emerged during operational testing, potentially with fatal consequences.

The CO₂ storage cylinders are located in an externally mounted, pressure-isolated equipment bay — the same bay that houses the hydrogen fuel cell and the high-pressure gas storage for the breathing atmosphere, but in a separate sub-compartment that is isolated from the fuel cell compartment by a water-filled bulkhead (the same water jacket design described in Section 2 for hydrogen containment, applied here to provide a thermal barrier between the CO₂ cylinders and any heat source that could cause the cylinders to overpressurize and rupture — although CO₂ cylinders are protected by frangible disc pressure relief devices that rupture at a pressure of approximately 20 MPa (200 bar, or 3,000 psig), well above the cylinder's normal operating pressure of 5.7 MPa, and that vent the cylinder contents to the surrounding seawater, where the CO₂ dissolves harmlessly without posing a toxicity hazard to the marine environment at the concentrations that would result from the venting of a single cylinder — approximately 35 kilograms of CO₂ dissolved in the approximately 10⁶ cubic meters of seawater within a 100-meter radius of the pod (the CO₂ would be diluted by turbulent diffusion to concentrations of approximately 0.035 milligrams per liter, a level that is undetectable against the natural background variability of dissolved CO₂ in coastal seawater, which ranges from approximately 1.0 to 2.5 milligrams per liter in Monterey Bay depending on the season, the depth, and the proximity to upwelling zones that bring deep, CO₂-rich water to the surface, a natural variability that dwarfs the contribution of a single cylinder venting event and that supports the environmental analysis finding — see PART-142 — that the Atlantis habitat's CO₂ fire suppression system has a negligible environmental impact, consistent with the National Marine Fisheries Service's Biological Opinion on the Monterey Bay National Marine Sanctuary's research and monitoring activities, which permits the use of compressed gas systems in underwater habitats as long as the total annual gas release does not exceed 1,000 kilograms, a threshold that the Atlantis system — with a total CO₂ inventory of 210 kilograms and an expected discharge frequency of approximately once per 10 years (based on the predicted fire ignition rate of approximately 10⁻⁴ per pod-year from the fault tree analysis of the ignition source control system, multiplied by a factor of 10 to account for unpredicted ignition scenarios that the fault tree analysis does not capture — the "unknown unknowns" that are the bane of probabilistic risk assessment and that the Zirconia analysis addresses by applying a "conservatism factor" of 10 to all risk estimates, a factor that is intended to cover the uncertainty in the input data, the model simplifications, and the inherently unpredictable nature of human error and equipment malfunction in a complex, novel system operating in an extreme environment, and that results in a predicted fire frequency of approximately 10⁻³ per pod-year, or roughly one fire every 1,000 pod-years of operation, a frequency that, for a fleet of 10 pods (the combined Monterey and Osa communities plus the anticipated expansion to additional sites), corresponds to a fleet-wide fire every 100 years — a risk that the Aegis C program considers acceptable, as it is comparable to the fire ignition frequency (approximately 10⁻³ to 10⁻² per occupied unit-year) for nuclear submarines, which are the closest analogue to the Atlantis habitat in terms of confined, recirculating atmosphere, high-energy electrical systems, and the catastrophic consequences of an uncontrolled fire, and for which the combination of ignition source control, materials flammability control, and automated total-flooding gaseous fire suppression (using Halon 1301 — bromotrifluoromethane, a compound that was phased out of production by the Montreal Protocol on Substances that Deplete the Ozone Layer in 1994 but that is still used in military applications because no equally effective alternative has been identified for flammable liquid and electrical fires in occupied spaces where water-based suppression would be ineffective or would damage critical equipment, and that the Atlantis system replaced with CO₂ because the Atlantis project's sustainability commitments — see PART-142 — prohibit the use of ozone-depleting substances, even in fire suppression, where the atmospheric release of the agent is an unavoidable consequence of system discharge) has produced a fire safety record that validates the general approach — ignition control, non-combustible materials, rapid automated suppression — and that the Atlantis program has built upon with the specific adaptations for the hyperbaric, hyperoxic environment described in this document, and with the continuous monitoring, training, and inspection programs that transform the theoretical fire safety design into a practical, living safety culture that every Atlantis occupant internalizes during their 12-week qualification program and that becomes as automatic and unquestioned as the donning of a seatbelt in an automobile — a habit of safety that, over the 90-day deployment, becomes part of the crew's identity and that, in the event of a fire, will trigger the trained response that saves their lives: don mask, confirm suppression activation, proceed to muster station, verify all crew accounted for, ventilate, assess, recover, and continue the mission, because the pod is not just a home — it is a survival capsule, and survival in a hyperbaric hyperoxic environment demands nothing less than absolute, uncompromising vigilance against the ancient enemy of fire, an enemy that the underwater environment paradoxically makes MORE dangerous, not less, because the increased pressure and oxygen density transform a surface nuisance — a small fire extinguished by a single person with a hand-held extinguisher — into an existential threat that can kill everyone in the habitat in less time than it takes to read this sentence aloud, a reality that the Atlantis fire safety protocol acknowledges in its foundational design principle, stated in the first line of the Aegis C Fire Safety Standard: "In a hyperbaric habitat, there is no such thing as a small fire."
