## PART 140 — INVESTMENT CASE

The net present value (NPV) of the Atlantis program, calculated over the 50-year operational life using a range of discount rates from 2 to 8 percent and a range of crustal displacement probabilities from 0.1 to 5.0 percent per 50-year period (corresponding to recurrence intervals of 50,000 to 1,000 years, based on the paleotsunami deposit evidence compiled in the Crustal Displacement Geological Analysis, which documents at least 4 major inundation events in the California coastal geological record over the past 200,000 years, yielding a mean recurrence interval of approximately 50,000 years and a 50-year event probability of approximately 0.1 percent — the "best estimate" scenario that the Zirconia analysis uses as the baseline for the investment case, but with sensitivity analysis extending to much higher probabilities (up to 5 percent per 50 years, corresponding to a 1,000-year recurrence interval, which is the "pessimistic" scenario that the program's scientific advisory board recommended the Zirconia analysis include because the geological record is inherently incomplete — the absence of evidence for more frequent events does not constitute evidence of their absence, and the epistemic uncertainty in the recurrence interval estimate is large enough that a prudent investment decision should consider the possibility that the true probability is significantly higher than the best estimate, a consideration that the Zirconia analysis accommodates through the sensitivity analysis and that results in a break-even probability — the minimum probability at which the investment's NPV becomes positive — of approximately 0.15 percent per 50 years (1 event per 33,000 years), a probability that is comfortably below the best-estimate recurrence interval and that therefore implies that the Atlantis investment generates a positive expected return (in terms of the monetized value of lives saved) even under the most conservative scientific estimate of the crustal displacement probability, a finding that the program's economists have presented to the Office of Management and Budget as part of the regulatory impact analysis for the FEMA BRIC grant, and that the OMB's Circular A-4 review process (which requires that all major federal regulations and investments be justified by a benefit-cost analysis demonstrating that the expected benefits exceed the costs) has accepted as sufficient to support the grant award, a significant bureaucratic milestone that the program's government relations team considers as important as the Congressional funding authorization itself, because the OMB's approval establishes a precedent for the use of the VSL framework in evaluating crustal displacement survival investments, a precedent that will apply to all future Atlantis program expansions and that will facilitate the program's access to federal funding for the global network, the multi-generational habitation, and the eventual integration with the space habitation modules — the entire Aegis C vision, validated at the highest level of the federal financial regulatory apparatus, as an investment whose expected benefits — measured in the lives of the people who will survive the next crustal displacement event, whenever it occurs, and in the knowledge, the technology, the economic activity, and the human resilience that the pods will generate in the decades before that event — exceed its costs, not marginally but overwhelmingly, by a factor of nearly ten to one, making the Atlantis program, in the dispassionate language of benefit-cost analysis, one of the most valuable investments that the United States government has ever made in the protection of its citizens against catastrophic risk, a conclusion that the program's economists, the OMB reviewers, the Congressional appropriators, and the Zirconia directorate all endorse, and that forms the financial foundation on which the program's future — from 40 occupants to 1,000, from two sites to 20, from the seabed to the stars — Will be built.

## PART 141 — MARITIME LAW

The Atlantis deployment sites at Monterey (36°47'N, 121°50'W) and Osa (8°33'N, 83°22'W) fall within the territorial seas of the United States and Costa Rica respectively — the Monterey site at 8 kilometers offshore lies well within the 12-nautical-mile territorial sea limit established by the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), to which the United States is a signatory (though not a ratifying party — the US has not ratified UNCLOS, but has stated, through a series of Presidential proclamations and State Department legal opinions dating from the Reagan Administration's 1983 Ocean Policy Statement to the present, that it considers the navigational provisions of UNCLOS to reflect customary international law and that it will act in accordance with those provisions, a legal position that the Atlantis program's maritime law advisor, a partner at a San Francisco law firm specializing in admiralty and international law, has analyzed in a 45-page legal memorandum concluding that the Atlantis pod, as a stationary underwater installation permanently attached to the seabed within the US territorial sea, falls under the jurisdiction of the United States for all purposes — environmental regulation (the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA), the Clean Water Act, the Marine Protection, Research, and Sanctuaries Act (which established the Monterey Bay National Marine Sanctuary within whose boundaries the pod is located, requiring a Sanctuary permit issued by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration's Office of National Marine Sanctuaries, which the Atlantis program obtained after a 14-month review process that included the preparation of an Environmental Impact Statement (EIS) analyzing the pod's effects on the Sanctuary's biological resources (the benthic community displaced by the pod's anchoring footprint — approximately 500 square meters of seabed, an area that the EIS determined to be biologically sparse, dominated by the sand dollar Dendraster excentricus and the polychaete worm Diopatra ornata, neither of which is a protected species or a significant component of the Sanctuary's food web), water quality (the brine discharge from the RO unit, the thermal plume from the ECLSS heat exchanger, and the occasional venting of excess HELIOX gas during lock operations — all of which the EIS modeling determined would be diluted to below the detection threshold within 10 to 50 meters of the discharge point, well within the Sanctuary's 100-meter mixing zone guideline), and the potential for an accidental release of hazardous materials (the diesel fuel, the hydraulic fluid, and the CO₂ fire suppression agent — all of which the EIS determined to have a negligible environmental impact at the predicted release frequencies and quantities, based on the fault tree analysis and the containment design described in PART-135). The Osa site, at 5 kilometers offshore, lies within Costa Rica's territorial sea and exclusive economic zone (EEZ) — Costa Rica is a party to UNCLOS and has enacted domestic legislation (the Ley sobre la Zona Marítimo Terrestre, Law No. 6043 of 1977, and the Ley de Aguas, Law No. 276 of 1942, as amended) that establishes the state's jurisdiction over the territorial sea, the EEZ, and the continental shelf, and that requires a concession from the Costa Rican government for any permanent installation on the seabed, a concession that the Atlantis program obtained from the Costa Rican Ministry of Environment and Energy (MINAE) with the approval of SETENA (the National Environmental Technical Secretariat), the Costa Rican Institute of Fisheries and Aquaculture (INCOPESCA), the National Coast Guard Service, and the Municipality of Golfito (the local government jurisdiction for the Osa Peninsula), a multi-agency approval process that required 18 months of negotiations, environmental studies, community consultations, and legal documentation, at a total cost of approximately $1.2 million — a cost that the Zirconia analysis considers an unavoidable expense of operating in a foreign jurisdiction with a robust regulatory framework and a strong tradition of environmental protection, and that the program's Costa Rican legal advisor — a partner at a San José law firm specializing in environmental and maritime law — describes as "the price of legitimacy — if you want to build an underwater habitat in Costa Rica, you must demonstrate to the satisfaction of every Ministry, every agency, every municipality, and every community that your project is safe, sustainable, and beneficial to the Costa Rican people, and the Atlantis program did exactly that, through a process that, while lengthy and expensive, established the legal foundation for the project's long-term operation and for the expansion of the Atlantis network to additional sites in Costa Rican waters, a foundation that is now codified in the bilateral US-Costa Rica agreement described in PART-144, and that represents a model for international cooperation in underwater habitation that will serve the Atlantis program well as it expands to the global sites of PART-147.

## PART 142 — ENVIRONMENTAL COMPLIANCE

The Atlantis program's environmental compliance framework is anchored by the NEPA Environmental Impact Statement for the Monterey deployment and the SETENA Environmental Viability License (Licencia de Viabilidad Ambiental) for the Osa deployment, both of which were issued after comprehensive reviews that the program's environmental compliance team — a group of 6 environmental scientists, 2 regulatory attorneys, and 1 public participation specialist, led by a former US Environmental Protection Agency Region 9 deputy administrator who joined the Atlantis program after a 25-year career in federal environmental regulation and who has described the Atlantis NEPA process as "the most technically demanding and procedurally rigorous environmental review I have ever overseen, exceeding even the reviews for the major offshore oil and gas production platforms in the Gulf of Mexico and the Beaufort Sea — not because the Atlantis pod's environmental impact is larger (it is not — the pod's footprint, emissions, and resource consumption are orders of magnitude smaller than an offshore platform's), but because the pod's novelty — the first civilian underwater habitat in US waters since the TEKTITE program in 1969-1970 — meant that there were no established precedents, no standard impact assessment methodologies, no regulatory 'off-the-shelf' solutions for the unique environmental questions that the pod raised: How do you assess the impact of a continuous, 50-year underwater human presence on the benthic community? How do you model the dispersion of a hyperbaric HELIOX gas vented from a depth of 45 meters? How do you evaluate the cumulative impact of 6 pods, each venting, discharging, radiating heat, and illuminating the seabed with its external LED lighting (a soft, blue-green illumination (490 nanometers, selected to minimize the attraction of phototactic zooplankton, which are most sensitive to the blue wavelengths (450-480 nm) that dominate the underwater light field, and to avoid the disruption of the vertical migration of mesopelagic organisms that rise to the surface at night to feed on phytoplankton and that would be disoriented by a white-light illumination — an ecological consideration that the program's consulting marine biologist, a professor at the Moss Landing Marine Laboratories who has studied the Monterey Canyon's bioluminescent community for 30 years, identified during the NEPA scoping process and that the Atlantis design team addressed by specifying the 490-nm monochromatic LED lighting for the external illumination, at an incremental cost of $3,500 per pod — approximately the cost of 4 high-quality external LED fixtures — for the custom-wavelength LED chips, a cost that the environmental compliance team considered a small price to pay for the protection of the canyon's unique biological community, and that the Sanctuary permit reviewer cited in the permit decision as evidence of the Atlantis program's commitment to environmental stewardship. The SETENA review for the Osa deployment focused on the pod's potential impact on the Golfo Dulce's unique anoxic layer (the sulfide bank described in PART-124), the coral communities that fringe the Osa Peninsula's coastline (the rocky reefs that support a diverse assemblage of scleractinian corals, gorgonians, and sponges, and that are a primary attraction for the eco-tourism industry), and the sea turtle nesting beaches of the Corcovado National Park (the most important nesting site in Central America for the Pacific leatherback turtle (Dermochelys coriacea), a critically endangered species whose global population has declined by approximately 98 percent over the past three generations, and whose nesting beaches on the Osa Peninsula are protected by the Park's strict "no development, no artificial lighting, no vehicle access" regulations — regulations that the Atlantis program exceeded, not merely complied with, by designing the Osa pod's external lighting to be invisible from the surface (the 490-nm LED illumination is absorbed by seawater within approximately 10 meters of the pod, ensuring that no light reaches the surface where it could disorient nesting turtles or their hatchlings, a "turtle-safe lighting" design that the Park's scientific director praised in a letter to the SETENA review committee and that the Atlantis program's Osa crew members, during their surface decompression period, have observed from the beach — standing on the sand at midnight, under the Milky Way and the bioluminescent waves, looking out to sea and seeing only the dark horizon, knowing that directly below them, at 30 meters depth, their pod is illuminated, active, and alive, but invisible — a small, beautiful secret that the sea keeps for them, and that they keep for the sea.

## PART 143 — HABITATION STANDARDS

The Atlantis pod's classification by the American Bureau of Shipping (ABS) as a "Manned Subsea Habitat" under the ABS Rules for Underwater Vehicles, Systems and Hyperbaric Facilities (2024 Edition) represents the first time that a civilian underwater habitat has been designed, constructed, and certified to the same classification standard as a commercial deep-submergence vehicle, and this classification — which the ABS granted after a comprehensive design review, a survey of the fabrication at the Mojave facility, a witnessing of the pressure testing to 6.0 ATA (the maximum allowable working pressure, 1.1 times the design pressure of 5.5 ATA, per the ABS Rules' hydrostatic test requirement for pressure vessels for human occupancy), and an on-site inspection of the Monterey deployment — provides the Atlantis program with the independent, third-party verification of the pod's structural integrity, its life support system reliability, its fire safety design, and its emergency evacuation capability, a verification that is essential for the program's insurance placement (as described in PART-135), for its regulatory compliance (the ABS classification satisfies the US Coast Guard's requirement for a "recognized classification society" to certify the structural and mechanical fitness of a fixed offshore structure, a category that the Coast Guard determined the Atlantis pod falls under, applying the regulatory framework of 33 CFR Part 140 (Outer Continental Shelf Activities) by analogy, because the Atlantis pod is the first structure of its kind in US waters and no specific regulation exists for a manned underwater habitat, a regulatory gap that the ABS classification fills by providing a technically rigorous, internationally recognized standard against which the pod's design and construction can be evaluated, a role that the ABS's Vice President for Offshore Technology described, in a speech at the 2025 Offshore Technology Conference in Houston, as "exactly the kind of challenge that the classification societies were created to address — when a new technology emerges that does not fit within the existing regulatory boxes, the classification society provides the technical framework that bridges the gap between innovation and regulation, enabling the technology to be deployed safely and responsibly while the regulatory framework catches up, a process that the Atlantis program has navigated with the ABS's guidance and that we believe will serve as the template for the classification of all future underwater habitats, worldwide, as more nations, more companies, and more communities recognize the potential of the subsea environment for habitation, research, and survival.

## PART 144 — INTERNATIONAL COORDINATION

The bilateral agreement between the United States and Costa Rica, formally entitled the "Memorandum of Understanding Concerning Cooperation in the Development and Operation of Subsea Habitation Facilities for Scientific Research, Disaster Resilience, and Marine Conservation," signed by the US Secretary of State and the Costa Rican Minister of Foreign Affairs at a ceremony in the Treaty Room of the State Department in Washington, DC, on March 15, 2026, with the Atlantis program's director, the US Ambassador to Costa Rica, the Costa Rican Ambassador to the United States, and the crew members of the Monterey and Osa pods in attendance — the crew members having traveled to Washington from Mojave on a C-130 transport aircraft provided by the California Air National Guard, a gesture of support that the Adjutant General of the California National Guard described, in a letter to the Atlantis program director, as "a small contribution to a large mission, and a recognition by the men and women of the California National Guard of the importance of the Atlantis program to the security and resilience of the State of California and the nation" — establishes the legal framework for the coordinated operation of the two demonstration sites, including the mutual recognition of each nation's regulatory approvals (the US NEPA EIS and the Costa Rican SETENA license are recognized as satisfying the environmental review requirements of both nations for the purposes of the Atlantis program, eliminating the need for a duplicative environmental review process in either nation), the procedures for cross-border emergency response (the US Coast Guard and the Costa Rican National Coast Guard Service have agreed to a joint rescue coordination protocol that enables either service to respond to an emergency at either deployment site, using the nearest available assets regardless of nationality, a protocol that was tested in a joint exercise in July 2025, involving a simulated medical evacuation from the Osa pod to the deck chamber of a US Coast Guard cutter — a scenario that required the coordinated operation of US and Costa Rican rescue helicopters, dive teams, and medical personnel across a language barrier that the exercise revealed was manageable through the use of bilingual coordination officers and standardized International Maritime Organization (IMO) emergency communication protocols — and that resulted in the successful "rescue" of the simulated patient within the 4-hour medical evacuation timeline established in PART-125, demonstrating that the bilateral framework is operationally viable and that it can serve as the model for the multi-national rescue coordination that the global Atlantis network of PART-147 will require, a model that the International Maritime Organization (IMO) has recognized by adopting — at the Maritime Safety Committee's 110th session in May 2026 — a new circular (MSC.1/Circ.1687, "Guidelines for the Rescue of Personnel from Manned Subsea Habitats") that establishes the international standards for subsea habitat rescue coordination, based on the Atlantis program's bilateral protocols and the US Navy's submarine rescue doctrine, and that the Atlantis program's maritime law advisor describes as "the culmination of three years of advocacy at the IMO — we have successfully created the international legal framework for the rescue of civilians from underwater habitats, a framework that did not exist before the Atlantis program and that will now guide the rescue operations of every underwater habitat, in every nation, for the indefinite future, a legacy of the Atlantis program that will save lives even if the crustal displacement event that the pods were designed for never occurs, because the rescue framework will be there, ready, when some future underwater habitat — a research station, a tourist hotel, a mining operation — experiences an emergency and the crew, trapped at depth, wait for the rescue that the Atlantis program taught the world how to perform.

## PART 145 — LIABILITY FRAMEWORK

The Atlantis liability framework is structured around three legal instruments that together define the allocation of risk among the program's participants: (1) the Crew Informed Consent and Waiver of Liability — a 22-page document, reviewed and approved by the Atlantis program's legal counsel, the program's insurance underwriters at Lloyd's, and an independent legal ethicist from the Stanford Law School's Center for Law and the Biosciences, which informs each crew member of the specific risks of the Atlantis deployment (the risks of decompression sickness, oxygen toxicity, fire, flood, toxic gas exposure, dental emergency, surgical complication, psychological decompensation, and death, with each risk being described quantitatively — the predicted DCS incidence of 0.5 to 1.0 percent per decompression, the fire ignition frequency of approximately 10⁻³ per pod-year, the flood probability of approximately 10⁻⁴ per pod-year for a catastrophic breach, the psychological decompensation rate of approximately 2 to 5 percent of crew members requiring early evacuation — derived from the Zirconia risk analysis, and each crew member acknowledges that they have read, understood, and voluntarily accepted these risks, and that they waive any claim against Carrington Storm Motors, the Atlantis program, the Aegis C program, the US government, the Costa Rican government, or any of their officers, employees, contractors, or agents for any injury, illness, disability, or death arising from their participation in the Atlantis program, to the fullest extent permitted by law — a waiver that the legal ethicist has opined is ethically valid because it meets the criteria for informed consent to hazardous activity: the risks are disclosed with specificity and quantified where possible; the crew member has the opportunity to consult with independent legal and medical advisors before signing (and the Atlantis program provides a $5,000 stipend specifically for this purpose — the crew member can use the stipend to hire their own attorney and their own physician to review the waiver and assess their personal medical fitness for the deployment, an unusual provision that the program's legal counsel insisted on, over the objections of the program's budget office, because "a waiver that is reviewed by the crew member's own attorney, at the program's expense, is a waiver that will withstand judicial scrutiny when the crew member's family sues us after a fatality — and there will be a fatality, eventually, because this program is too complex, too dangerous, and too long to operate without ever losing someone, and the legal framework must be robust enough to protect the program, its employees, and its partners from the crushing liability that a wrongful death lawsuit would impose, while still honoring our moral obligation to the crew and their families, an obligation that the stipend and the independent review process represent — we are not asking the crew to sign away their rights blindly; we are asking them to sign with full knowledge of what they are giving up and what they are gaining, and we are paying for them to verify that knowledge with their own experts, a practice that I believe should be the standard for informed consent in all hazardous occupations, from commercial fishing to spaceflight, and that the Atlantis program is pioneering, along with the other innovations of Part 125 (the hyperbaric anesthesia protocol), Part 126 (the psychological compatibility assessment), and Part 127 (the 12-week qualification program), as a contribution to the broader field of human safety in extreme environments.
