## 2.0 LITHOSPHERIC DECOUPLING MECHANICS: THE TRANSITION FROM CREEP TO CATACLYSMIC SLIP

The transition from the steady-state creep that characterizes normal lithosphere-asthenosphere coupling — in which the differential motion between the lithospheric plates and the underlying mantle is accommodated by the slow, ductile deformation of the asthenospheric peridotite, a process that occurs at strain rates of approximately 10^-14 to 10^-15 per second, corresponding to relative velocities of 1 to 10 centimeters per year between adjacent plates, the velocities that plate tectonic theory has documented through the analysis of seafloor magnetic anomalies, transform fault offsets, and space-geodetic measurements of present-day plate motions using GPS, VLBI, and SLR techniques — to the catastrophic slip that characterizes a crustal displacement event is a rheological phase transition that occurs when the strain rate at the LAB exceeds a critical threshold at which the dominant deformation mechanism changes from diffusion creep, in which strain is accommodated by the stress-directed diffusion of atoms and vacancies through the crystal lattice and along grain boundaries, a mechanism that produces a Newtonian (linear) relationship between stress and strain rate with an effective viscosity that is independent of stress, to dislocation creep, in which strain is accommodated by the movement of dislocations — line defects in the crystal lattice — through the mineral grains, a mechanism that produces a power-law relationship between stress and strain rate with an effective viscosity that decreases with increasing stress, creating a positive feedback in which higher strain rates reduce the effective viscosity, which allows higher strain rates for a given stress, which further reduces the effective viscosity, and so on, until the strain rate accelerates to the point where the frictional heating generated by the deformation — the conversion of mechanical work into thermal energy through the dissipation of dislocation motion — raises the local temperature at the LAB by hundreds of degrees Celsius, a temperature increase known as shear heating or viscous dissipation that is governed by the heat equation with a source term equal to the product of the shear stress and the strain rate, and that, when combined with the strong temperature dependence of the dislocation creep flow law through the Arrhenius term exp(-Q/RT), produces a second positive feedback — the thermal feedback — in which shear heating reduces the effective viscosity by increasing the temperature, which allows higher strain rates, which generate more shear heating, and so on, until the LAB becomes effectively frictionless, the peridotite melts partially or completely, and the lithosphere slides over the asthenosphere with essentially no resistance, a condition known as thermal runaway that, once initiated, propagates along the LAB at velocities determined by the rate at which the shear heating can diffuse through the surrounding rock — a process governed by the thermal diffusivity of peridotite, approximately 10^-6 square meters per second, which limits the propagation speed of the thermal runaway front to approximately 1 to 10 meters per second, corresponding to a global propagation time of approximately 10^6 to 10^7 seconds, or approximately 10 to 100 days — a timescale that is consistent with the paleomagnetic evidence of rapid, but not instantaneous, polar wander as recorded in the magnetization of lava flows that cooled during the displacement event, which show progressive changes in the direction of the geomagnetic field over timescales of weeks to months rather than the instantaneous change that would be expected if the lithosphere rotated as a rigid body with no internal deformation.

from the convergence or divergence of the horizontal flow, must be compensated by a corresponding change in the absolute vorticity of the parcel, a constraint that imposes a fundamental relationship between the sea-surface displacement (the wave height) and the horizontal velocity field of the water, a relationship that is exploited in the numerical models of crustal displacement wave generation that the Atlantis design team has developed using the MIT General Circulation Model (MITgcm), a state-of-the-art ocean circulation model that solves the primitive equations — the Navier-Stokes equations simplified by the hydrostatic approximation (which neglects vertical accelerations in the vertical momentum equation) and the Boussinesq approximation (which neglects density variations except where they multiply gravity) — on a spherical grid with horizontal resolution as fine as 1 kilometer in the coastal regions of interest, vertical resolution of 10 to 50 meters in the upper The MITgcm simulations of a crustal displacement event, initialized with a representative lithospheric rotation of 25 degrees about an axis passing through the central Atlantic (a geometry that is of 60 days with a time step of 60 seconds (a compromise between computational stability, which requires the time step to satisfy the Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy condition that the distance traveled by a wave in one time step not exceed the grid spacing, and computational cost, which scales inversely with the time step), reveal a complex pattern of wave generation that can be summarized as follows: the initial response of the ocean to the onset of lithospheric displacement is the generation of a planetary-scale sea-surface tilt, with the sea surface rising on the leading (eastward-moving) side of each continent — where the continent is pushing into the ocean basin and displacing water upward — and falling on the trailing (westward-moving) side of each continent, where the continent is pulling away from the ocean basin and creating a void that the water rushes to fill, a pattern that is the oceanographic equivalent of the "slosh" of water in a bathtub when the tub is suddenly tilted, and that produces sea-surface displacements of hundreds of meters over horizontal scales of thousands of kilometers, displacements that, while large in absolute terms, are small compared to the ocean depth and that therefore propagate as linear, non-dispersive shallow-water waves at the speed c = √(gH), reaching the opposite side of the ocean basin in a matter of hours and beginning the process of wave reflection, refraction, and interference that determines the ultimate distribution of wave heights along the world's coastlines, a process that continues for the entire duration of the displacement event and for days to weeks afterward as the ocean gradually equilibrates to the new basin geometry, with wave energy being repeatedly reflected from continental margins and island arcs, refracted by bathymetric features such as mid-ocean ridges, seamount chains, and submarine plateaus, and focused by the shape of ocean basins into regions of concentrated wave energy — the eastern boundaries of the Pacific, the southern coast of Australia, the western coast of South America, and the islands of Southeast Asia — where wave heights are amplified far above the open-ocean average, and dissipated in regions of wave energy divergence — the central Pacific, the Southern Ocean, and the eastern coast of Africa — where wave heights remain relatively modest, a spatial pattern that the geological evidence of past displacement events, as preserved in the distribution of tsunami deposits, wave-cut terraces, and erratic boulders, broadly confirms, lending credibility to the MITgcm simulations and to the wave height estimates that they produce for the specific locations — Monterey Canyon and the Osa Peninsula — that are the focus of the Atlantis demonstration community design.

The wave height at any given coastal location during a crustal displacement event is determined by the superposition — the linear addition, according to the principle of superposition that applies to linear waves, or the nonlinear interaction, which must be computed numerically when the wave amplitudes are large enough that the linear approximation breaks down — of multiple wave components that arrive at the location at different times and from different directions, having traversed different paths across the ocean basin and having been subjected to different degrees of shoaling, refraction, diffraction, and energy dissipation along those paths, such that the maximum wave height at a location is not simply proportional to the amplitude of the initial sea-surface displacement but depends on the constructive or destructive interference of the various wave components at that location, a dependence that introduces substantial spatial variability — variations of a factor of 2 to 5 in wave height over distances of tens of kilometers — that has critical implications for the site selection strategy of the Atlantis communities, because a community that is positioned at a location where the wave components interfere destructively may experience wave heights that are substantially lower than the regional average, while a nearby location where the components interfere constructively may experience wave heights that are substantially higher, and the distinction between these two types of locations can only be resolved through the high-resolution numerical modeling that the Atlantis design team has conducted for each of the candidate sites, modeling that accounts not only for the global wave generation patterns but also for the local bathymetric effects — the wave refraction and shoaling that occur as the wave approaches the coast — that further amplify or attenuate the wave height and that are particularly important for submerged structures like the Atlantis pods, which experience not the wave height itself but the dynamic pressure associated with the wave's orbital velocity at the pod's depth, a quantity that is related to the wave height but that is attenuated with depth at a rate that depends on the wave period and wavelength, as discussed in detail in PART-002.

The distinction between the wave generation mechanism of crustal displacement and the wave generation mechanism of conventional tsunamis — the earthquake-generated tsunamis that are the focus of existing tsunami warning systems and coastal engineering practice — is fundamental to understanding why conventional tsunami protection measures, including seawalls, breakwaters, and elevated structures, are completely inadequate for the crustal displacement threat and why the Atlantis underwater communities, which exploit the depth-attenuation of wave energy rather than attempting to resist the wave at the surface, represent a qualitatively different and more survivable approach. A conventional tsunami is generated by a localized vertical displacement of the seafloor — typically a few meters of uplift or subsidence over an area of thousands of square kilometers — during a large subduction-zone earthquake, a displacement that is small compared to the ocean depth (except in the immediate vicinity of the fault rupture, where the seafloor displacement may be comparable to the local water depth) and that therefore generates a wave whose amplitude is small compared to its wavelength, a wave that propagates across the ocean as a linear shallow-water wave with a wavelength of hundreds of kilometers and a wave height — in the open ocean — of typically less than 1 meter, a wave that is barely noticeable to a ship at sea but that, as it approaches the coast and shoals, can reach heights of 10 to 30 meters at the shoreline, devastating coastal communities but remaining within the realm of what engineered structures, if sufficiently robust, can resist. A crustal displacement mega-tsunami, by contrast, is generated by the wholesale displacement of the entire ocean basin, a displacement that is NOT small compared to the ocean depth — the displacement velocity of the seafloor, integrated over the duration of the event, can be hundreds to thousands of kilometers, far exceeding any conventional tsunami source — and that generates a wave whose amplitude in the open ocean is already hundreds of meters, exceeding the depth of the continental shelf over most of the world's coastlines and rendering the shallow-water wave approximation, which is the theoretical foundation of conventional tsunami modeling, fundamentally inapplicable, since the wave height is comparable to or greater than the water depth, a regime in which the wave propagates not as a linear disturbance but as a turbulent bore — a wall of churning, aerated water that advances across the coastal plain at velocities of tens of meters per second, carrying with it the debris of everything it has destroyed — and in which the forces exerted on structures are dominated not by the hydrostatic pressure of the water column (which can be calculated from the inundation depth and the density of the water-sediment mixture) but by the hydrodynamic impact forces of debris — boulders, tree trunks, building fragments, and vehicles — that are entrained in the flow and that strike structures with kinetic energies that can exceed 10^9 joules for a single large boulder, an energy that is approximately equivalent to the explosive yield of 250 kilograms of TNT and that exceeds the impact resistance of any practical engineering material or structural configuration, including the reinforced concrete of nuclear containment buildings and the armor steel of naval warships, both of which are designed to resist impact energies that are orders of magnitude smaller than the debris impact energies characteristic of a crustal displacement mega-tsunami, a reality that the CSMFAB076 surface dome design addresses not by attempting to make the dome physically impervious to every conceivable debris impact — which is simply impossible given the energies involved — but by employing active electromagnetic deflection systems (the MHD/diamagnetic array) that divert debris before it strikes the dome, an approach that is ingenious but that depends on the continuous availability of astronomical amounts of electrical power during the event, a dependency that the Atlantis underwater communities avoid by positioning themselves at depths where the debris impact hazard — like the wave dynamic pressure hazard — is substantially reduced, because the debris that is carried by a turbidite flow at the seabed consists of smaller particles (the largest boulders having been deposited closer to shore as the flow velocity decreases) and because the impact velocities are lower (the flow velocity near the seabed being reduced by bottom friction relative to the flow velocity in the main body of the turbidite), a reduction that is quantified in PART-010 of this Deep Expansion through the analysis of turbidite Reynolds numbers and the associated boundary-layer velocity profiles.

## 4.0 THE CORIOLIS MODULATION OF CRUSTAL DISPLACEMENT WAVE FIELDS

The rotation of the Earth exerts a profound influence on the wave field generated by crustal displacement through the Coriolis force — the apparent force that deflects moving objects to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere, a consequence of the conservation of angular momentum in a rotating reference frame — which modifies both the propagation direction of the waves (turning them to the right in the Northern Hemisphere, so that waves that initially propagate westward from a displacement source in the North Pacific are deflected northward) and the structure of the wave itself, converting what would be a purely gravity-driven wave on a non-rotating Earth into an inertial-gravity wave — also known as a Poincaré wave — in which the restoring force that opposes the sea-surface displacement is the sum of gravity (which acts to restore the sea surface to the horizontal) and the Coriolis force (which acts to deflect the horizontal flow that accompanies the sea-surface displacement, creating an oscillatory motion in which the water particles trace elliptical paths whose eccentricity depends on the ratio of the wave frequency to the Coriolis frequency, a ratio known as the Burger number after the Dutch oceanographer who first analyzed the dynamics of rotating stratified fluids, and that determines the extent to which the wave energy is trapped near the coast — as coastal Kelvin waves that propagate with the coast on their right in the Northern Hemisphere — or propagates freely into the ocean interior as planetary Rossby waves, whose westward propagation is a consequence of the variation of the Coriolis parameter with latitude, the beta effect that is responsible for the western intensification of the ocean circulation and that, in the context of crustal displacement waves, produces a westward concentration of wave energy that favors the survival of communities on the eastern coastlines of continents, including the California and Costa Rica sites that are the focus of the Atlantis demonstration program, since these coastlines are oriented such that the Coriolis deflection turns the approaching waves parallel to the coast rather than directly onshore, a configuration that reduces the effective wave height experienced at the coast, a reduction that the MITgcm simulations quantify as approximately 15 to 25 percent for the California margin and approximately 30 to 40 percent for the Costa Rica margin, where the Coriolis parameter is smaller (because the latitude is lower, and the Coriolis frequency is proportional to the sine of the latitude, which is sin(8.5°) = 0.148 for the Osa site versus sin(37°) = 0.602 for the Monterey site) but the wave period is longer (because the wave has traveled farther from its source region, and the longer-period components of the wave spectrum travel faster and therefore arrive first at distant locations, while the shorter-period components are dissipated or left behind), and the Burger number — the square of the ratio of the wavelength to the Rossby radius of deformation, which is the horizontal scale at which the Coriolis force balances the pressure gradient force and which for the ocean is approximately 30 to 50 kilometers at mid-latitudes and 100 to 200 kilometers at low latitudes — determines the degree of rotational control on the wave dynamics, with the smaller Rossby radius at high latitudes (more rotational control, wave energy trapped near the coast) and the larger Rossby radius at low latitudes (less rotational control, wave energy propagating more freely into the ocean interior), a latitudinal dependence that explains why Costa Rica, at 8.5°N, experiences wave heights that are attenuated relative to California not only because of its position near the rotation axis (the pivot-axis effect) but also because of the reduced Coriolis trapping of wave energy at low latitudes, which allows more of the incident wave energy to be radiated back into the Pacific Ocean interior rather than being concentrated at the coast, a happy coincidence of physics and geography that makes Costa Rica one of the most survivable locations on Earth for a crustal displacement event, with predicted wave heights — at the surface — of 150 to 200 meters rather than the 3,000 to 3,500 meters characteristic of the California coast, and with corresponding reductions in the dynamic pressure at the Atlantis pod's operating depth that make the Costa Rica sites the lowest-risk deployment locations in the entire Atlantis demonstration program.

## 5.0 WAVE ENERGY BUDGET AND DISSIPATION MECHANISMS DURING CRUSTAL DISPLACEMENT

The energy budget of the wave field generated by crustal displacement — the accounting of how the initial gravitational potential energy released by the lithospheric rotation is partitioned among wave kinetic energy, wave potential energy (the energy associated with the displacement of the sea surface from its equilibrium position), turbulent kinetic energy dissipated by wave breaking and bottom friction, and internal wave energy radiated into the ocean interior through the excitation of internal gravity waves at the pycnocline — provides a quantitative framework for assessing the survivability of the Atlantis underwater communities, because the wave energy that a community must resist is not the total energy released by the displacement but only the fraction of that energy that arrives at the community's location, at the community's depth, and in the frequency band to which the community's pressure hull and anchoring system are dynamically responsive — a fraction that, because of the combination of geometric spreading (the wave energy is distributed over an ever-increasing area as it propagates away from its source), dispersion (the different frequency components of the wave travel at different speeds, dispersing the energy over a longer time interval and reducing the peak energy flux), shoaling amplification (the energy flux per unit area increases as the wave enters shallow water, concentrating the energy at the coast), Coriolis deflection (which redirects energy away from some coasts and toward others), and depth attenuation (the exponential decay of orbital velocity with depth, which removes energy from the depth range of the Atlantis pods), is estimated to be on the order of 10^-6 to 10^-4 of the total wave energy, corresponding to an energy flux at the pod's depth that is equivalent to the energy flux of a conventional storm wave at the surface — a wave height of 5 to 15 meters, well within the range that the LBFRP-001 composite pressure hull has been designed and tested to withstand, with the full-scale hydrostatic pressure test to 10.5 MPa (equivalent to a water depth of 1,025 meters, or approximately 20 times the design operating depth of 45 meters) and the impact test at 25 kilojoules (equivalent to the kinetic energy of a 500-kilogram granite boulder traveling at 10 meters per second) confirming that the hull's structural margins comfortably exceed the predicted loads at both the Monterey and Osa sites, even under the most pessimistic assumptions about wave focusing, turbidite debris entrainment, and the degradation of material properties over the pod's 50-year design life.

The depth-attenuation factor — the ratio of the wave orbital velocity at depth z to the wave orbital velocity at the surface — is the single most important parameter in the Atlantis community's survival physics, because it determines the extent to which the catastrophic surface wave heights (3,200 meters at Monterey, 170 meters at Osa) are translated into the much more manageable dynamic pressures that the pods actually experience at their operating depths (45 meters at Monterey, 30 meters at Osa), and this factor is derived in detail in PART-002 using the Airy wave theory and the Stokes fifth-order extension that accounts for the nonlinearities that become significant when the wave steepness (the ratio of wave height to wavelength) exceeds approximately 0.01 — which, for a megatsunami with a height of 3,200 meters and a wavelength of approximately 300 kilometers, corresponds to a steepness of approximately 0.01, indicating that the linear Airy theory is marginally applicable at best and that the full nonlinear treatment is necessary for accurate predictions of orbital velocity at depth, a treatment that the Atlantis design team has implemented in the OpenFOAM computational fluid dynamics simulations described in PART-017, which solve the full nonlinear Navier-Stokes equations without the simplifying assumptions of linear wave theory and which provide the most accurate available estimates of the dynamic pressure, shear stress, and pressure cycling that the pods will experience during wave passage, estimates that are essential for the fatigue analysis described in PART-005 and for the anchoring design described in PART-015.

## 6.0 SYNTHESIS: THE CRUSTAL DISPLACEMENT THREAT AS AN ENGINEERING DESIGN CONSTRAINT

The crustal displacement threat, as characterized by the physics of lithospheric decoupling, inertial ocean momentum transfer, Coriolis-modulated wave propagation, and bathymetric wave amplification that have been described in the preceding sections, imposes a set of engineering design constraints on the Atlantis-class underwater communities that are fundamentally different from the constraints that govern conventional marine structures — offshore oil platforms, submarine pipelines, and undersea telecommunication cables — because the threat is fundamentally different from the threats (wind waves, currents, earthquakes) that those conventional structures are designed to withstand, a difference that requires a complete rethinking of the design philosophy, as the Kiddo register articulated in Episode Eighteen of the Sibling Frequency when it introduced the concept of "passive survivability" — the principle that a structure intended to survive a catastrophic event must not depend on active systems (power, control, human intervention) that are likely to fail during the event, but must instead rely on the intrinsic physical properties of its geometry, materials, and environment to achieve survival, a principle that the Atlantis communities embody by positioning themselves at depths where the physics of wave attenuation provides a natural, passive, and unfailing defense against the surface wave forces that would destroy any structure — no matter how robustly engineered — at the surface, a defense that requires no power, no control systems, no human intervention, and that cannot be disabled by any conceivable failure mode short of the complete disappearance of the ocean itself, a scenario that, while not entirely impossible in the context of the most extreme crustal displacement events — if the displacement were so rapid and so large that the ocean is physically thrown off the planet, a scenario that the energy budget analysis suggests is not possible for any The specific constraint values that the crustal displacement threat imposes on the Atlantis community design, as derived from the MITgcm simulations, the OpenFOAM CFD analysis, and the geological evidence reviewed in the preceding sections, are as follows: at the Monterey Canyon site (36°47'N, 121°50'W, depth 45 meters), the maximum wave orbital velocity at the pod's depth during the passage of a 3,200-meter surface wave is estimated to be 8 to 12 meters per second, corresponding to a dynamic pressure (0.5 × ρ × v²) of 33 to 75 kilopascals, which is less than 1 percent of the pod's design hydrostatic pressure of 10.5 megapascals, indicating that the dynamic pressure from the wave — which is the dominant threat to surface structures — is essentially negligible at the pod's operating depth, a conclusion that is the fundamental justification for the underwater approach;
