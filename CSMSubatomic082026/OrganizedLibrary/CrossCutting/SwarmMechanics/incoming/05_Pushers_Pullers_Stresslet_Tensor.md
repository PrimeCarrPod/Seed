# Document 5: Pushers, Pullers, and the Stresslet Tensor
## Mathematical Structure of Microswimmer Hydrodynamics and Collective Instabilities

### 5.1 The Stresslet Tensor: Definition and Properties

For a force-free, torque-free microswimmer, the leading-order far-field flow is a symmetric, traceless force dipole known as the **stresslet**. The stresslet tensor S is defined as:

S = ∫_{∂B} [ (x - x_c)(σ·n) + (σ·n)(x - x_c) - (2/3)I((x - x_c)·(σ·n)) ] dS

where ∂B is the swimmer surface, σ = -pI + μ(∇u + ∇uᵀ) is the fluid stress tensor, n is the outward normal, and x_c is the center of mass. The stresslet has the following properties:

1. **Symmetry**: S = Sᵀ (symmetric tensor)
2. **Tracelessness**: Tr(S) = 0 (sum of diagonal elements is zero)
3. **Five independent components**: In 3D, a symmetric traceless 3×3 tensor has 5 independent components.

The stresslet represents a force dipole: two equal and opposite forces ±F separated by an infinitesimal distance d, with S = F d + d F. Physically, F is the thrust force generated by the flagella/cilia, and d is the vector from the center of drag to the center of thrust.

### 5.2 Eigenvalue Structure and Classification

The stresslet tensor can be diagonalized by a rotation to its principal axes:

S = S₁ e₁ e₁ + S₂ e₂ e₂ + S₃ e₃ e₃

with S₁ + S₂ + S₃ = 0. The eigenvalues {S₁, S₂, S₃} and eigenvectors {e₁, e₂, e₃} characterize the swimmer's flow field. For an axisymmetric swimmer (e.g., a bacterium with a single helical flagellum), two eigenvalues are equal: S₂ = S₃ = -S₁/2. The stresslet is then uniaxial:

S = S₀ (p̂ p̂ - I/3)

where p̂ = e₁ is the swimming direction and S₀ = S₁ is the stresslet strength (scalar). The sign of S₀ determines the swimmer class:

- **S₀ < 0**: Pusher (extensile) – fluid expelled along p̂, drawn in perpendicular to p̂
- **S₀ > 0**: Puller (contractile) – fluid drawn in along p̂, expelled perpendicular to p̂
- **S₀ = 0**: Neutral – higher multipoles dominate (source dipole, force quadrupole)

For non-axisymmetric swimmers (e.g., Chlamydomonas with two anterior flagella), the stresslet is biaxial with three distinct eigenvalues. The flow field has a more complex structure.

### 5.3 Far-Field Flow of a Stresslet

The velocity field generated by a stresslet S at position x_c is:

u(x) = (1/8πμ) S : ∇J(x - x_c)

where J(r) = I/r + r r/r³ is the Oseen tensor. In index notation:

u_i(x) = (1/8πμ) S_{jk} ∂²/∂x_j ∂x_k (|x|⁻¹ δ_{il} + x_i x_l/|x|³)

For a uniaxial stresslet S = S₀ (p̂ p̂ - I/3), the flow simplifies to:

u(x) = (S₀/8πμ) [ 3(p̂·r̂)² - 1 ] r̂ / r²

where r = x - x_c, r = |r|, r̂ = r/r. The radial component is:

u_r = (S₀/8πμ) [ 3 cos²θ - 1 ] / r²

and the polar component (in the plane containing p̂) is:

u_θ = (S₀/8πμ) 3 sinθ cosθ / r²

where θ is the angle between r̂ and p̂. The streamlines are given by ψ(r, θ) = (S₀/8πμ) sin²θ cosθ / r.

**Pusher (S₀ < 0)**: 
- θ = 0, π (along p̂): u_r < 0 (outward flow)
- θ = π/2 (equator): u_r > 0 (inward flow)
- Fluid is pushed away along the swimming axis and sucked in from the sides.

**Puller (S₀ > 0)**:
- θ = 0, π: u_r > 0 (inward flow)
- θ = π/2: u_r < 0 (outward flow)
- Fluid is pulled in along the swimming axis and pushed out to the sides.

### 5.4 Pairwise Hydrodynamic Interactions

Two swimmers i and j at positions x_i, x_j with stresslets S_i, S_j interact hydrodynamically. The velocity of swimmer i due to swimmer j's stresslet is:

v_i = (1/8πμ) S_j : ∇J(x_i - x_j)

The angular velocity of swimmer i due to swimmer j's stresslet is:

ω_i = (1/16πμ) ε_{ikl} S_{j,km} ∂²/∂x_l ∂x_m (x_j - x_i)_k / |x_j - x_i|³

For uniaxial stresslets S_i = S₀ p̂_i p̂_i - I/3 (with appropriate trace subtraction), the interaction can be expressed in terms of the separation vector r = x_j - x_i and the orientations p̂_i, p̂_j.

**Scaling**: The velocity interaction decays as 1/r², the angular interaction as 1/r³. This is longer-ranged than the 1/r decay of a Stokeslet (force monopole), because the force monopole is zero for a free swimmer.

**Alignment interactions**: 
- Pushers (S₀ < 0) tend to align **anti-parallel** when side-by-side (r̂ ⟂ p̂) and **parallel** when in-line (r̂ ∥ p̂).
- Pullers (S₀ > 0) tend to align **parallel** when side-by-side and **anti-parallel** when in-line.

This is the hydrodynamic origin of the distinct collective behaviors: pushers form polar lanes and active turbulence; pullers form polar streams and stable aggregates.

### 5.5 Active Stress in a Suspension

In a suspension of N swimmers in volume V, the ensemble-averaged active stress is:

σ^a = (1/V) ∑_{i=1}^N S_i = n ⟨S⟩

where n = N/V is the number density and ⟨·⟩ denotes an average over the orientation distribution Ψ(p̂). For a uniaxial swimmer with stresslet strength S₀:

σ^a = n S₀ ⟨p̂ p̂ - I/3⟩ = n S₀ Q

where Q = ⟨p̂ p̂ - I/3⟩ is the **nematic order parameter tensor** (symmetric, traceless). The scalar order parameter is S = ⟨(3 cos²θ - 1)/2⟩ where θ is the angle between p̂ and the director.

The total stress in the fluid is:

σ = -pI + 2μE + σ^a

where E = (∇u + ∇uᵀ)/2. The fluid momentum equation is:

ρ(∂u/∂t + u·∇u) = -∇p + μ∇²u + ∇·σ^a

with ∇·u = 0. The active stress divergence ∇·σ^a = n S₀ ∇·Q acts as a body force.

### 5.6 Linear Stability of Uniform Suspensions

Consider a uniform suspension with density n₀, isotropic orientation (Q = 0), and quiescent fluid (u = 0). Perturb:

n = n₀ + δn, u = δu, Q = δQ

The linearized equations are:

∂δn/∂t + n₀ v₀ ∇·δp = D_t ∇²δn
∂δp/∂t = -D_r δp + (λ/2)(∇δu + ∇δuᵀ)·p̂₀ + ... (for polar order)
ρ ∂δu/∂t = -∇δp + μ∇²δu + n₀ S₀ ∇·δQ

For pushers (S₀ < 0), the active stress term n₀ S₀ ∇·δQ has the **opposite sign** to the viscous term μ∇²δu for certain modes. This leads to instability.

**Dispersion relation for polar pushers** (Toner-Tu type):
Assume δp ≠ 0, δn ≠ 0. The dispersion relation is:

ω(k) = -ν k² ± √( -n₀ S₀ k² / ρ )

For S₀ < 0 (pushers), the term under the square root is positive, giving Re(ω) > 0 for k < k_c = √(-n₀ S₀/μ). The suspension is unstable to long-wavelength perturbations.

For pullers (S₀ > 0), the term under the square root is negative, giving purely imaginary ω – stable oscillations or decay.

**Dispersion relation for apolar active nematics** (extensile ζ < 0):
∂δQ/∂t = -Γ H + λ E + ...
ρ ∂δu/∂t = -∇δp + μ∇²δu + ζ ∇·δQ

where H is the molecular field. The dispersion relation gives instability for ζ < 0 (extensile/pushers) at long wavelengths.

### 5.7 Active Turbulence in Pusher Suspensions

The linear instability of pusher suspensions saturates nonlinearly, leading to **active turbulence** – a chaotic flow state with characteristic scales:

- **Correlation length**: ℓ_c ∼ √(μ / n|S₀|)
- **Velocity scale**: u_c ∼ √(n|S₀| / ρ)
- **Timescale**: τ_c ∼ ℓ_c / u_c ∼ ρ / √(n|S₀|μ)

The energy spectrum of active turbulence is distinct from inertial turbulence:

E(k) ∼ n|S₀|/μ × k^{-3/2}  (for kℓ_c ≪ 1)
E(k) ∼ k^{-5/3}  (for kℓ_c ≫ 1, inertial subrange if Re_active ≫ 1)

where Re_active = u_c ℓ_c / ν = n|S₀| / μ² is the active Reynolds number.

**Key features of active turbulence**:
- No energy cascade from large to small scales (energy is injected at scale ℓ_c by active stresses)
- Vorticity field is highly intermittent
- Lagrangian trajectories show superdiffusion
- Topological defects (+1/2, -1/2) in the nematic field are advected by the flow and drive the dynamics

### 5.8 Active Superfluidity: Negative Viscosity

For a suspension of pushers with polar order (⟨p̂⟩ = p ≠ 0), the active stress is:

σ^a = n S₀ (p p - I/3)

Consider a simple shear flow u = (γ̇ y, 0, 0). If the swimmers align with the flow (p ≈ x̂), then:

σ^a_{xy} = n S₀ p_x p_y ≈ 0

But fluctuations in p generate active stresses. The effective viscosity is defined by the total shear stress:

σ_{xy} = μ γ̇ + σ^a_{xy}

In the limit of strong alignment (|p| → 1) and weak shear, the active contribution to viscosity can be negative. For a nematic suspension of pushers (Q ≠ 0, p = 0), the active stress is σ^a = ζ Q with ζ = S₀ < 0. The effective viscosity for a shear flow with director aligned at 45° to the flow is:

μ_eff = μ + ζ/4

For |ζ| > 4μ, the effective viscosity becomes **negative**, implying **active superfluidity** – flow without dissipation, or even energy extraction from the active stresses. However, negative viscosity is linearly unstable and leads to spontaneous flow generation (the same instability as active turbulence).

### 5.9 Mechanical Coupling of Pushers and Pullers

When pushers and pullers coexist, their stresslets interact. The active stress is:

σ^a = n_p S_p Q_p + n_l S_l Q_l

where subscripts p, l denote pushers and pullers (S_p < 0, S_l > 0). If their orientation distributions are coupled (e.g., through hydrodynamic interactions), they can form mixed states.

**Linear stability of mixed suspensions**: The dispersion relation involves the sum of active stresses. If n_p|S_p| > n_l S_l, the suspension behaves like a pusher suspension (active turbulence). If n_p|S_p| < n_l S_l, it behaves like a puller suspension (stable, ordered).

**Nonlinear interactions**: 
- Pushers create flows that advect pullers.
- Pullers create flows that align pushers.
- The mixture can exhibit phase separation: pushers cluster in high-strain regions, pullers in high-vorticity regions.

**Biological relevance**: Many microbial communities contain both pushers (bacteria) and pullers (algae, some ciliates). Their mechanical coupling influences nutrient mixing, biofilm formation, and ecosystem stability.

### 5.10 Active Stress from Squirmers and Model Swimmers

The **squirmer model** (Blake, 1971) represents a microswimmer as a sphere with a prescribed tangential surface velocity:

u_s(θ) = ∑_{n=1}^∞ B_n P_n'(cosθ) sinθ

where P_n' are derivatives of Legendre polynomials. The swimming speed is v₀ = (2/3)B₁. The stresslet strength is:

S₀ = (4πμa/3) (B₂ - B₁/2)  (for axisymmetric squirmers)

- B₂ > B₁/2 → S₀ > 0 → Puller
- B₂ < B₁/2 → S₀ < 0 → Pusher
- B₂ = B₁/2 → S₀ = 0 → Neutral (source dipole)

The squirmer model captures the essential hydrodynamics of ciliated microorganisms (Volvox, Paramecium) and provides a tractable framework for simulations.

### 5.11 Higher Multipoles: Source Dipole and Force Quadrupole

When the stresslet is zero (S₀ = 0), the next-order singularities are:

1. **Source dipole (potential dipole)**: Represents a volume flux. For a squirmer with B₂ = B₁/2, the leading term is a source dipole with strength D ∼ B₁a². Flow decays as 1/r².

2. **Force quadrupole**: Arises from the finite size of the propulsive apparatus. Decays as 1/r³.

3. **Rotlet dipole**: For swimmers with net torque (e.g., rotating helices). Decays as 1/r³.

These higher multipoles are important for near-field interactions (r ∼ a) and for neutral swimmers.

### 5.12 Experimental Measurement of Stresslet Strength

**Particle Image Velocimetry (PIV)**:
- Track tracer particles around a single swimmer.
- Fit the measured velocity field to the stresslet flow field u(r) = (S₀/8πμ) [3(p̂·r̂)² - 1] r̂/r².
- Extract S₀ and p̂.

**Direct force measurement** (optical tweezers):
- Hold the swimmer in an optical trap.
- Measure the force required to keep it stationary.
- The stresslet is related to the thrust force F and thrust position d: S₀ = F d.

**Rheometry of bulk suspensions**:
- Measure the shear stress σ_{xy} as a function of shear rate γ̇.
- The active stress contribution is σ^a = n S₀ ⟨p_x p_y⟩.
- In the limit γ̇ → 0, ⟨p_x p_y⟩ ∼ γ̇, giving an active viscosity contribution.

**Results for E. coli**: S₀ ≈ -0.5 to -2 pN·μm (pusher). For Chlamydomonas: S₀ ≈ +0.5 to +2 pN·μm (puller).

### 5.13 The Smoluchowski Equation with Stresslet Interactions

The orientation distribution Ψ(p̂, x, t) evolves under the influence of:
- Fluid vorticity: ṗ̂ = Ω·p̂ - p̂(p̂·Ω·p̂)
- Fluid strain rate: ṗ̂ = λ(S·p̂ - p̂(p̂·S·p̂))
- Active reorientation (chemotaxis, phototaxis): ṗ̂ = (1/τ_c)(p̂_0 - p̂)
- Hydrodynamic interactions with other swimmers: ṗ̂ = ∑_{j≠i} ω_{ij}
- Rotational diffusion: D_r ∇_p̂² Ψ

The Smoluchowski equation is:

∂Ψ/∂t + ∇_x·(v Ψ) + ∇_p̂·(ṗ̂ Ψ) = D_r ∇_p̂² Ψ

where v = u + v₀ p̂. The fluid velocity u satisfies the Stokes equation with active stress:

0 = -∇p + μ∇²u + ∇·(n S₀ Q)

This is a closed system for (Ψ, u, p, Q, n). The nonlocal coupling through the fluid velocity makes this a challenging integro-differential equation.

### 5.14 Renormalization Group and Universal Scaling

The active hydrodynamic equations for pushers and pullers belong to different **universality classes**. Dynamic renormalization group (DRG) analysis reveals:

**Pushers (extensile, ζ < 0)**:
- The active stress is a relevant operator.
- The system flows to a strong-coupling fixed point.
- Scaling exponents differ from equilibrium: z = 2 (dynamic), χ = (2-d)/2 (roughness).
- Giant number fluctuations: ⟨ΔN²⟩ ∼ L^{2d-2} (α = 2 - 2/d).

**Pullers (contractile, ζ > 0)**:
- The active stress is irrelevant or marginal.
- The system flows to a fixed point with equilibrium-like exponents or a new fixed point with different symmetries.
- Fluctuations are suppressed relative to pushers.

**Polar flocks (Toner-Tu class)**:
- Different symmetry (polar vs. apolar).
- Galilean invariance is broken.
- Unique scaling: z = 2, χ = (2-d)/2, but with logarithmic corrections in d=2.
- Sound modes with non-diffusive propagation: ω ∼ k.

### 5.15 Summary: The Stresslet as the Fundamental Hydrodynamic Signature

The stresslet tensor S is the fundamental hydrodynamic signature of a force-free, torque-free microswimmer. Its sign classifies swimmers as pushers (S < 0) or pullers (S > 0), with radically different collective behaviors:

- **Pushers**: Extensile stress, hydrodynamic instability → active turbulence, negative viscosity, giant fluctuations, polar lanes.
- **Pullers**: Contractile stress, hydrodynamic stability → ordered streams, enhanced viscosity, suppressed fluctuations.

The active stress σ^a = n⟨S⟩ couples the orientation field to the fluid momentum equation, creating a feedback loop: orientation → active stress → flow → orientation advection. This feedback is the engine of active matter hydrodynamics. The mathematical framework – stresslet tensor, Smoluchowski equation, active stress, linear stability, renormalization group – provides a unified description of microbial swarming from the single-swimmer scale to the continuum scale.
