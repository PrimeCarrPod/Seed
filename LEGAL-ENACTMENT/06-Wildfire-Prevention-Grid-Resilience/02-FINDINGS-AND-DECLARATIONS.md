## SECTION 201. CONGRESSIONAL FINDINGS

The Congress finds the following:

**(1) Wildfire Crisis Severity**
Wildfires have increased in frequency, intensity, and destructiveness over the past two decades. The six largest fire seasons in United States recorded history have occurred since 2020. In 2025 alone, wildfires consumed 12.4 million acres, destroyed 47,000 structures, caused 312 fatalities, and generated $89 billion in direct damages.

**(2) Climate Change Acceleration**
Climate change is extending fire seasons, increasing the geographic extent of fire-prone areas, and intensifying fire behavior beyond historical norms. Average fire season length has increased from 120 days in 1990 to 265 days in 2025. Average temperatures in fire-prone regions have increased 2.3°F since 1980, reducing snowpack and extending the dry season.

**(3) Electrical Infrastructure Ignition Source**
Electrical infrastructure has been identified as the ignition source for multiple catastrophic wildfires. The 2018 Camp Fire (85 fatalities, $16.5B damages), 2020 Oregon Fire Siege (11 fatalities, $6B damages), and 2021 Marshall Fire (2 fatalities, $5B damages) were all ignited by electrical infrastructure.

**(4) Wildland-Urban Interface Expansion**
The Wildland-Urban Interface (WUI) has expanded by 40% since 1990, placing more communities at risk. An estimated 44 million homes are now located in the WUI. Current building codes in most WUI jurisdictions do not require fire-resistant construction materials or defensible space.

**(5) Vegetation Management Deficit**
Decades of fire suppression have created dangerous fuel loads on federal, state, and private lands. The USDA Forest Service estimates 80 million acres of national forestland require immediate vegetation management. Current treatment rates address less than 2% of the annual need.

**(6) Grid Hardening Underinvestment**
Electrical utilities have underinvested in grid hardening despite known ignition risks. Only 2,500 miles of the 200,000+ miles of overhead lines in high fire risk areas have been hardened. At current rates, full hardening would take 80+ years.

**(7) Early Warning Gap**
Early warning and detection systems cover less than 15% of high fire risk areas. Average alert time in covered areas is 8 minutes — insufficient for evacuation of vulnerable populations. False alarm rates of 35% reduce public trust and compliance.

**(8) Response Force Obsolescence**
Federal wildfire response forces rely on aging aircraft and inadequate coordination frameworks. The average age of the federal air tanker fleet is 52 years. Interagency coordination protocols have not been updated since 2003.

## SECTION 202. DECLARATIONS OF POLICY

**SEC. 202.** It is the policy of the United States that:

**(1) Wildfire Prevention as National Priority**
The prevention of catastrophic wildfire shall be a national priority on par with national defense, public health, and infrastructure investment. All federal agencies shall prioritize wildfire prevention in their planning, budgeting, and operations.

**(2) Climate Resilience Integration**
Climate change adaptation shall be integrated into all federal infrastructure planning, land management, and disaster risk reduction programs. Federal actions shall consider wildfire risk in all decisions affecting fire-prone areas.

**(3) Public Safety First**
The protection of human life shall be the paramount consideration in all wildfire management decisions. Property protection shall be secondary to life safety. Ecological considerations shall be integrated with public safety objectives.

**(4) Science-Based Decision Making**
Wildfire management decisions shall be based on the best available science. Research and development shall be prioritized to improve understanding of fire behavior, suppression effectiveness, and prevention strategies. Monitoring and adaptive management shall inform program adjustments.

**(5) Collaborative Governance**
Wildfire management shall be a collaborative effort among federal, state, Tribal, local, and private stakeholders. Decision-making shall be shared across jurisdictional boundaries. Traditional ecological knowledge shall be incorporated into planning and implementation.

**(6) Environmental Justice**
Wildfire prevention and response shall prioritize the needs of environmental justice communities. Funding shall be targeted to communities with the greatest need and least capacity. Community engagement shall be meaningful and sustained.

**(7) Fiscal Responsibility**
Wildfire prevention funding shall be managed with the highest standards of fiscal responsibility. Programs shall be evaluated regularly for effectiveness and efficiency. Waste, fraud, and abuse shall be prevented through robust oversight.

**(8) Innovation and Technology**
Innovation and technology development shall be encouraged in all aspects of wildfire management. Federal programs shall accelerate the deployment of proven technologies. Research partnerships with academia and industry shall be prioritized.

**(9) Workforce Development**
A skilled wildfire management workforce shall be developed and maintained. Training programs shall be expanded and modernized. Career pathways shall be created for wildfire prevention and response professionals.

**(10) International Leadership**
The United States shall be a global leader in wildfire prevention and management. International cooperation shall be strengthened through mutual aid agreements, technology transfer, and joint research programs.

---

## SECTION 203. PURPOSES

**SEC. 203.** The purposes of this Act are to:

**(1)** Establish a dedicated funding mechanism for wildfire prevention and grid resilience that is sustainable, reliable, and sufficient to address the scale of the crisis.

**(2)** Mandate vegetation management standards for utility rights-of-way and federal lands that reduce ignition risk to the maximum extent practicable.

**(3)** Update building codes for fire resistance in the Wildland-Urban Interface to protect lives and property.

**(4)** Deploy advanced early warning and detection systems that provide adequate time for evacuation and response.

**(5)** Modernize federal wildfire response capabilities to meet the challenges of the 21st century.

**(6)** Create economic incentives for fire-hardened infrastructure that accelerate private sector investment.

**(7)** Protect lives, property, and natural resources from catastrophic wildfire.

**(8)** Advance environmental justice through prioritized funding for disadvantaged communities.

**(9)** Reduce the economic burden of wildfire on individuals, businesses, and governments.

**(10)** Fulfill international obligations for disaster risk reduction and climate resilience.

## SECTION 204. DEFINITIONS

**SEC. 204.** In this Act:

**(1) "Wildland-Urban Interface (WUI)"**
The zone where structures and other human development meet or intermingle with undeveloped wildland or vegetative fuels, as mapped by the USDA Forest Service SILVIS laboratory or successor mapping program.

**(2) "Vegetation Management Plan (VMP)"**
A documented plan for the systematic management of vegetation within utility rights-of-way and on federal lands to reduce wildfire ignition risk, consistent with the standards established under Title I of this Act.

**(3) "Fire-Resistant Building Material"**
Any construction material that meets or exceeds the Class A fire resistance rating under ASTM E84 and has been certified under the Fire-Resistant Material Certification Program established under Sec. 302.

**(4) "Grid Hardening"**
The physical modification of electrical infrastructure to reduce ignition risk during high-wind, low-humidity events, including but not limited to covered conductor installation, undergrounding, rapid fault isolation, and fast curve switching.

**(5) "Public Safety Power Shutoff (PSPS)"**
The preemptive de-energization of electrical lines during extreme fire weather conditions, subject to the standards and protocols established under Sec. 204.

**(6) "Defensible Space"**
The area within 100 feet of a structure where vegetation and combustible materials are managed to reduce fire spread potential, as defined by the California Department of Forestry and Fire Protection (CAL FIRE) or equivalent state authority.

**(7) "Microgrid"**
A group of interconnected loads and distributed energy resources within clearly defined electrical boundaries that acts as a single controllable entity with respect to the grid, as defined by IEEE 2030.7.

**(8) "Covered Conductor"**
An electrical conductor encased in a thick layer of insulating material rated to withstand momentary contact with vegetation without arcing or ignition, consistent with IEEE 1202 standards.

**(9) "High Fire Risk Area"**
Any area designated as Fire Hazard Severity Zone "High" or "Very High" by the state fire marshal, or any area within the WUI as mapped by the USDA Forest Service.

**(10) "Disadvantaged Community"**
Any community identified as disadvantaged under the Justice40 Initiative, CalEnviroScreen, or equivalent state screening tool, or any community with median household income below 80% of the state median.

**(11) "Tribal Nation"**
Any federally recognized Indian Tribe, Alaska Native village, or Native Hawaiian community eligible for federal Indian programs.

**(12) "Environmental Justice"**
The fair treatment and meaningful involvement of all people regardless of race, color, national origin, or income with respect to the development, implementation, and enforcement of environmental laws, regulations, and policies.

**(13) "Carbon Pricing"**
A fee on fossil fuel combustion based on carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2e) emissions, as established under Sec. 601.

**(14) "Green Bond"**
A debt instrument issued by the U.S. Treasury specifically designated for climate resilience and environmental infrastructure investment.

**(15) "Traditional Ecological Knowledge"**
The evolving knowledge acquired by Indigenous and local peoples over hundreds or thousands of years through direct contact with the environment, encompassing spiritual relationships, cultural values, and practices.

## SECTION 227. SENSE OF CONGRESS

**SEC. 227.** It is the sense of Congress that:

**(1)** Wildfire prevention and grid resilience are national priorities requiring sustained investment over multiple decades.

**(2)** Climate change adaptation must be integrated into all infrastructure planning, land management, and disaster risk reduction programs.

**(3)** Federal agencies should prioritize nature-based solutions where feasible, including prescribed burning, forest thinning, and ecosystem restoration.

**(4)** Private sector innovation should be incentivized through public-private partnerships, tax credits, and regulatory certainty.

**(5)** Environmental justice communities should receive prioritized access to grant programs and technical assistance.

**(6)** International cooperation on wildfire management should be strengthened through mutual aid agreements, technology transfer, and joint research.

**(7)** The United States should be a global leader in wildfire prevention and management, sharing knowledge and resources with other nations.

**(8)** Traditional ecological knowledge held by Tribal nations should be respected and incorporated into wildfire management planning.

**(9)** The workforce of the future must be trained in wildfire prevention, grid resilience, and climate adaptation.

**(10)** Every dollar invested in prevention returns nearly six dollars in avoided damages, making this Act one of the most cost-effective investments Congress can make.
