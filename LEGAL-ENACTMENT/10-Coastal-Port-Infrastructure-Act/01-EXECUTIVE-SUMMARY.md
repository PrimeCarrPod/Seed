The legislation represents the most significant federal investment in maritime infrastructure since the construction of the Interstate Highway System and reflects the understanding that port infrastructure is not merely a transportation asset but a foundational element of national economic security, environmental stewardship, and global competitiveness.

The Act authorizes $187.5 billion in federal investment over a ten-year period (Fiscal Years 2027–2037), distributed across six major programmatic titles: Port Modernization ($62.5 billion), Supply Chain Resilience ($35 billion), Maritime Transportation ($25 billion), Coastal Resilience ($32 billion), Environmental Protection ($18 billion), and Funding Mechanisms ($15 billion direct appropriation plus leveraged investment). This investment represents the single largest federal commitment to port infrastructure since the Harbor Maintenance Tax Act of 1986 and reflects the urgent need to address decades of deferred maintenance, capacity constraints, climate vulnerability, and technological obsolescence across the national port system.

The economic rationale for this investment is compelling. The Act builds on and extends the foundational work of previous federal infrastructure legislation, including the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act of 2021, the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022, and the CHIPS and Science Act of 2022. While these earlier acts included significant port-related provisions, none addressed the full scope of the nation's port infrastructure challenge. The Coastal Port Infrastructure Act of 2026 fills this gap by providing the comprehensive, coordinated, and sustained investment that the nation's ports require.

## VI. THE PROBLEM: CURRENT STATE OF U.S. PORT INFRASTRUCTURE

### A. Navigation Channel Deficiencies

The United States maintains approximately 1,200 federal navigation channels along its Atlantic, Pacific, Gulf, and Great Lakes coastlines, encompassing over 25,000 miles of navigable waterways. These channels serve as the arterial network connecting domestic and international commerce to the national economy. They enable the movement of over 2.4 billion tons of foreign and domestic cargo annually, support the nation's energy security through the import of crude oil and export of refined products and liquefied natural gas, and provide the access necessary for the U.S. military to project power and sustain operations worldwide. Despite their critical importance, the current state of these channels represents a systemic failure of sustained federal investment that has accumulated over decades and now threatens the nation's economic competitiveness and security.

**(1) Depth and Width Deficiencies.** As of 2026, only 35 percent of federal navigation channels are maintained at their fully authorized depth and width. The remaining 65 percent suffer from sedimentation, erosion, and inadequate dredging, creating restrictions that limit vessel size, reduce cargo capacity, and increase transit times. The average depth deficiency across restricted channels is 4.2 feet, forcing container vessels to reduce cargo loads by an average of 12 percent per voyage. This means that for every 100 vessels that transit these channels, the equivalent of 12 vessels' worth of cargo capacity is wasted—a massive inefficiency that increases per-unit shipping costs, reduces the competitiveness of U.S. exports, and contributes to highway congestion as shippers shift to overland transportation modes to compensate for maritime capacity limitations.

**(2) Dredging Backlog.** The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers estimates a dredging backlog exceeding 300 million cubic yards across all federal navigation channels. At current funding levels, this backlog would require more than 25 years to eliminate—an unacceptable timeline given the urgency of the nation's port infrastructure needs. The Harbor Maintenance Trust Fund, which receives revenues from the Harbor Maintenance Tax on imported and domestic cargo, has accumulated an unspent balance of $9.2 billion while critical dredging needs go unmet. This fiscal anomaly—collecting dedicated revenues while failing to spend them on their intended purpose—represents a failure of budgetary discipline that has directly contributed to the deterioration of the nation's navigation channels. The Act addresses this by requiring that all Harbor Maintenance Trust Fund revenues be fully spent on harbor maintenance and improvement within two years of collection.

**(3) Channel Access Limitations.** Fifteen of the nation's top 20 container ports face channel depth restrictions that prevent them from fully accommodating post-Panamax and ultra-large container vessels (ULCVs) with capacities exceeding 14,000 TEU. This limitation forces cargo to be transshipped through foreign ports—primarily in Mexico, Canada, and the Caribbean—or distributed across multiple smaller vessels, increasing costs by an estimated $8–12 billion annually. The economic implications extend beyond direct shipping costs: transshipment adds time to supply chains, increases the risk of cargo damage and loss, reduces supply chain visibility, and cedes economic activity to foreign port workers and service providers. The Act's Navigation Channel Improvement Program directly addresses this limitation by authorizing the deepening and widening of navigation channels at the nation's top 50 container ports to accommodate the largest vessels in the global fleet.

**(4) Sedimentation Patterns.** Climate change is accelerating sedimentation rates at many navigation channels through increased precipitation intensity, altered river flows, and coastal erosion. Channels that historically required dredging every 3–5 years now require annual maintenance in some cases, straining the capacity of the Corps of Engineers and increasing costs. The Act includes funding for research into advanced dredging technologies, real-time sedimentation monitoring, and predictive modeling to optimize dredging schedules and reduce costs. The Act also funds the development of autonomous dredging vessels that can operate continuously with minimal crew, reducing the cost of channel maintenance by an estimated 30–40 percent.

**(5) Beneficial Use of Dredged Material.** The Act establishes a national policy of maximizing the beneficial use of dredged material, recognizing that dredged sediment is a resource rather than a waste product. Beneficial uses include coastal restoration, habitat creation, beach nourishment, construction material production, and agricultural soil amendment. The Act requires that a minimum of 50 percent of all dredged material from federal navigation channels be put to beneficial use, reducing disposal costs while providing environmental and economic benefits. The Act funds research into new beneficial use technologies and establishes regional dredged material management plans that identify beneficial use opportunities and coordinate dredging schedules with restoration needs.

**(6) Channel Maintenance Technology.** The Act funds the development and deployment of advanced channel maintenance technologies that reduce costs, improve efficiency, and minimize environmental impacts. The Act establishes a Channel Maintenance Technology Center within the Corps of Engineers that coordinates research, development, and deployment of these technologies.

The physical infrastructure of U.S. port facilities has deteriorated significantly due to age, heavy use, and exposure to corrosive marine environments. The economic cost of these delays is substantial: each minute of truck wait time at a major container port costs the economy approximately $100 in lost productivity, excess fuel consumption, and increased emissions. The 2011 Tohoku earthquake and tsunami in Japan caused $12 billion in port infrastructure damage, demonstrating the vulnerability of port facilities to seismic events. The Act requires that all port infrastructure in seismically active regions be designed to current seismic standards and that existing infrastructure be retrofitted to meet these standards. The Act funds seismic vulnerability assessments at all West Coast ports and provides grants for seismic retrofitting of critical facilities.

**(4) Compound Climate Risks.** The interaction of multiple climate hazards—sea-level rise, increased storm intensity, altered precipitation patterns, and elevated temperatures—creates compound risks that exceed the sum of individual hazards. The Act requires port authorities to assess compound climate risks and design resilience measures that address the full range of potential climate impacts. **(5) Extreme Heat Impacts.** Rising temperatures pose direct threats to port operations, including heat-related health risks for outdoor workers, thermal expansion of infrastructure materials, reduced equipment efficiency, and increased cooling demands for temperature-sensitive cargo. The Act requires port authorities to develop heat action plans that protect workers from heat-related illness, incorporate thermal expansion into infrastructure design, and ensure adequate cooling capacity for temperature-sensitive operations. The Act funds research into heat-resistant materials and cooling technologies for port applications.

**(6) Water Supply and Drought.** Port operations require significant quantities of water for dust suppression, vessel ballast, fire suppression, and other purposes. Drought conditions can constrain water availability, limiting port operations and increasing costs. The Act requires port authorities to develop water conservation plans that reduce water consumption, increase water recycling, and develop alternative water sources including desalination and rainwater harvesting. The Act funds the development of water recycling systems at major ports that reduce freshwater consumption by a minimum of 50 percent.

### D. Supply Chain Fragility

The average U.S. manufacturer now holds only 15 days of inventory for critical imported components, compared to 30 days in 2000. This lean inventory strategy reduces costs under normal conditions but creates extreme vulnerability to disruptions. The Act's investments in port capacity, supply chain visibility, and resilience are designed to restore the buffer capacity that the nation's supply chain requires without forcing a return to inefficient inventory practices.

**(3) Labor Market Constraints.** The port workforce faces significant challenges including an aging demographic, difficulty recruiting younger workers, and the need for new skills related to automation, digital systems, and clean energy technologies.
