# PART 004: California Underwater Demonstration Site Gamma — Santa Barbara Channel Northern Margin (34°24'N, 120°05'W): Hydrocarbon Seep Geology, Active Tectonic Basin Deformation, and Submerged Community Deployment in a Complex Multi-Hazard Environment

## 4.1 Geographic Setting, Tectonic Framework, and the Rationale for the Santa Barbara Channel Deployment

California Underwater Demonstration Site Gamma occupies a position on the northern margin of the Santa Barbara Channel, at precisely 34°24'N latitude and 120°05'W longitude, approximately 12 kilometers south of Point Conception and 25 kilometers offshore of the Gaviota coastline of Santa Barbara County, at a depth of 55 meters below mean sea level on the gently seaward-dipping (approximately 1° to 2°, or 18 to 35 meters per kilometer) surface of the inner continental shelf, within the western portion of the Santa Barbara Channel — an east-west-trending, tectonically active, structurally complex, hydrocarbon-rich, semi-enclosed marine basin that extends for approximately 130 kilometers from Point Conception in the west to the Hueneme-Mugu submarine canyon system in the east, bounded on the north by the steep, fault-controlled, uplifted, actively deforming Santa Ynez Mountains and the southward-verging, seismogenic Santa Ynez Fault system, and on the south by the east-west-trending, structurally elevated, partly emergent, partly submerged Santa Rosa Island, Santa Cruz Island, Anacapa Island, and Santa Barbara Island — the northern Channel Islands — and their associated submarine ridges and banks, and underlain by a thick (2 to 6 kilometers), complexly deformed, petroleum-generating and petroleum-accumulating sequence of Cenozoic sedimentary rocks — the Monterey, Sisquoc, and Pico Formations — that have been folded, faulted, rotated, and uplifted by the ongoing, Pliocene-to-Recent, north-south-directed crustal compression associated with the "Big Bend" of the San Andreas Fault system, where the predominantly right-lateral strike-slip plate-boundary motion is transpressional — combining the horizontal, dextral shear with a component of the north-south crustal shortening — producing the east-west-trending, south-verging, actively folding and faulting anticlinal ridges, the synclinal troughs, and the thrust-fault systems that characterize the structural geology of the Santa Barbara Channel and the adjacent onshore and offshore regions, and that are responsible for the high level of seismicity (the region has experienced more than a dozen earthquakes of magnitude 5.0 or greater in the instrumental record, including the 1925 Santa Barbara earthquake at magnitude 6.8, the 1978 Santa Barbara earthquake at magnitude 5.1, and the 2020 Montecito earthquake at magnitude 5.2), the active hydrocarbon seepage (the natural oil and gas seeps of the Santa Barbara Channel — particularly the prolific Coal Oil Point seep field, located approximately 10 kilometers west of Site Gamma, which releases an estimated 100 to 150 barrels of liquid petroleum and 5 to 10 million cubic feet of natural gas per day from the fractured and faulted reservoir rocks of the Miocene Monterey Formation — are among the largest and the most active marine hydrocarbon seeps in the world, and the presence of the seeps, and of the associated tar mounds, tar mats, and tar-impregnated sediments on the seafloor, must be accommodated in the design of the Atlantis pod anchoring system and the seabed-interface components, which must resist the chemical degradation, the weakening, and the embrittlement that can result from the prolonged exposure to the hydrocarbons and the hydrogen sulfide that are present, in varying concentrations, in the near-surface sediments of the active seep areas), and the extreme geological and geomorphological complexity that, collectively, make the Santa Barbara Channel one of the most challenging — and, from the perspective of the comprehensive validation of the Atlantis subsea habitation architecture, one of the most informative — environments on the California continental margin for the deployment of an underwater demonstration community.

The selection of Site Gamma as the third California demonstration site was motivated, in large part, by the desire to test the Atlantis architecture in an environment that combines — within a single, geographically compact, logistically accessible, scientifically well-characterized, and institutionally well-supported (through the ongoing research and monitoring programs of the University of California, Santa Barbara, the U.S. Geological Survey, the Bureau of Ocean Energy Management, and the various state and federal agencies that regulate the offshore oil and gas activities in the channel) locality — several of the most challenging geohazards and geo-environmental conditions that the Atlantis communities will encounter, to varying degrees, at many of the candidate deployment sites across the global continental margins: the active tectonic deformation (the folding, the faulting, the uplift, the subsidence, and the seismicity), the hydrocarbon-seep-related geochemistry and the associated corrosion, biofouling, and sediment-stability issues, the complex, multi-layered, variably indurated, variably fractured, variably hydrocarbon-impregnated sedimentary-rock substrate, and the potential for the earthquake-triggered submarine landslides and the turbidity currents that could, even under non-catastrophic, non-crustal-displacement conditions, pose a threat to the integrity of the Atlantis pods and the safety of their occupants. The successful deployment, the sustained operation, and the survival — through both the normal, non-catastrophic environmental loading and the simulated, or the actual, crustal-displacement-event loading — of the Atlantis pod at Site Gamma will demonstrate that the Atlantis architecture is robust, adaptable, and survivable across a far broader range of geological, oceanographic, and geochemical conditions than can be tested at the relatively benign (geotechnically competent, seismically less active, hydrocarbon-free) Site Alpha (Monterey Canyon) and Site Beta (Gulf of the Farallones), and will provide the engineering data, the operational experience, and the confidence that are necessary to extend the Atlantis deployment program, in the later phases, to the even more challenging environments — the subduction-zone forearcs, the active volcanic arcs, the methane-hydrate-bearing continental slopes, and the permafrost-underlain Arctic shelves — that will be encountered as the ECD survival infrastructure is expanded to a global scale, encompassing the full range of the tectonic, the climatic, and the oceanographic settings that characterize the Earth's continental margins.

## 4.2 Substrate Geology, Geotechnical Properties, and Hydrocarbon-Seep Geochemistry at Site Gamma

The seabed substrate at Site Gamma — at the precise coordinates 34°24'N, 120°05'W, on the northern margin of the Santa Barbara Channel, at 55 meters depth — consists of a complex, multi-layered, variably lithified, variably fractured, variably hydrocarbon-impregnated sequence of late Cenozoic marine sedimentary rocks that includes, from the seabed downward: (1) a thin (0.3 to 1.5 meters), discontinuous, Holocene-age, bioturbated, silty-clay, hemipelagic drape, containing variable amounts of the tar (the biodegraded, weathered, heavy-hydrocarbon residue of the natural oil seeps, which has accumulated, over the millennia, as the sticky, semi-solid, black, asphalt-like masses that are found on the beaches of Santa Barbara and that coat the surface of the seabed in the active seep areas) and of the authigenic carbonate (the calcite, the aragonite, and the dolomite that precipitate, at or near the seabed, as a byproduct of the microbial — predominantly archaeal — anaerobic oxidation of the methane (AOM) that diffuses upward, from the deeper, gas-charged reservoir rocks, through the fractured and the faulted overlying sediments, and that, at the sulfate-methane transition zone (SMTZ), typically a few centimeters to a few meters below the seabed, is oxidized, by the consortia of the methanotrophic archaea and the sulfate-reducing bacteria, using the sulfate (SO₄²⁻) dissolved in the seawater-derived pore fluid as the terminal electron acceptor, producing the bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) and the hydrogen sulfide (H₂S), the bicarbonate precipitating as the authigenic carbonate — the calcite, the aragonite, the dolomite, and the high-magnesium calcite — that cements the sediment grains together, forming the hard, resistant, low-porosity, irregularly shaped, pod-like, slab-like, or chimney-like concretions, nodules, and crusts that are characteristic of the methane-seep environments worldwide and that, at Site Gamma, complicate the installation of the helical-pile anchoring system, because the piles, during the driving or the drilling process, may encounter the hard, cemented carbonate layers — with UCS values that can exceed 50 to 100 MPa — that cannot be penetrated by the standard helical-pile installation equipment, requiring either the pre-drilling of the pile holes through the carbonate layers or the repositioning of the piles to avoid the carbonate obstacles); (2) the Pico Formation — a Pliocene-to-Pleistocene (approximately 1 to 5 million years before present), weakly to moderately indurated, fine-grained, predominantly siltstone and claystone sequence, deposited in a deep-marine, bathyal (200 to 2,000 meters depth), turbidite-fan and hemipelagic environment, with interbedded, thin to medium, normally graded, fine-to-medium-grained, arkosic-to-lithic sandstone beds (the "turbidite sands" of the Pico Formation), representing the episodic, catastrophic, sediment-gravity-flow events that transported the coarse-grained, terrigenous sediment from the adjacent, rapidly uplifting, actively eroding Santa Ynez Mountains and the Coast Ranges into the deep-marine basin of the Santa Barbara Channel; (3) the Sisquoc Formation — a late Miocene to early Pliocene (approximately 4 to 8 million years before present), moderately indurated, diatomaceous, siliceous, organic-rich, finely laminated, dark-brown to black, petroliferous mudstone and shale, with interbedded, thin, fine-grained, silty to sandy, phosphatic, and dolomitic layers, representing the deposition, under the highly productive, upwelling-favorable, oxygen-minimum-zone conditions of the late Miocene Pacific margin, of the organic-rich, biosiliceous sediments — the diatoms, the radiolarians, the silicoflagellates, and the coccolithophorids — that are the source rocks for the prolific petroleum accumulations of the Santa Barbara Channel (the Monterey Formation, which is the lateral, deeper-water, more siliceous, more organic-rich equivalent of the Sisquoc Formation, is the primary petroleum source rock of the California continental margin, and the oil and the gas that are generated by the thermal maturation of the Monterey organic matter, at depths of 2 to 5 kilometers and at temperatures of 80°C to 150°C, migrate, along the fractures, the faults, and the permeable, sandy carrier beds, upward into the overlying, shallower, more porous, more permeable reservoir rocks — the Pico and the Sisquoc sandstones — and, where the migration pathways intersect the seafloor, escape into the ocean as the natural oil and gas seeps); and (4) the Monterey Formation — a middle to late Miocene (approximately 8 to 17 million years before present), moderately to strongly indurated, highly siliceous, highly organic-rich, finely laminated, dark-brown to black, petroliferous, porcelanitic, cherty, and phosphatic mudstone, shale, and porcelanite, representing the classic, world-renowned, petroleum-source-rock facies of the California Miocene Monterey Formation, the most prolific petroleum source rock in California, responsible for the generation of the vast majority of the oil and the gas that have been produced, over the past century, from the onshore and the offshore fields of the Santa Maria Basin, the Santa Barbara Channel, the Ventura Basin, and the Los Angeles Basin, and the primary target — at depths of 3 to 6 kilometers — of the ongoing, and the planned, deep-penetration, subsalt, subthrust, high-pressure, high-temperature, ultra-deepwater (the depths are not ultra-deepwater in the global, Gulf-of-Mexico or Brazil-pre-salt sense — the Santa Barbara Channel is a relatively shallow-water, shelf-depth basin — but the drilling depths, the reservoir pressures, and the reservoir temperatures are extreme, by any standard, and the technical challenges of the deep-penetration drilling in the complexly deformed, fractured, overpressured, gas-charged Monterey Formation are among the most formidable in the global petroleum industry) exploration and development drilling programs that target the deep, subsalt, Monterey-sourced petroleum accumulations beneath the Santa Barbara Channel.

The geotechnical properties of the Pico and the Sisquoc Formation sediments at Site Gamma — as determined from the geotechnical boreholes GAM-01, GAM-02, and GAM-03, drilled to depths of 80, 120, and 180 meters below the seabed, respectively, using the same remotely operated seafloor drill rig (the "SeaMole-1") described in Part 003 for the Site Beta deployment — are intermediate, in their engineering characteristics, between the competent, well-indurated, moderately strong Purisima Formation sandstones of Site Alpha (Monterey Canyon) and the incompetent, intensely sheared, low-strength Franciscan mélange matrix of Site Beta (Gulf of the Farallones). The Pico Formation siltstones and claystones at the Site Gamma depth have representative UCS values of 5 to 15 MPa (classifying them as "weak" to "moderately weak" rocks, per the ISRM classification), effective stress friction angles (φ') of 22° to 28°, and cohesion intercepts (c') of 50 to 200 kPa (indicating that the sediments, while not strongly cemented, do possess a finite, if modest, cohesive strength component that contributes to their short-term, undrained stability), and the interbedded Pico sandstone beds have correspondingly higher UCS values (15 to 30 MPa), higher friction angles (28° to 35°), and higher cohesion intercepts (100 to 500 kPa). The Sisquoc Formation at the Site Gamma depth — the upper, less deeply buried, less thermally mature, less indurated portion of the Sisquoc sequence — has similar, or slightly lower, UCS values (3 to 12 MPa), similar friction angles (20° to 28°), and similar cohesion intercepts (20 to 150 kPa), but the presence of the diatomaceous, biosiliceous component — the diatom frustules, the radiolarian tests, and the silicoflagellate skeletons that comprise, in some of the Sisquoc beds, 30 to 60 percent of the sediment volume — imparts a distinctive, problematic, geotechnical behavior to the Sisquoc Formation that is not present in the more clastic-dominated, less biosiliceous Pico Formation: the diatomaceous sediment, when it is subjected to the cyclic, reversing, earthquake- or wave-induced shear loading, can undergo a dramatic, sudden, and, in many cases, catastrophic loss of strength — a phenomenon known as "cyclic softening" or "liquefaction-like behavior" — due to the collapse of the delicate, hollow, high-porosity, high-water-content diatom frustules under the imposed shear stresses, resulting in the generation of the excess pore-water pressures, the loss of the effective confining stress, and the transformation of the sediment from a solid-like, load-bearing material to a fluid-like, non-load-bearing slurry, with the consequent loss of the bearing capacity and the lateral resistance of the foundation elements — the anchor piles and the pod base — that are embedded in the softened, liquefied sediment, a failure mode that, if not properly accounted for in the foundation design (through the use of the appropriately conservative, cyclic-softening-adjusted, residual-strength parameters — the φ'_residual of 12° to 18° and the c'_residual of zero, as determined from the cyclic triaxial and the cyclic simple-shear testing of the Sisquoc Formation samples), could lead to the catastrophic failure of the Site Gamma Atlantis pod anchoring system during a major earthquake (such as a magnitude 7+ event on the Santa Ynez Fault or the other, nearby, active faults) that occurs, even under non-catastrophic, non-crustal-displacement conditions, during the 50-year design life.

The hydrocarbon-seep geochemistry at Site Gamma — the concentration, the composition, and the spatial distribution of the liquid and the gaseous hydrocarbons in the near-surface sediments — has been characterized, during the site-survey phase, through the analysis of the sediment cores (the headspace-gas and the solvent-extractable-organic-matter analyses, performed at the UCSB Marine Science Institute's petroleum-geochemistry laboratory, using the gas-chromatography, the gas-chromatography-mass-spectrometry, and the stable-carbon-isotope-ratio-mass-spectrometry techniques that are standard in the petroleum-geochemistry industry), through the deployment of the in-situ, seafloor, benthic-chamber flux measurements (the "Lander" deployments, in which a tethered, autonomous, seafloor instrument platform — equipped with the dissolved-oxygen, the dissolved-methane, the dissolved-hydrogen-sulfide, the pH, the redox-potential, and the temperature sensors, and with the time-series, water-sampling, syringe-and-bag-based, sediment-pore-fluid samplers — is deployed at the Site Gamma location for periods of 24 to 72 hours, measuring the fluxes — the rates of the advection and the diffusion — of the methane, the hydrogen sulfide, and the other, chemically reactive, biogeochemically important, dissolved species across the sediment-water interface), and through the visual observations and the sampling conducted during the ROV (remotely operated vehicle) dives (the "Jason" and the "Ventana" ROVs, operated by MBARI and by the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution, which have conducted, over the past two decades, extensive, detailed, high-resolution, video-and-still-image, sampling, and in-situ-measurement surveys of the hydrocarbon-seep communities — the chemosynthetic, sulfide-oxidizing, bacterial mats, the sulfide-oxidizing, symbiotic, tube-worm, clam, and mussel aggregations, and the associated, heterotrophic, predator, scavenger, and detritivore fauna — of the Santa Barbara Channel, providing a rich, well-documented, and easily accessible baseline of the ecological and the geochemical conditions at the Site Gamma location). The concentration of the liquid hydrocarbons — the total petroleum hydrocarbons (TPH), measured as the solvent-extractable, C10-to-C40, aliphatic-and-aromatic-hydrocarbon fraction — in the upper 1 to 2 meters of the Site Gamma sediment ranges from less than 10 milligrams per kilogram (mg/kg) — the background, non-seep-affected, natural, biogenic-hydrocarbon level — in the areas remote from the active seep vents and the tar mounds, to more than 10,000 mg/kg (1 percent by weight) in the areas directly adjacent to the active seep vents, where the migrating oil, gas, and formation water, emerging from the fractures and the faults in the underlying Sisquoc and Monterey Formations, saturate the near-surface sediment, forming the tar-impregnated, anoxic, reducing, strong-smelling (the hydrogen sulfide, the volatile organic compounds, and the reduced sulfur species impart a distinctive, pungent, "rotten-egg" and "gasoline-like" odor to the seep-affected sediment), black, sticky, cohesive, low-shear-strength sediment that is encountered, in the ROV video surveys, as the irregular, patchy, meter-to-decametre-scale, dark-colored, biota-poor (the hydrogen sulfide, at the high concentrations — exceeding 1 to 10 millimoles per liter in the pore fluid — that characterize the active seep vents, is toxic to most of the aerobic, benthic, multicellular organisms, although the specialized, chemosynthetic, sulfide-oxidizing, symbiotic fauna — the vestimentiferan tube worms, the vesicomyid and the solemyid clams, and the bathymodiolid mussels — thrive in these environments, forming the dense, biomass-rich, biodiverse, ecologically unique communities that are the focus of the extensive, ongoing, marine-biological and biogeochemical research in the Santa Barbara Channel). The concentration of the dissolved methane — the CH₄ that is dissolved in the sediment pore fluid and that, at the concentrations exceeding the saturation limit (approximately 1 to 2 millimoles per liter, depending on the temperature, the salinity, and the hydrostatic pressure), exsolves as the free-gas bubbles that are visible, in the ROV video surveys, as the streams of the bubbles escaping from the seafloor and rising, dissolving, and dispersing in the overlying water column — ranges from less than 0.1 millimoles per liter (the background, atmospheric-equilibrium, non-seep-affected level) to more than 10 millimoles per liter at the active seep vents, and the presence of the free-gas methane — which, at the 55-meter depth of Site Gamma, is stable as the gaseous phase (the methane-hydrate stability zone, where the methane, combined with the water, forms the solid, ice-like, clathrate compound, the methane hydrate, begins at water depths greater than approximately 500 to 600 meters, depending on the bottom-water temperature and the dissolved-gas composition, and Site Gamma, at 55 meters depth, is well above the hydrate-stability-zone, so that the methane, if it is present in the sediment at the concentrations exceeding the solubility limit, will exist as the free-gas phase, not as the solid hydrate phase) — in the Site Gamma sediment is a potential hazard, for the Atlantis pod and for the anchoring system, because the free-gas-charged sediment, when it is disturbed — by the pod installation, by the anchor-pile driving, by the earthquake shaking, or by the turbidite-flow loading — can undergo the sudden, rapid, explosive release of the trapped gas, a phenomenon known as the "shallow-gas blowout" or the "gas-pockmark eruption" that has been responsible for the catastrophic loss of the buoyancy, the stability, and the structural integrity of numerous offshore platforms, pipelines, and foundations around the world (the most famous example being the 1979 Ixtoc I blowout in the Bay of Campeche, Mexico, where the penetration of a shallow, overpressured, gas-charged zone, at approximately 100 meters below the seabed, during the drilling of an offshore oil well, resulted in the catastrophic, uncontrolled release of the gas and the oil, the loss of the drilling rig, and the largest marine oil spill — prior to the 2010 Deepwater Horizon disaster — in history), and because the gas, when it is released, reduces the density — and, consequently, the bearing capacity — of the sediment, potentially causing the settlement, the tilting, or the complete-foundation-failure of the Atlantis pod, and, being flammable and, in the confined, enclosed environment of the pod interior, potentially explosive, the gas that migrates along the anchor-pile annuli and accumulates in the pod's bilge spaces or in the inter-pod tunnel network constitutes a fire and an explosion hazard that must be managed, through the incorporation of the gas-detection, the gas-alarm, the gas-ventilation, and the gas-isolation systems into the pod and the tunnel designs, and through the careful selection of the pod location — using the high-resolution, chirp-sonar sub-bottom profiling and the ROV video surveys — to avoid, as far as is possible, the areas of the active, high-flux gas seepage.

## 4.3 Oceanographic Regime, Current Dynamics, and Wave Exposure in the Semi-Enclosed Santa Barbara Basin

The oceanographic regime of the Santa Barbara Channel is dominated by the interaction of the California Current System — the broad, slow, southward-flowing, eastern-boundary current that transports the cool, fresh, nutrient-rich, subarctic-origin water along the coast of California — with the complex, semi-enclosed, basin-and-island topography of the channel, which funnels, steers, accelerates, decelerates, and deflects the regional currents, generating a complex, three-dimensional, seasonally and tidally modulated, mesoscale-and-submesoscale-eddy-rich circulation pattern that has been the subject of extensive, ongoing, observational and modeling studies by the researchers at UCSB, at the Naval Postgraduate School, at MBARI, and at the other institutions that participate in the Santa Barbara Channel research community, and that presents a dynamic, energetic, and, at times, challenging environment for the deployment and the operation of the Atlantis subsea habitation module. The mean current velocity at the Site Gamma depth of 55 meters, as measured by the ADCP mooring deployed during the site-survey phase, is 0.15 meters per second (range 0.02 to 0.7 m/s), with the flow direction predominantly along-channel — the east-southeastward flow (the "down-channel" flow, toward the Hueneme-Mugu submarine canyon) and the west-northwestward flow (the "up-channel" flow, toward Point Conception) — reflecting the channeling of the regional, shelf, and slope currents by the basin-and-island topography, and with the periodic, semidiurnal (12.42-hour period) tidal-current oscillations, of amplitude 0.05 to 0.15 meters per second, superimposed on the subtidal, wind-driven, and geostrophic mean flow. During the winter storm events — when the intense, extratropical cyclones, the "Pineapple Express" or the "atmospheric river" systems, approach the California coast from the west-southwest, generating the strong, sustained, southerly-to-southwesterly winds (20 to 30 meters per second, gusting to 40 to 50 m/s) and the high, long-period (14 to 20 seconds), highly energetic, North Pacific swell waves that propagate, from the distant storm-generation regions of the Aleutian Low and the Gulf of Alaska, across the eastern North Pacific and into the Santa Barbara Channel, where they are partially sheltered, partially refracted, and partially diffracted by the offshore islands and the complex bathymetry, but where they can still produce significant-wave heights (H_s) of 4 to 6 meters at the offshore buoys and 2 to 4 meters at the Site Gamma location — the current velocity at Site Gamma can be intensified, by the wind-driven, down-channel, geostrophically adjusted flow, to values of 0.5 to 1.0 meters per second, and the wave-induced, near-bed, orbital velocities — the oscillatory, reversing, sinuous flow that the water particles execute, at the seabed, as the surface waves pass overhead — can reach 0.3 to 0.6 meters per second, values that are sufficient to resuspend the fine-grained, unconsolidated, hemipelagic seabed sediment, generating the near-bed, suspended-sediment clouds — the "nepheloid layers" — that can reduce the visibility, increase the sedimentation rate on the pod surfaces, and, over the 50-year design life, contribute to the sediment burial and the sediment-loading of the pod, the inter-pod tunnels, and the anchoring system, phenomena that must be accommodated, in the site design, through the elevation of the pod base above the seabed (the pod is supported on a 2-meter-high, six-legged, truss-type, LBFRP-001 support structure that elevates the pressure hull clear of the seabed, reducing the rate of the sediment burial and the sediment loading, and providing a clearance — the "air-gap" between the seabed and the hull — that prevents the hull from being trapped, and potentially crushed, by the accumulating sediment), and through the periodic, ROV-conducted, sediment-removal and sediment-redistribution operations that maintain the site geometry and the pod accessibility over the design life.

## 4.4 Turbidite Flow Modeling at Site Gamma: The Hueneme-Mugu Canyon Conduit and the Coastal Range Drainage Integration

The turbidite-flow exposure at Site Gamma, under the crustal displacement recession-phase scenario, is governed by the drainage of the vast volume of the seawater — the temporary inland sea that, as modeled in Part 04 of the CDGA and in Part 015 of the present mega-analysis, will occupy the California Central Valley, the Los Angeles Basin, the Ventura Basin, and the other, lower-elevation, coastal and interior basins — through the complex, interconnected, canyon-and-channel systems of the southern California continental borderland, of which the Hueneme-Mugu submarine canyon system — a major, deeply incised, dendritic, meandering, sediment-transporting, turbidity-current conduit that heads, in multiple, coalescing, headward-eroding branches, near the shoreline at Port Hueneme and Point Mugu, approximately 80 kilometers east-southeast of Site Gamma, and that extends, as a continuous, sinuous, levee-bounded, deeply entrenched channel, for approximately 100 kilometers across the continental shelf and the continental slope, to the abyssal depths (3,000 to 4,000 meters) of the Santa Monica Basin and the San Pedro Basin — is the dominant, and by far the largest, the most active, and the most efficient, sediment-transport pathway in the Santa Barbara Channel region, capable of conveying, during a single, large, earthquake-triggered or storm-triggered turbidity-current event, the hundreds of millions to the billions of cubic meters of the sediment — the sand, the silt, the clay, and the gravel — from the nearshore, the shelf, and the upper-slope sediment-accumulation zones to the deep, abyssal, sediment-fan depositional systems, resculpting the seafloor morphology, redistributing the sediment, the organic matter, and the pollutants (the DDT, the PCBs, the heavy metals, and the other, industrial-era, anthropogenic contaminants that have accumulated, over the past century, in the sediments of the Santa Barbara Channel and the adjacent coastal basins), and maintaining the canyon as a dynamic, active, geomorphologically evolving, turbidity-current-dominated, submarine-drainage system. The CFD simulations of the recession-phase turbidite flows at Site Gamma, presented in Part 015, indicate that the flows, originating from the flooded Central Valley and the Los Angeles Basin and funneled through the Hueneme-Mugu canyon system, will sweep across the northern margin of the Santa Barbara Channel, at velocities of 12 to 20 meters per second and at suspended-sediment concentrations of 30 to 150 grams per liter, and will subject the Site Gamma Atlantis pod to lateral loads of 120 to 200 kN — loads that are within the design capacity of the eight-helical-pile anchoring system (with each pile providing a working load of 200 kN, for a total capacity of 1,600 kN — a factor-of-8 safety margin over the maximum predicted load), provided that the piles are installed in the areas of the Site Gamma seabed that are free of the hydrocarbon-seep-related tar mounds, the hard, authigenic-carbonate-cemented layers, and the free-gas-charged, potentially liquefiable, diatomaceous sediment, and that the piles are extended to the depth — 15 to 20 meters below the seabed — that is sufficient to anchor them into the competent, relatively unaffected, relatively gas-free, relatively strong Pico Formation siltstones and sandstones that underlie the problematic, seep-affected, near-surface sediment layer. The CFD results also indicate that the turbidite flows, as they sweep across the northern channel margin, will be deflected, to some extent, by the submarine-ridge topography — the anticlinal, fault-bounded, east-west-trending ridges that characterize the channel's structural geology — and that the selection of the precise Site Gamma location — within the "flow-shadow" zone on the northern flank of one of these anticlinal ridges, where the flow velocity and the boulder-impact probability are reduced, relative to the channel-axis values, by factors of 2 to 4 — is a critical determinant of the pod's survivability and that the detailed, site-specific CFD simulations, using the high-resolution (10-meter-grid-spacing) bathymetry and the site-specific geotechnical and oceanographic input parameters, are essential for the final, optimized, site-selection decision.

The Site Gamma deployment, with its combination of the active tectonics, the hydrocarbon-seep geochemistry, the complex, multi-layered, variably competent, variably problematic substrate geology, and the high, recession-phase, turbidite-flow exposure, represents the most demanding, the most comprehensive, and the most informative of the five California demonstration sites, and the successful completion of the Site Gamma deployment — the installation of the pod, the connection to the inter-pod tunnel network, the commissioning of the life-support, the power, and the communication systems, and the sustained, multi-year, fully autonomous operation of the pod through the normal, the storm, and, potentially, the seismic environmental loading conditions — will constitute a major milestone in the Atlantis program, demonstrating that the Atlantis architecture is capable of surviving and of operating, with the required safety margins and with the required reliability, in the most challenging, the most complex, and the most hazard-rich marine environments on the California continental margin, and providing the engineering confidence and the operational experience that are the necessary prerequisites for the expansion of the Atlantis underwater habitation program to the global scale, encompassing the subduction-zone forearcs, the active volcanic arcs, the methane-hydrate-bearing slopes, and the permafrost-underlain shelves that will be the sites of the future, larger, deeper, higher-capacity Atlantis communities, and that will, collectively, constitute the distributed, redundant, survivable, knowledge-preserving, civilization-preserving underwater infrastructure that is the ultimate objective of the Aegis C program and the essential prerequisite for the survival of the human species through the next Earth Crustal Displacement event, whenever it arrives.
