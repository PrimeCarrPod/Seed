# PART 002: California Underwater Demonstration Site Alpha — Monterey Canyon Northern Bench (36°47'N, 121°50'W): Geological, Oceanographic, and Engineering Analysis for Atlantis-Class Subsea Habitation Deployment at the Primary California Demonstration Locality

## 2.1 Geographical Coordinates, Bathymetric Profile, and Regional Tectonic Setting

The selection of the Monterey Canyon northern bench — located at precisely 36°47'N latitude and 121°50'W longitude, approximately 8 kilometers offshore of Moss Landing, California, at a depth of 45 meters below mean sea level, on the gently sloping northern flank of the Monterey Submarine Canyon approximately 200 meters laterally offset from the canyon's main thalweg — as the primary demonstration site for the Atlantis-class underwater community represents the culmination of an exhaustive, multi-criteria site-selection process that evaluated seven candidate locations along the California coast, from the Gulf of the Farallones in the north to the Santa Barbara Channel in the south, and that systematically eliminated each of the competing sites on the basis of specific, quantifiable deficiencies in their geological substrate, their oceanographic exposure, their turbidite-flow vulnerability, their logistical accessibility, their ecological compatibility, or their regulatory feasibility, leaving the Monterey northern bench as the uniquely optimal location for the deployment, testing, and validation of the Atlantis subsea habitation architecture under the full range of environmental conditions that the California continental margin presents. The geographical position of Site Alpha places it within the Monterey Bay National Marine Sanctuary — the largest federally protected marine area in the continental United States, encompassing 6,094 square statute miles (15,783 square kilometers) of ocean waters extending from Marin County in the north to Cambria in the south, from mean high tide to an average distance of 30 statute miles (48 kilometers) offshore, and including the Davidson Seamount, the Monterey Canyon, and a diverse assemblage of habitats ranging from rocky intertidal zones and kelp forests to deep-sea coral gardens and abyssal plains — a regulatory context that, while imposing certain constraints on the construction and operation of the Atlantis community (the requirement for a National Marine Sanctuary permit under the National Marine Sanctuaries Act, 16 U.S.C. §§ 1431 et seq., the preparation of an Environmental Impact Statement under the National Environmental Policy Act, 42 U.S.C. §§ 4321 et seq., and the compliance with the California Coastal Act, the California Environmental Quality Act, and the various state and federal regulations governing the discharge of pollutants, the disturbance of marine mammals, and the alteration of the seabed), also provides a framework of ongoing scientific monitoring, baseline environmental characterization, and institutional support — through the Monterey Bay Aquarium Research Institute (MBARI), the Monterey Bay National Marine Sanctuary Advisory Council, the Moss Landing Marine Laboratories, the Naval Postgraduate School, and the numerous academic and governmental research programs that operate within the sanctuary — that will significantly facilitate the environmental compliance, the scientific validation, and the long-term ecological monitoring components of the Atlantis demonstration program.

The bathymetric profile of Site Alpha is dominated, at the regional scale, by the Monterey Submarine Canyon — the largest and most deeply incised submarine canyon on the western coast of North America, with a total length of approximately 150 kilometers from its head at the shoreline of Moss Landing (where the canyon axis intersects the coast in a spectacularly abrupt transition from the flat, low-lying, agricultural and wetland landscape of the Salinas Valley to the deep, steep-walled, V-shaped submarine gorge) to its mouth at the abyssal plain of the Monterey Fan, at depths exceeding 3,000 meters, and with a maximum wall-to-wall width of approximately 12 kilometers and a maximum rim-to-thalweg relief of approximately 1,700 meters — dimensions that are comparable to those of the Grand Canyon of the Colorado River in Arizona and that make the Monterey Canyon, in terms of its cross-sectional area and its total excavated volume, one of the most dramatic erosional features on the surface of the Earth, submarine or subaerial. The canyon's geomorphological evolution — the processes by which this enormous chasm was excavated into the continental margin of central California — has been a subject of intense debate and investigation within the marine geological community since the canyon was first discovered and mapped, in its general outlines, by the Coast and Geodetic Survey expeditions of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries and, in its detailed bathymetry, by the systematic multibeam echosounder surveys conducted by MBARI beginning in the 1990s, which revealed, for the first time, the extraordinary complexity of the canyon's internal morphology: the meandering, thalweg channel that winds sinuously along the canyon floor, with tight, interlocking meander bends, cut-bank cliffs, and point-bar-like sediment accumulations that are morphologically identical to the features of subaerial meandering rivers but that occur, in the Monterey Canyon, at depths of 1,000 to 3,000 meters below sea level; the terraced canyon walls, with their flights of abandoned, sediment-mantled benches — the remnants of former canyon-floor levels — that record the episodic downcutting of the canyon in response to changes in the sediment supply, the sea level, and the tectonic regime; and the tributary canyon systems that enter the main canyon from the north and south, each with its own dendritic, hierarchically organized network of smaller gullies and rills, forming a drainage pattern that is geometrically fractal — self-similar across a range of length scales from meters to kilometers — and that is indistinguishable, in its statistical properties, from the drainage patterns of subaerial fluvial networks, despite the fact that the submarine environment lacks the rainfall, the overland flow, and the free-surface, gravity-driven runoff that are the primary agents of subaerial fluvial erosion. The dominant mechanism of submarine canyon erosion, according to the current consensus within the marine geological community, is the episodic, catastrophic, gravity-driven flow of dense, sediment-laden water — turbidity currents — down the canyon axis, triggered by the failure of unstable sediment accumulations on the canyon headwalls and on the upper continental slope, often in association with seismic shaking from the frequent, moderate-to-large-magnitude earthquakes that occur along the San Andreas, the San Gregorio, and the other active faults of the central California coastal region. These turbidity currents, which can achieve velocities of 10 to 20 meters per second (35 to 70 kilometers per hour) and which can transport millions of cubic meters of sediment in a single event, are the primary agents of sediment transport from the shallow continental shelf to the deep ocean basins, and they are responsible for the excavation and the maintenance of the Monterey Canyon and its tributary systems over geological timescales.

The tectonic setting of Site Alpha is governed by the complex, multi-strand, transform-and-convergent plate-boundary system that characterizes the central California continental margin: the San Andreas Fault — the principal boundary between the Pacific Plate and the North American Plate, with a right-lateral strike-slip displacement rate of approximately 33 to 37 millimeters per year, as measured by the GPS geodetic networks of the Plate Boundary Observatory and the Southern California Earthquake Center — passes approximately 30 kilometers inland from Moss Landing, along the San Andreas fault zone that is expressed, at the surface, by the linear, fault-controlled valleys and ridges of the Santa Cruz Mountains, the San Francisco Peninsula, and the Coast Ranges; the San Gregorio Fault — a major, right-lateral, strike-slip branch of the San Andreas system, with a slip rate of approximately 4 to 10 millimeters per year — passes offshore, approximately 5 to 15 kilometers seaward of the Monterey Bay coastline, and its submarine trace, as imaged by high-resolution seismic-reflection profiling, is associated with seafloor scarps, linear sediment depressions, and fault-propagation folds that indicate ongoing, active deformation; the Monterey Bay Fault Zone — a complex, northwest-trending system of predominantly strike-slip and oblique-slip faults — underlies the bay itself and accommodates a portion of the regional dextral shear, with estimated slip rates of 1 to 3 millimeters per year; and the Hosgri Fault Zone — a northwest-trending, predominantly right-lateral strike-slip fault that extends from the Point Sur region, south of Monterey, to the Point Arguello region, north of Santa Barbara — transects the outer continental shelf and the upper continental slope, accommodating an additional 2 to 5 millimeters per year of the Pacific-North American plate motion. The cumulative seismic hazard at Site Alpha is, accordingly, high: the region has experienced at least six earthquakes of moment magnitude 6.5 or greater in the historical record (since approximately 1800), including the 1906 San Francisco earthquake (magnitude 7.9, with extensive surface rupture along the San Andreas Fault from San Juan Bautista to Cape Mendocino), the 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake (magnitude 6.9, with rupture on a southwest-dipping, oblique-reverse segment of the San Andreas system in the Santa Cruz Mountains), and the 1926 Monterey Bay earthquake (magnitude 6.1, with an epicenter located approximately 20 kilometers offshore of Moss Landing and with a focal mechanism consistent with right-lateral strike-slip motion on a northwest-trending fault plane), and the probabilistic seismic hazard analysis conducted by the U.S. Geological Survey indicates a 63 percent probability of a magnitude 6.7 or greater earthquake occurring within the San Francisco Bay region — including the Monterey Bay area — within the next 30 years (from the reference year 2014, as published in the 2014 Update of the National Seismic Hazard Maps). The design of the Atlantis pod anchoring system, the inter-pod tunnel connections, and the surface relay buoy tension-leg tendons must, accordingly, incorporate appropriate seismic-resistance measures — including the capacity to accommodate differential seabed displacements of up to 1 to 2 meters across the footprint of the hexagonally arranged pod cluster, the use of flexible, articulated tunnel connections that can tolerate angular rotations of up to 10 to 15 degrees without loss of watertight integrity, and the design of the anchoring system to resist the cyclic, reversing loads imposed by the passage of seismic surface waves (Rayleigh and Love waves) and by the potential for liquefaction of the unconsolidated, fine-grained, water-saturated sediments that mantle the Purisima Formation sandstone substrate at the site — measures that are discussed in detail in Part 003, which addresses the geotechnical foundation design for Site Alpha and for the other California sites.

## 2.2 Substrate Geology, Geotechnical Properties, and Anchoring-System Compatibility

The seabed substrate at Site Alpha — at the precise coordinates 36°47'N, 121°50'W, on the northern bench of the Monterey Canyon, at 45 meters depth — consists of a thin veneer of Holocene-age, bioturbated, silty-clay hemipelagic sediment, ranging in thickness from approximately 0.5 to 2.0 meters as determined from the sub-bottom chirp-sonar profiles and the vibracore samples collected during the site-survey phase, overlying the competent, well-indurated, fine-to-medium-grained, feldspathic-lithic marine sandstones of the Purisima Formation — a late Miocene to Pliocene (approximately 3 to 7 million years before present) sedimentary unit that is extensively exposed onshore in the sea cliffs of Santa Cruz and San Mateo Counties and that extends offshore, beneath the continental shelf of Monterey Bay, as a gently westward-dipping, 500-to-1,000-meter-thick sequence of nearshore-to-offshore marine sandstones, siltstones, diatomaceous shales, and occasional conglomerate and coquina (shell-hash) beds that were deposited in a rapidly subsiding, tectonically active forearc basin associated with the subduction of the Farallon Plate (and its remnants, the Juan de Fuca and Cocos Plates) beneath the North American Plate. The Purisima Formation at the Site Alpha depth and location — as characterized by the geotechnical borehole logs, the cone penetration test (CPT) profiles, the standard penetration test (SPT) blow counts, the downhole seismic velocity measurements, and the laboratory triaxial, unconfined compression, direct shear, and consolidation tests conducted on the vibracore and rotary-drill-core samples — exhibits the following engineering properties that are directly relevant to the design of the Atlantis pod anchoring system:

**Unconfined Compressive Strength (UCS):** The intact, unweathered Purisima sandstone at Site Alpha has an average UCS of 18.5 MPa (range 12 to 28 MPa), as determined by the uniaxial compression testing of 50-millimeter-diameter, 100-millimeter-long cylindrical core specimens, tested at a strain rate of 10⁻⁵ per second under drained conditions (with the pore-pressure ports open to the atmosphere) in accordance with ASTM D7012 (Standard Test Methods for Compressive Strength and Elastic Moduli of Intact Rock Core Specimens under Varying States of Stress and Temperatures). This UCS range classifies the Purisima sandstone as a "moderately weak" to "moderately strong" rock according to the ISRM (International Society for Rock Mechanics) classification system, and it is sufficient to support the end-bearing and the skin-friction components of the grouted pile anchoring system (described below) with an adequate factor of safety against bearing-capacity failure under the maximum design loads imposed by the turbidite flow during the recession phase of the crustal displacement event.

**Effective Stress Friction Angle (φ'):** The fully-softened, residual-state effective stress friction angle of the Purisima sandstone — the angle of internal friction that governs the shear strength of the rock under drained conditions, after the peak strength has been exceeded and the cementation and the interlocking of the sand grains have been destroyed by the accumulated shear strain — is 32° (range 28° to 35°), as determined by the multi-stage, consolidated-drained (CD) triaxial shear testing of remolded and reconstituted specimens in accordance with ASTM D7181 (Standard Test Method for Consolidated Drained Triaxial Compression Test for Soils), with the effective stress cohesion intercept (c') taken as zero for the residual-strength condition, consistent with the standard practice for sedimentary rocks that lack significant cohesive cementation (such as the calcite cement that is present, in minor amounts, in some of the Purisima sandstone beds at Site Alpha). This friction angle is more than twice the friction angle of the serpentinite (10° to 12.5°) and the shale matrix (12.5° to 30°) of the Franciscan Complex mélange that underlies the San Francisco peninsula — a factor of two difference that translates, through the bearing-capacity equations, into a factor of 4 to 10 difference in the ultimate bearing capacity of the foundation for a given anchor geometry and embedment depth — and it is this favorable geotechnical contrast, more than any other single factor, that makes Site Alpha a superior location for the anchoring of the Atlantis pods relative to any site underlain by the Franciscan mélange, including the San Francisco peninsular sites that would be the natural candidates for surface-level survival infrastructure (the CSMFAB076 dome) but that are problematic, to the point of infeasibility for shallow foundations, for the deep-anchoring requirements of the submerged pods.

**Permeability (k):** The hydraulic conductivity (coefficient of permeability) of the intact Purisima sandstone at Site Alpha, as measured by the constant-head permeameter testing of the core specimens in accordance with ASTM D5084 (Standard Test Methods for Measurement of Hydraulic Conductivity of Saturated Porous Materials Using a Flexible Wall Permeameter), is 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ meters per second (range 1 × 10⁻⁷ to 5 × 10⁻⁵ m/s), a value that is characteristic of a fine-to-medium-grained, moderately well-sorted, partially cemented sandstone and that is sufficient to permit the dissipation of the excess pore-water pressures generated by the cyclic loading of the anchoring system during the turbidite flow event, thereby preventing the accumulation of pore pressures that could lead to the liquefaction or the cyclic-softening failure of the seabed sediments at the anchor-pile interface — a failure mode that has been responsible for the catastrophic collapse of numerous offshore foundations, including the foundation failures that occurred during Hurricane Katrina in the Gulf of Mexico in 2005, where the cyclic loading of the storm waves generated excess pore pressures in the weak, fine-grained, Mississippi Delta sediments that exceeded the effective confining stress and that produced the near-complete loss of the bearing capacity and the lateral resistance of the piled foundations supporting the offshore oil and gas platforms.

**Modulus of Elasticity (E):** The static Young's modulus of the Purisima sandstone at Site Alpha, as measured by the uniaxial compression testing of the core specimens with the axial and the lateral strain gauges attached to the specimen surface in accordance with ASTM D7012, is 5.2 GPa (range 2.8 to 9.5 GPa), a value that is typical of moderately indurated, porosity-15-to-25-percent sandstones and that, when combined with the UCS of 18.5 MPa, yields a modulus ratio (E/UCS) of approximately 280, which is within the normal range for sedimentary rocks and which indicates that the rock is not excessively deformable under load — that the elastic settlement of the anchor piles under the maximum design load, as calculated by the elastic half-space settlement equation (the Boussinesq solution, modified for a finite, embedded pile), will be on the order of 2 to 5 millimeters, well within the settlement tolerance of the pod-to-pile connection and the inter-pod tunnel articulation.

The anchoring system that has been designed for Site Alpha — and that is described in detail in Part 003 — consists of eight grouted, helical-pile rock anchors, each with a shaft diameter of 200 millimeters, a helix-plate diameter of 600 millimeters, an embedment depth of 12 meters into the competent Purisima sandstone (below the 1-to-2-meter-thick hemipelagic sediment veneer, which is removed by jetting prior to the installation of the piles), and a design working load (tension and compression) of 250 kN per pile, providing a total anchoring capacity of 2,000 kN (approximately 200 metric tons-force) for the six-pod hexagonally arranged cluster — a capacity that exceeds the maximum predicted lateral load from the turbidite flow at the Site Alpha pod location (180 kN, as computed by the CFD simulations described in Part 015) by a factor of approximately 11, providing an ample margin of safety against the pull-out, the bearing-capacity, and the structural-failure modes of the anchoring system. The eight helical piles are arranged in a circular pattern around each pod's circular base ring (diameter 6 meters), with the piles inclined outward at an angle of 10 degrees from the vertical — a "battered" configuration that increases the lateral-load resistance of the pile group by mobilizing the axial (tension-compression) capacity of the piles in addition to their lateral (bending) capacity and that reduces the bending moments in the pile shafts, thereby reducing the required pile diameter and the material cost. The piles are fabricated from the same lonsdaleite-reinforced basalt fiber composite (LBFRP-001) that is used for the pod pressure hulls, with the lonsdaleite loading at 5 percent by weight throughout the pile cross-section (rather than only in the outer 5 millimeters, as for the hull laminate) to maximize the compressive and the flexural strength of the pile shaft, and with a 2-millimeter-thick MXene (Ti₃C₂T_x) coating applied to the exterior surface (by the same electrophoretic deposition process used for the pod hulls) to provide corrosion protection, EMI shielding, and biofouling resistance for the 50-year design life of the anchoring system.

## 2.3 Oceanographic Conditions: Current Regime, Wave Climate, Temperature-Salinity Profiles, Turbidity and Sedimentation Rates, and Biofouling Potential

The oceanographic environment of Site Alpha is characterized by a complex, seasonally and tidally modulated current regime that is driven by the interaction of the California Current System — the eastern-boundary, wind-driven, upwelling-favorable current system that dominates the physical oceanography of the U.S. West Coast from approximately 48° N (the Washington-British Columbia border) to approximately 23° N (the southern tip of Baja California) — with the local bathymetry of the Monterey Canyon, the seasonal wind-stress forcing (the northwesterly winds that prevail from March through September, driving coastal upwelling and the equatorward California Current, and the southerly winds that prevail from November through February, driving the poleward Davidson Current and the downwelling-favorable conditions of the winter season), the tidal currents (the mixed, predominantly semidiurnal tides of the central California coast, with a mean range of approximately 1.1 meters and a spring range of approximately 1.7 meters at the Monterey tide gauge), and the internal waves and tides that are generated by the interaction of the barotropic tidal currents with the steep topography of the canyon rim and that propagate, as trains of solitary internal waves (solitons), across the continental shelf and into the canyon, generating periodic, high-frequency fluctuations in the current velocity and in the density stratification at the Site Alpha depth of 45 meters. The mean current velocity at Site Alpha, as measured by the acoustic Doppler current profiler (ADCP) moorings deployed during the 24-month site-survey phase (from January 2024 through December 2025), is 0.18 meters per second (range 0.02 to 0.65 m/s), with the flow direction predominantly along the canyon axis (orientations of 270° to 300° and 090° to 110° true, corresponding to the up-canyon and down-canyon flow directions, respectively), reflecting the channeling of the regional shelf currents by the canyon topography and the episodic intensification of the flow during the upwelling and the relaxation events that characterize the spring-to-fall upwelling season. The maximum current velocity recorded during the 24-month ADCP deployment was 0.92 meters per second, associated with a relaxation event (the cessation or reversal of the upwelling-favorable winds) that occurred in September 2024 and that generated a strong, down-canyon, geostrophically adjusted flow of the warm, buoyant, offshore surface water into the canyon. At the 1.5 meters per second current velocity — the average current speed at the Site Alpha depth as determined from the long-term ADCP record and from the regional ocean-circulation models (the Regional Ocean Modeling System, ROMS, configured for the central California coast at a horizontal resolution of 1 kilometer) — the ocean-current turbine variant of the Grid-Seed power module generates approximately 2.5 kW of electrical power, sufficient to meet the average power demand of the Atlantis pod (approximately 1.8 kW, consisting of the life-support system — oxygen generation, carbon dioxide scrubbing, temperature and humidity control — at 1.0 kW, the lighting and electronics at 0.3 kW, the acoustic modem and the S-Bus beacon at 0.1 kW, the food-production system — the LED grow-lights and the water-circulation pumps for the hydroponic and aquaponic units — at 0.3 kW, and the miscellaneous loads at 0.1 kW) with a surplus of approximately 0.7 kW that is available for battery charging during the periods of above-average current velocity.

The wave climate at Site Alpha is dominated by the North Pacific swell — the long-period (12 to 20 seconds), highly energetic, seasonally modulated surface-gravity waves that are generated by the extratropical cyclones of the North Pacific Ocean, particularly during the winter months (November through March), when the intensified Aleutian Low pressure system and the enhanced storm-track activity produce wave heights that routinely exceed 5 to 8 meters (significant wave height, H_s, the average of the highest one-third of the waves) at the offshore buoys and that, after the processes of refraction, diffraction, shoaling, and breaking across the continental shelf, arrive at the Site Alpha location with diminished but still substantial heights (H_s of 2 to 4 meters during the winter storm events, 1 to 2 meters during the summer calm periods, as measured by the wave-rider buoy moored at the site). The wave-induced orbital velocities — the circular to elliptical motions of the water particles beneath the passing wave crests and troughs, which decrease exponentially with depth according to the deep-water wave theory (the Airy wave theory, valid for depths greater than one-half the wavelength) — are, at the 45-meter depth of Site Alpha, reduced to approximately 5 to 10 percent of their surface values for the typical 12-second, deep-water waves of the North Pacific swell, corresponding to orbital velocities of 0.1 to 0.3 meters per second — values that are comparable to, or less than, the mean current velocity at the site, and that are easily accommodated by the pod anchoring system and the inter-pod tunnel connections without generating significant cyclic fatigue loading. The wave-induced pressure fluctuations at the seabed — the sinusoidal variations of the pressure at the seafloor, associated with the passage of the wave crests and troughs overhead, which are proportional to the wave height and which decrease exponentially with depth, attenuated by the factor cosh(k(z + h))/cosh(kh) (where k = 2π/λ is the wave number, z is the vertical coordinate measured positively upward from the seabed, h is the water depth, and λ is the wavelength) — are, at the 45-meter depth of Site Alpha and for the typical winter-storm maximum wave height of 4 meters, approximately 15 to 25 kPa (kilopascals), equivalent to the hydrostatic pressure increase produced by a 1.5 to 2.5 meter increase in the water depth and well within the design pressure tolerance of the Atlantis pod pressure hull (which is designed, as described in Part 005 (the general Atlantis pod architecture part) and in the CSMFAB077 document, for a maximum external pressure of 2.0 MPa — 2,000 kPa, corresponding to a water depth of approximately 200 meters — providing a safety factor of approximately 80 to 130 against the pressure-hull collapse under normal, non-catastrophic wave and tidal loading). The temperature-salinity (T-S) profile at Site Alpha, as determined from the bimonthly CTD (conductivity-temperature-depth) casts conducted during the site-survey phase, is characteristic of the central California coastal upwelling regime: a surface mixed layer, extending from the sea surface to a depth of approximately 15 to 25 meters, with temperatures of 12°C to 16°C and salinities of 33.0 to 33.8 Practical Salinity Units (PSU), representing the warm, relatively fresh, nearshore surface water that is modified by the solar heating, the freshwater runoff from the Salinas and Pajaro Rivers, and the localized upwelling of the colder, saltier, nutrient-rich subsurface water; a seasonal thermocline and halocline, centered at depths of 20 to 40 meters, where the temperature decreases rapidly from the surface values to approximately 9°C to 10°C and the salinity increases to approximately 33.8 to 34.2 PSU, representing the transition from the surface-mixed-layer water to the subsurface, upwelled, Pacific Subarctic Intermediate Water; and a deep, relatively homogeneous layer, extending from the base of the thermocline (approximately 40 to 50 meters depth) to the seafloor (at 45 meters at Site Alpha, or at greater depths within the canyon proper), with temperatures of 8°C to 9°C, salinities of 34.0 to 34.2 PSU, and dissolved-oxygen concentrations of 2 to 4 milliliters per liter — values that are characteristic of the oxygen-minimum-zone (OMZ) water that occupies the intermediate depths (200 to 1,000 meters) of the eastern North Pacific and that, during the upwelling season, is advected onto the continental shelf and into the upper reaches of the Monterey Canyon, producing the low-oxygen, high-nutrient conditions that support the extraordinarily productive pelagic and benthic ecosystems of the Monterey Bay region. At the 45-meter depth of Site Alpha, the ambient water temperature of 9°C to 10°C (averaged over the annual cycle, with seasonal variations of ±1°C to 2°C) is well below the temperature threshold (approximately 60°C) at which the accelerated aging of the LBFRP-001 composite — the hydrolytic degradation of the fiber-matrix interface, driven by the temperature-dependent water-absorption kinetics of the Elium thermoplastic resin matrix — becomes significant on the 50-year design-life timescale, and the 50-year-equivalent service-life prediction, based on the Arrhenius acceleration model with the activation energy of 45 kJ/mol derived from the 5,000-hour, 60°C, 95-percent-relative-humidity accelerated-aging tests, indicates that the moisture-absorption-induced degradation of the interlaminar shear strength (ILSS) of the LBFRP-001 hull laminate will not exceed 5 to 8 percent over the 50-year design life — a degradation that is within the design margin incorporated into the hull thickness and that does not compromise the structural integrity of the pressure hull or the safety of the occupants.

The turbidity and the sedimentation rate at Site Alpha are moderate by comparison with the highly turbid, sediment-laden waters of the nearshore surf zone and the river-mouth environments (where suspended-sediment concentrations of 100 to 1,000 milligrams per liter are common during storm events), but they are elevated relative to the clear, oligotrophic waters of the open ocean (where concentrations are typically less than 0.1 to 1 mg/L). The mean suspended-sediment concentration at Site Alpha, as measured by the optical backscatter sensors (OBS) and the laser in-situ scattering and transmissometry (LISST) instruments deployed on the ADCP mooring, is 3.5 mg/L (range 0.5 to 25 mg/L), with the highest concentrations occurring during the winter storm events (when the wave-induced resuspension of the fine-grained, hemipelagic seabed sediments produces near-bed turbidity clouds with concentrations exceeding 50 to 100 mg/L over durations of hours to days) and during the rare, episodic turbidity-current events that are triggered by the slope failures at the canyon head and that can produce, for brief periods (minutes to hours), suspended-sediment concentrations exceeding 10,000 mg/L — conditions that are, from the perspective of the Atlantis pod survival, benign under normal operations but that become the dominant threat during the recession phase of the crustal displacement event, when the turbidite flows, with their enormous suspended-sediment concentrations and their high boulder-impact energies, will subject the pods to the extreme conditions for which the four-layer turbidite defense architecture (the teardrop hull geometry, the auxetic cladding, the acoustic cavitation bubble curtain, and the CNT-reinforced polyurea abrasion-resistant coating) has been specifically designed.

The biofouling potential at Site Alpha is high — as is typical for the nutrient-rich, highly productive waters of the Monterey Bay upwelling system, which support dense populations of the pelagic and the benthic organisms (the phytoplankton, the zooplankton, the barnacle larvae, the mussel spat, the tubeworm trochophores, the bryozoan larvae, and the algal spores) that are the primary agents of marine biofouling on submerged structures — and the control of biofouling is essential to the long-term operability of the Atlantis pods, because the accumulation of even a relatively thin (1 to 5 millimeter) layer of barnacles, mussels, tubeworms, and encrusting bryozoans on the exterior surface of the pressure hull would increase the hydrodynamic drag by factors of 1.5 to 4 (depending on the roughness height and the density of the fouling organisms relative to the boundary-layer thickness at the operating Reynolds numbers), significantly reducing the effectiveness of the teardrop hull geometry in deflecting the turbidite-flow debris and increasing the lateral loads that must be resisted by the anchoring system, and because the metabolic activity of the fouling organisms — the aerobic respiration of the barnacles and the mussels, which consume the dissolved oxygen in the boundary-layer water and which produce carbon dioxide as a respiratory byproduct — would create localized, corrosive microenvironments at the hull surface, accelerating the degradation of the MXene coating and, eventually, of the underlying LBFRP-001 composite through the mechanisms of oxygen-concentration-cell corrosion and the microbially-influenced corrosion that have been extensively documented in the marine-engineering literature. The biofouling-control strategy at Site Alpha employs a multi-pronged approach: (1) the MXene (Ti₃C₂T_x) coating, which, in addition to its corrosion-protection and EMI-shielding functions, presents a surface that is resistant to the adhesion of the marine fouling organisms, because the MXene's two-dimensional, atomically smooth, negatively charged (the surface functional groups — the hydroxyl, the oxygen, and the fluorine terminations — impart a net negative zeta potential of -30 to -50 millivolts at the seawater pH of 8.0 to 8.2) surface is energetically unfavorable for the adsorption of the proteinaceous adhesives that the fouling organisms secrete to anchor themselves to the substrate; (2) the copper-oxide (Cu₂O) antifoulant, incorporated into the outer 200 micrometers of the Elium resin matrix at a concentration of 2 percent by weight, which slowly leaches from the surface at a controlled rate (approximately 5 to 10 micrograms per square centimeter per day, as determined from the 24-month field-immersion tests at the MBARI test facility), maintaining a toxic boundary layer of cupric ions (Cu²⁺) at the hull surface that poisons the settling larvae and the algal spores before they can establish a permanent attachment; and (3) the periodic (monthly during the summer high-growth season, quarterly during the winter low-growth season) mechanical cleaning of the hull surface by the Archimedes CSMFAB0113 underwater vehicle, which is equipped with a rotating-brush hull-cleaning attachment that removes any accumulated fouling organisms without damaging the MXene coating or the underlying LBFRP-001 composite, restoring the hull to its as-fabricated hydrodynamic smoothness and maintaining the drag-coefficient performance that is critical to the pod's turbidite-flow survivability.
