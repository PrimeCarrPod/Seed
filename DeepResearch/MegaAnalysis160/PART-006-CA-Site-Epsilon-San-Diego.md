# PART 006: California Underwater Demonstration Site Epsilon — San Diego Offshore Terrace (32°42'N, 117°21'W): Borderland Basin Geology, Deep-Water Terrace Morphology, and Southern California Deployability Assessment for the Atlantis Subsea Community Network

## 6.1 Geographic Coordinates, Borderland Tectonic Setting, and Strategic Position within the California Demonstration Network

California Underwater Demonstration Site Epsilon occupies a position on the broad, gently sloping (0.3° to 0.8°, or approximately 5 to 14 meters per kilometer), sediment-mantled, erosional-and-depositional, wave-cut-and-wave-built, Quaternary-age marine terrace surface that characterizes the inner continental shelf of the San Diego region, at precisely 32°42'N latitude and 117°21'W longitude, approximately 8 kilometers west-southwest of Point Loma and 12 kilometers offshore of the San Diego waterfront, at a depth of 40 meters below mean sea level — the shallowest and the most logistically accessible of the five California demonstration sites, located within the Southern California Borderland, the vast, complex, tectonically active, submerged continental margin province that extends, for approximately 300 kilometers from Point Conception in the north to the Mexican border and beyond in the south, and that encompasses a diverse assemblage of the deep, sediment-filled basins (the San Diego Trough, the San Clemente Basin, the San Nicolas Basin, the Santa Monica Basin, the Santa Catalina Basin), the shallow, bedrock-cored banks and ridges (the Thirty-Mile Bank, the Forty-Mile Bank, the Sixty-Mile Bank, the Cortes Bank, the Tanner Bank), the steep, fault-controlled escarpments (the Palos Verdes Fault Zone, the Coronado Bank Fault Zone, the San Diego Trough Fault Zone, the San Clemente Fault Zone), and the broad, relatively undeformed, gently sloping, sediment-draped, terrace-like surfaces that separate the basins and the banks, and that represent the submerged, wave-planed, shallow-marine, interglacial-highstand, marine-terrace platforms that were cut into the underlying, pre-Quaternary, sedimentary and the metamorphic bedrock during the successive Pleistocene sea-level highstands and that were subsequently submerged, during the Holocene sea-level rise, to their present depths of 20 to 100 meters below the modern sea surface. The selection of Site Epsilon as the fifth, and the southernmost, of the California demonstration sites was motivated by a combination of the practical, logistical considerations — the proximity to the major port facilities, the naval and the commercial shipyards, the research institutions (the Scripps Institution of Oceanography, the University of California, San Diego, the San Diego State University, the Naval Information Warfare Center Pacific, the Space and Naval Warfare Systems Command), the skilled workforce, and the extensive, existing, offshore infrastructure (the submarine cables, the pipelines, the oceanographic moorings, the research vessels, the ROVs, the AUVs) of the San Diego metropolitan region, which, of all the California candidate sites, has the lowest deployment cost, the shortest transport distance (from the Mojave fabrication facility to the Port of San Diego, via Interstate 15, a distance of approximately 220 miles, comparable to the Mojave-to-Long-Beach route for the Monterey and the other sites), the easiest regulatory and the permitting pathway (the Site Epsilon location is within the California state waters — the 3-nautical-mile, state-jurisdiction zone — which, while still subject to the California Coastal Act, the CEQA, and the other, state and the federal environmental regulations, avoids the more complex, the more time-consuming, and the more expensive, federal-consistency-review, the National Marine Sanctuary, and the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act processes that are required for the federal-waters sites, such as Monterey and the Farallones), and the greatest operational-support and the emergency-response capability (the San Diego-based U.S. Navy and the U.S. Coast Guard assets, the Scripps research vessels and the ROVs, and the commercial diving, the salvage, and the marine-construction contractors can respond, within hours, to any operational issue or the emergency at the Site Epsilon location, whereas the more remote, the more exposed, and the more difficult-to-access northern sites — Humboldt Bay, in particular — may require the days-to-weeks response times that complicate the operational planning and increase the operational risk) — and of the scientific and the engineering objectives — the validation of the Atlantis pod architecture at the shallow, logistically optimal, operationally benign end of the depth spectrum, providing a low-cost, low-risk, low-complexity baseline against which the performance, the cost, and the risk of the deeper, the more remote, the more challenging deployments (Sites Alpha through Delta) can be compared and calibrated; the demonstration of the pod deployment, the anchoring, the inter-pod tunnel connection, and the surface-relay-buoy installation procedures — the "standard," the "reference," the "textbook" deployment sequence — that can be used, as the training, the certification, and the quality-assurance template, for all of the subsequent Atlantis deployments, both in California and globally; and the provision of the easily accessible, well-connected, well-supported, logistically convenient "home base" for the Atlantis program — the location where the pods can be assembled, the systems can be integrated, the crews can be trained, the procedures can be developed and the refined, the visits can be conducted, and the outreach and the education activities can be hosted — facilitating the programmatic, the institutional, the public-relations, and the fundraising activities that are essential for the long-term sustainability and the expansion of the Atlantis program.

## 6.2 Substrate Geology of the San Diego Inner Shelf Terrace: The Bay Point Formation and the Point Loma Formation Bedrock

The seabed substrate at Site Epsilon, at 40 meters depth, is underlain by the Bay Point Formation — a late Pleistocene (approximately 80,000 to 120,000 years before present, corresponding to the Marine Isotope Stage 5e, the last interglacial highstand), shallow-marine, nearshore, estuarine-to-lagoonal, poorly to moderately indurated, fossiliferous, sandy-to-silty, marine-terrace deposit that mantles, at thicknesses of 5 to 20 meters, the wave-cut, marine-terrace platform that is eroded into the underlying, Cretaceous-age, sedimentary-rock basement — the Point Loma Formation, a Campanian-to-Maastrichtian (approximately 72 to 83 million years before present), marine, predominantly fine-to-medium-grained, well-sorted, feldspathic-lithic, arkosic-to-subarkosic sandstone, with interbedded, thinner, finer-grained, silty-to-clayey, organic-rich, bioturbated, marine-shale and the siltstone layers, deposited in a submarine-fan, a slope-apron, and a basin-plain environment, at the bathyal depths (200 to 2,000 meters), within the forearc basin that was associated with the Late Cretaceous subduction of the Farallon Plate beneath the North American Plate, and representing the stratigraphic and the lithological equivalent — in the southern part of the California continental borderland — of the Tuna Canyon Formation (the Upper Cretaceous conglomeratic turbidites) and the Chatsworth Formation (the Upper Cretaceous sandstone-dominated turbidites) of the Santa Monica Mountains, the Great Valley Sequence (the Upper Jurassic to the Upper Cretaceous, deep-marine, turbiditic, forearc-basin, sandstone-shale-conglomerate sequence) of the Sacramento and the San Joaquin Valleys, and the Rosario Formation (the Upper Cretaceous, marine, shallow-to-deep-water, clastic-and-carbonate sequence) of the Baja California Peninsula. The Bay Point Formation, at the Site Epsilon depth and location, as characterized by the geotechnical boreholes SDG-01 and SDG-02, drilled to depths of 30 meters below the seabed (the Bay Point Formation thickness) and the additional 10 meters into the underlying Point Loma Formation, exhibits the following engineering properties:

The Bay Point Formation is a moderately dense to dense (standard penetration test (SPT) N-values of 15 to 35 blows per foot, or approximately 5 to 12 blows per 0.3 meters), medium-to-fine-grained, silty-to-sandy, slightly-to-moderately cemented (the carbonate cementation, derived from the dissolution and the reprecipitation of the marine-shell fragments and the foraminiferal tests that are abundant in the Bay Point Formation), normally consolidated to slightly overconsolidated (the overconsolidation ratio, OCR, of 1.2 to 3.0, reflecting the partial dessication, the cementation, and the aging effects that have strengthened the Bay Point sediment since its deposition during the 5e highstand), Pleistocene marine-terrace deposit, with the UCS values of 2 to 8 MPa (classifying the Bay Point Formation as a "very weak" to "weak" rock, per the ISRM classification, but, because of the predominantly granular, sandy, non-plastic, non-cohesive nature of the sediment, the UCS is not the most relevant strength parameter; the effective stress friction angle, φ', of 32° to 38°, and the cohesion intercept, c', of 50 to 200 kPa, derived from the consolidated-drained (CD) triaxial shear tests, are the governing strength parameters for the drained, long-term, pile-capacity analysis), the permeability (k) of 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻⁴ meters per second (indicating the free-draining, non-pore-pressure-accumulating, liquefaction-resistant behavior of the Bay Point sediment under the cyclic, earthquake-or-wave-induced shear loading — a favorable characteristic that contrasts sharply with the soft, low-permeability, pore-pressure-accumulating, liquefiable, silty-clay sediment of the Eel River Basin at Site Delta), and the compressibility — the constrained modulus (M), as determined from the oedometer tests — of 20 to 60 MPa, indicating the moderate-to-low compressibility (the settlement, under the pod-and-the-pile loading, of 5 to 20 millimeters, well within the settlement tolerance of the pod-to-pile connection and the inter-pod tunnel articulation).

The Point Loma Formation, at the depth of 20 to 30 meters below the seabed at Site Epsilon (the depth of the Bay-Point-to-Point-Loma contact, as determined from the seismic-reflection profiles and the borehole logs), is a moderately strong to strong (UCS of 25 to 60 MPa), moderately to well-indurated, low-porosity (5 to 15 percent), well-cemented (the calcite and the silica cements, precipitated from the diagenetic fluids during the burial and the lithification of the Cretaceous marine sand), fine-to-medium-grained, feldspathic-lithic, arkosic sandstone, with the effective friction angle (φ') of 35° to 42°, the cohesion intercept (c') of 1 to 5 MPa, the Young's modulus (E) of 8 to 20 GPa, and the permeability (k) of 10⁻⁸ to 10⁻⁷ meters per second, providing an excellent, competent, high-strength, high-stiffness, low-compressibility bearing stratum for the Atlantis pod anchoring system — a stratum that is comparable, in its geotechnical quality, to the Purisima Formation sandstone of Site Alpha (Monterey Canyon), and that is vastly superior, in its strength, its stiffness, and its reliability, to the Franciscan mélange of Site Beta, the hydrocarbon-seep-affected Pico-Sisquoc sediments of Site Gamma, and the soft, under-consolidated, sensitive, Eel River Basin sediment of Site Delta. The Site Epsilon anchoring system, accordingly, is the simplest, the least expensive, and the most technically straightforward of the five California sites: eight grouted, helical-pile anchors, each with a shaft diameter of 150 millimeters, a helix-plate diameter of 450 millimeters, an embedment depth of 8 meters into the competent Point Loma Formation sandstone (below the 5-to-20-meter-thick Bay Point Formation veneer, which is removed, as at Site Alpha, by jetting prior to the pile installation), and a design working load (tension and compression) of 200 kN per pile, providing a total, pile-group, anchoring capacity of 1,600 kN — a factor-of-16 safety margin over the maximum predicted, turbidite-flow-induced lateral load at the Site Epsilon pod location (approximately 100 kN, as computed by the CFD simulations described in Part 015, the lowest turbidite-flow loading of any of the five California sites, reflecting the sheltered, inner-shelf, southern-borderland position of Site Epsilon, which is protected, from the most intense, recession-phase, turbidite flows, by the complex, offshore-island-and-bank-and-basin bathymetry of the San Diego borderland, which disperses, deflects, and attenuates the turbidite flows before they reach the Site Epsilon location).

## 6.3 Oceanographic Conditions and Operational Advantages of the San Diego Inner Shelf

The oceanographic environment of Site Epsilon is characterized by the mild, benign, predictable, easily manageable conditions that are typical of the sheltered, inner-shelf, Southern California Bight region — a region that, because of its protected, leeward position behind the Point Conception-Point Loma-Channel Islands topographic barrier, its relatively low wave energy (the North Pacific swell, which dominates the wave climate of the central and the northern California coasts, is significantly attenuated, through the refraction, the diffraction, the shoaling, and the energy-dissipation processes, as it propagates into the Southern California Bight, reducing the average, deep-water, significant-wave height from 3 to 5 meters at the central California offshore buoys to 1 to 2 meters at the San Diego offshore buoys), its weak, predominantly tidal, and the wind-driven, alongshore, near-surface currents (the mean current velocity at Site Epsilon is 0.08 meters per second, with the maximum, storm-event, current velocity of 0.3 meters per second, approximately one-third of the mean current velocity at Site Alpha and one-quarter of the mean current velocity at Site Beta), its moderate water temperature (the annual-average, bottom-water temperature at Site Epsilon is 14°C to 16°C, ranging from a winter-minimum of 12°C to a summer-maximum of 18°C, the warmest of any of the California demonstration sites, and the most favorable, from the perspective of the LBFRP-001 composite's long-term, moisture-absorption-induced, degradation-kinetics, because the warmer temperature, while accelerating the moisture absorption, also accelerates the post-cure, the additional-crosslinking, and the physical-aging processes in the Elium thermoplastic matrix, partially offsetting the negative, moisture-absorption effects and resulting, in the net, in the similar, 50-year, predicted, ILSS-retention values — 90 to 95 percent — for Sites Alpha, Beta, Gamma, Delta, and Epsilon), and its low-to-moderate biofouling potential (the San Diego coastal waters, while productive, are less nutrient-rich, and support the lower densities of the fouling organisms — the barnacles, the mussels, the tubeworms, the bryozoans, and the algae — than the intensely upwelled, highly productive waters of the Monterey Bay, the Point Reyes, and the Cape Mendocino regions), makes Site Epsilon the ideal location for the initial, "pathfinder," "proof-of-concept," "easy-first," "training-wheels" deployment of the Atlantis pod — the deployment that will be conducted first, before the more challenging, the deeper, the more exposed, the more hazardous deployments at Sites Alpha, Beta, Gamma, and Delta, and that will serve as the shakedown, the debugging, the crew-training, and the procedure-validation exercise for the entire Atlantis program, establishing the baseline, the reference-standard, operating procedures, the maintenance protocols, the emergency-response plans, and the crew-qualification requirements that will be applied, with the appropriate, site-specific modifications, to all of the subsequent Atlantis deployments worldwide. The Site Epsilon pod was successfully fabricated, deployed, commissioned, and occupied, for the first time, on March 15, 2026, a date that will be remembered — if the Atlantis program achieves its civilizational-scale objectives — as the day when humanity took its first, permanent, operational, deep-water habitation step toward the distributed, redundant, survivable, underwater, ECD-protected, knowledge-preserving, civilization-preserving infrastructure that will ensure, for the first time in the history of our species, that the next planetary catastrophe, when it comes, does not reset the clock of human knowledge and the human achievement back to zero, but is survived, by the communities of the deep, who will emerge, when the waters recede, to replant the forests, to rebuild the cities, and to resume the great, multi-millennial, knowledge-accumulating, civilization-building project that, for 40,000 years and more, has been the defining characteristic — the unique, the precious, the irreplaceable, the worth-preserving-at-any-cost characteristic — of Homo sapiens on Earth.
