# PART 003: California Underwater Demonstration Site Beta — Gulf of the Farallones Outer Shelf Edge (37°42'N, 123°02'W): Deep-Water Protocol Validation at Extended Depth, Franciscan Complex Foundation Challenges, and High-Energy Turbidite Exposure Analysis

## 3.1 Site Location, Regional Geological Framework, and Strategic Rationale for Deep-Water Deployment

California Underwater Demonstration Site Beta occupies a position on the outer continental shelf edge, at precisely 37°42'N latitude and 123°02'W longitude, approximately 45 kilometers west-southwest of the Golden Gate and 35 kilometers offshore of the Marin County coastline, at a depth of 85 meters below mean sea level — a depth that places Site Beta at the outermost extremity of the continental shelf, where the gradually sloping, sediment-mantled shelf surface (with an average gradient of 0.2° to 0.5°, or approximately 3 to 9 meters per kilometer) gives way, over a horizontal distance of only 2 to 5 kilometers, to the precipitously steep, canyon-incised continental slope (with gradients exceeding 10° to 20° in many places, and locally approaching 30° to 45° in the headwalls of the major submarine canyons that dissect the slope), descending rapidly to the abyssal depths (3,000 to 4,000 meters) of the Pacific Ocean floor within a mere 50 to 80 kilometers of the coastline. This transitional position — straddling the shelf-break, the fundamental bathymetric boundary that separates the shallow, illuminated, biologically hyper-productive, terrigenous-sediment-dominated continental shelf from the deep, dark, oligotrophic, pelagic-sediment-dominated continental slope and abyssal plain — confers upon Site Beta a unique combination of oceanographic, geological, and ecological characteristics that distinguish it from the shallower, shelf-interior Site Alpha in Monterey Bay, and that make the successful deployment and operation of an Atlantis-class subsea habitation module at this location a particularly demanding and, consequently, a particularly informative test of the Atlantis architecture's adaptability, robustness, and performance margins across the full range of depths and environmental conditions that the California continental margin presents.

The selection of Site Beta as the second California demonstration site was motivated by several strategic considerations that emerged from the design review and the risk analysis conducted during the initial phases of the Atlantis program, as documented in the CSMFAB077 specification and the associated engineering reports. First, the greater depth of Site Beta (85 meters, compared with 45 meters at Site Alpha) subjects the Atlantis pod to a substantially higher ambient hydrostatic pressure — 0.85 MPa (8.5 atmospheres, approximately 125 psi) at Site Beta, compared with 0.45 MPa (4.5 atmospheres, approximately 65 psi) at Site Alpha — and, consequently, to a more stringent test of the LBFRP-001 pressure hull's long-term creep-rupture and fatigue resistance under sustained compressive loading. The pressure-hull design for the standard Atlantis pod (as described in the CSMFAB077 document) is rated for a maximum operating depth of 200 meters (2.0 MPa external pressure), providing a safety factor of approximately 2.35 against the collapse pressure at the 85-meter Site Beta depth — a safety factor that, while comfortable for the short-term, proof-test validation of the hull design, is sufficiently modest that the long-term (50-year) performance of the hull at this depth will generate valuable data on the creep-deformation, the microcracking, and the progressive damage accumulation mechanisms that may eventually limit the service life of the LBFRP-001 composite in deep-water applications, data that will inform the design of the larger, deeper, higher-capacity Atlantis-family pods that are planned for the later phases of the Aegis C underwater habitation program (the "Atlantis-II" and "Atlantis-III" variants, with depth ratings of 500 meters and 1,000 meters, respectively, designed to exploit the greater depths that may be necessary to achieve complete protection from the surface-wave dynamic pressures of the largest crustal displacement events, as the exponential attenuation of the orbital velocity with depth, while dramatic in the upper 30 to 50 meters of the water column, continues — at an ever-decreasing rate — to depths of hundreds to thousands of meters, and the additional attenuation that can be achieved by deploying the pods at depths of 200, 500, or 1,000 meters rather than 45 meters may be necessary, depending on the results of the refined crustal displacement hydrodynamic modeling, to ensure the survival of the pods through displacement events of the maximum predicted intensity).

Second, the outer shelf-edge location of Site Beta exposes the Atlantis pod to the full, unattenuated force of the deep-ocean swell and the storm-generated waves that, at the shallower, more sheltered Site Alpha location within Monterey Bay, are partially dissipated — through the processes of wave refraction, diffraction, shoaling, and bottom-friction energy loss — before reaching the pod depth of 45 meters. At Site Beta, the wave-induced orbital velocities at the seabed (at the 85-meter depth) are approximately 15 to 25 percent of their surface values for the typical 14-second, deep-water North Pacific swell waves — corresponding to orbital velocities of 0.3 to 0.8 meters per second during the winter storm events, and up to 1.5 to 2.0 meters per second during the most extreme, 50-to-100-year-return-period storm events — values that, while still modest relative to the turbidite-flow velocities that are the primary design-load condition for the anchoring system, are sufficiently high that they will subject the pod, over the 50-year design life, to a cumulative fatigue-loading cycle count of approximately 10⁸ to 10⁹ cycles (each wave passage, with a typical period of 10 to 15 seconds, constituting one loading cycle, and the 50-year design life encompassing approximately 1.6 × 10⁹ seconds, corresponding to approximately 1.1 × 10⁸ wave cycles at a 14-second period), a cycle count that is within the high-cycle fatigue regime (10⁶ to 10⁹ cycles) of the LBFRP-001 composite and that will, over the design life, provide an extensive and statistically meaningful dataset on the fatigue-degradation behavior of the composite under realistic, variable-amplitude, spectrum-loading conditions — data that are essential for the life-extension and the recertification of the Atlantis pods beyond the initial 50-year design life, as the post-event recovery period may extend for decades to centuries and the pods may need to remain operational, and occupied, for substantially longer than the nominal 50-year design horizon.

Third, the substrate geology at Site Beta — which, unlike the competent, well-indurated, moderately strong Purisima Formation sandstones of Site Alpha, is underlain by the Franciscan Complex tectonic mélange, the same chaotic, block-in-matrix, serpentinite-and-shale-matrix-dominated bimrock that underlies the San Francisco peninsula and that presents the most severe foundation-engineering challenges in the entire ECD survival architecture (as discussed at length in the CSMFAB076 document and in Parts 005 and 013 of the present mega-analysis) — provides an opportunity to validate the deep-strand anchoring technology that the CSMFAB076 dome requires and to determine whether the Atlantis pod anchoring system, which at Site Alpha relies on the relatively straightforward grouted helical piles in the competent Purisima sandstone, can be adapted, through the use of the same deep-strand tendon technology (the multi-strand, post-tensioned, carbon-fiber-reinforced-polymer or high-grade-steel tendons, grouted into boreholes extending hundreds of meters into the underlying, competent basement rock, bypassing the weak, sheared, low-friction-angle serpentinite and shale matrix of the Franciscan mélange), for deployment on the Franciscan substrate that underlies much of the California continental margin from the Golden Gate northward to the Oregon border, and that would otherwise, because of its geotechnical deficiencies, be excluded from consideration for the Atlantis pod deployments. The successful adaptation of the deep-strand anchoring technology to the submerged, remotely installed Atlantis pod — an adaptation that requires the development of a seafloor-deployable, ROV-operated, directional-drilling and grouting system capable of installing the strand anchors to depths of 100 to 300 meters below the seabed, in the challenging and unpredictable ground conditions of the Franciscan mélange, without the benefit of the surface-based drilling rigs and the real-time, human-operated steering and monitoring that are employed for the terrestrial deep-strand anchor installations — is one of the most technically demanding components of the Site Beta deployment, and the lessons learned from this deployment will be directly applicable to the foundation design of the larger, future Atlantis communities that may need to be sited on Franciscan-mélange substrates, either because of the constraints of the available sites (the competent Purisima and other well-indurated sedimentary formations are not everywhere present beneath the California continental shelf) or because of the strategic requirements (the need to position the communities at specific depths, at specific distances from the coastline, or at specific azimuthal orientations relative to the predicted displacement wave approach direction, regardless of the underlying geology).

## 3.2 Detailed Geological Characterization of the Franciscan Complex Substrate at Site Beta

The Franciscan Complex at Site Beta, as characterized by the detailed geophysical surveys (the high-resolution, multi-channel seismic-reflection profiles, collected using a 24-channel, 300-meter-long streamer and a 210-cubic-inch, sleeve-gun seismic source, providing a vertical resolution of approximately 3 to 5 meters and a depth of penetration of 500 to 1,000 meters below the seabed), the geotechnical boreholes (three rotary-drill boreholes, designated SFB-01, SFB-02, and SFB-03, drilled to depths of 150, 200, and 250 meters below the seabed, respectively, using a remotely operated seafloor drill rig deployed from the Charlemagne-class vessel CSM Charles Darwin), and the laboratory testing of the recovered core samples (the triaxial compression, the direct shear, the consolidation, the unconfined compression, the point-load, and the slake-durability tests, conducted in accordance with the ASTM and the ISRM standards), exhibits the full, characteristic suite of Franciscan mélange features that have made this formation notorious among the geotechnical engineers who have had the misfortune of designing foundations — for bridges, for dams, for tunnels, for buildings, for landslide-stabilization systems — within its chaotic, unpredictable, and mechanically treacherous confines.

The Franciscan mélange at Site Beta consists of a heterogeneous, tectonically sheared, block-in-matrix assemblage in which variably sized, variably shaped, variably lithologied blocks — ranging from centimeter-scale, angular fragments of radiolarian chert, of graywacke sandstone, of greenstone (altered, low-grade-metamorphosed, submarine basalt), of blueschist (the high-pressure, low-temperature, glaucophane-lawsonite-epidote-bearing metamorphic rock that is the definitive lithological indicator of the subduction-zone origin of the Franciscan Complex), and of eclogite (the even-higher-pressure, garnet-omphacite-bearing metamorphic rock that records the deepest levels of the subduction-zone metamorphism, at depths of 50 to 100 kilometers within the paleo-subduction zone) to meter-scale to decameter-scale blocks of more resistant lithologies — are chaotically suspended within a pervasively sheared, intensely fractured, low-strength, clay-rich matrix composed predominantly of the alteration products of the ultramafic rocks (serpentinite, derived from the hydration and the low-grade metamorphism of the mantle peridotites that were tectonically emplaced into the accretionary prism during the subduction process) and of the argillaceous, fine-grained, hemipelagic sediments (the shale and the mudstone, derived from the terrigenous sediment that was deposited on the oceanic plate before its subduction and that was subsequently scraped off, accreted, and incorporated into the mélange). The matrix is characteristically scaly-foliated — the closely spaced, anastomosing, polished and striated shear surfaces (the "scaly fabric" of the geotechnical literature), produced by the intense, non-coaxial, ductile-to-brittle shearing that the mélange has experienced during its accretion and its subsequent tectonic deformation — and it weathers rapidly, upon exposure to air and water, to a soft, swelling, low-strength clay (the montmorillonite-smectite group of clay minerals, derived from the alteration of the serpentinite and the volcanic ash, are particularly abundant and problematic, because of their high cation-exchange capacity, their high specific surface area, and their propensity to absorb water and to swell — increasing their volume by factors of 2 to 5 — upon wetting, generating swelling pressures that can exceed 1 to 2 MPa and that can cause the heaving and the cracking of foundations, the jacking of anchor tendons, and the destabilization of the seabed slopes).

The geotechnical properties of the Franciscan mélange matrix at Site Beta, as determined from the triaxial shear testing of the undisturbed and the remolded matrix samples recovered from the SFB-01, SFB-02, and SFB-03 boreholes, are as follows: the effective stress friction angle (φ') of the sheared, remolded, residual-state matrix clay — which is the relevant strength parameter for the evaluation of the long-term, drained stability of the anchor tendon bond zone, because the matrix, having been pre-sheared by the tectonic deformation, will tend to fail along the pre-existing, residual-state shear surfaces rather than developing new, peak-strength failure surfaces — ranges from 8° to 14°, with an average value of 11°, and the effective stress cohesion intercept (c') is essentially zero (less than 5 kPa, the resolution limit of the triaxial testing apparatus), consistent with the scaly-foliated, pre-sheared, low-cohesion character of the mélange matrix, which relies entirely on the interlocking friction of the scaly-foliated fabric — a friction that, as noted, is minimal — for its shear resistance, and which, under the sustained, long-duration, high-magnitude loading of the anchor tendon, will creep (deform viscoplastically at a slow but continuous rate, driven by the stress concentrations at the asperities of the shear surfaces) and may eventually fail through the progressive, time-dependent rupture of the interlocking asperities — a failure mode that is well known in the geotechnical literature as "creep rupture" or "static fatigue" and that has been responsible for the delayed, long-term failure of numerous rock-anchor and soil-nail systems installed in the Franciscan mélange and in other, similar, tectonically sheared, low-strength, clay-rich formations.

The intact, unsheared blocks within the mélange — the competent, low-porosity, well-cemented graywacke sandstone blocks, the dense, fine-grained, brittle, conchoidally fracturing chert blocks, and the tough, massive, high-strength greenstone and blueschist blocks — have engineering properties that are orders of magnitude superior to those of the surrounding matrix: the graywacke blocks have unconfined compressive strengths (UCS) of 50 to 150 MPa, effective stress friction angles (φ') of 30° to 40°, and cohesion intercepts (c') of 5 to 20 MPa; the chert blocks have UCS values of 100 to 300 MPa, friction angles of 35° to 45°, and cohesion intercepts of 10 to 30 MPa; and the greenstone and blueschist blocks have UCS values of 80 to 250 MPa, friction angles of 35° to 50°, and cohesion intercepts of 15 to 40 MPa — values that, individually, would be more than adequate to support the anchor-tendon bond stresses, but the problem, of course, is that the blocks are not continuous; they are isolated, randomly distributed, unpredictably sized and shaped inclusions within the weak, creeping, failing matrix, and an anchor tendon that is grouted into a block — rather than into the matrix — will experience a radically different, and far more favorable, load-transfer response than a tendon that is grouted into the matrix, but the tendon cannot distinguish, at the time of installation, whether the bond zone has intercepted a block or whether it is entirely within the matrix, and the resulting uncertainty in the tendon capacity — the possibility that a tendon that, on the basis of the average matrix properties, is predicted to fail at a load of, say, 500 kN, might actually, because the bond zone has intercepted a large, competent graywacke block, be capable of supporting 2,000 or 3,000 kN, or might, conversely, because the bond zone is entirely within the weak matrix and, moreover, within a particularly weak, particularly clay-rich, particularly intensely sheared zone of the matrix, fail at a load of only 200 to 300 kN — is an irreducible feature of the Franciscan mélange foundation environment that must be accommodated, in the design of the anchoring system, through the use of conservative, statistically-based, lower-bound design parameters (the φ' of 8°, the c' of zero), through the deployment of a sufficiently large number of anchor tendons per pod (the Site Beta design calls for 16 tendons — twice the number at Site Alpha — each with a design working load of 200 kN, providing a total anchoring capacity of 3,200 kN, approximately 18 times the predicted maximum lateral load from the turbidite flow at the Site Beta pod location), and through the incorporation of a robust, real-time, fiber-optic strain-monitoring system (the Brillouin Optical Time-Domain Reflectometry, BOTDR, system, in which a single, continuous, looped optical fiber, embedded in the grout column of each anchor tendon, interrogates the strain distribution along the full length of the tendon — from the seabed anchor head to the distal end of the bond zone — at a spatial resolution of approximately 1 meter and a strain resolution of approximately 20 microstrain, providing continuous, real-time monitoring of the load transfer along each tendon and enabling the early detection of the tendon distress — the progressive debonding, the creep rupture of the matrix, or the sudden failure of the grout-to-rock bond — that, if undetected, could lead to the catastrophic, progressive collapse of the anchoring system).

## 3.3 Oceanographic Regime, Turbidity-Current Exposure, and Wave-Current-Seabed Interaction at the Shelf Edge

The oceanographic regime at Site Beta is dominated by the interaction of the California Current System — the southward-flowing, eastern-boundary, upwelling-favorable surface current that transports the cool, nutrient-rich, subarctic-origin water along the U.S. West Coast — with the complex, canyon-incised, shelf-edge bathymetry that generates intense, localized, three-dimensional current features: the upwelling filaments and the squirts (the narrow, jet-like, offshore-directed surface-current features, typically 5 to 25 kilometers wide and 50 to 200 kilometers long, that transport the upwelled, nutrient-rich, high-chlorophyll coastal water from the nearshore upwelling centers — such as the Point Reyes and the Año Nuevo upwelling centers, north and south of Site Beta, respectively — into the offshore, oligotrophic waters of the California Current), the cyclonic and the anticyclonic eddies (the rotating, 10-to-50-kilometer-diameter, surface-intensified, subsurface-extending current features that are generated by the instability of the alongshore coastal jet and that can persist, as coherent, identifiable features, for weeks to months, trapping the upwelled water, the plankton, and the larval fish within their rotating cores and transporting them, as the eddies drift slowly — at 1 to 5 kilometers per day — westward and southwestward, into the open-ocean environment), and the internal tides and the internal solitary waves (the subsurface, density-interface oscillations that are generated by the interaction of the barotropic tidal currents with the steep, canyon-incised, shelf-edge bathymetry and that propagate, as trains of large-amplitude — 10 to 50 meters vertical displacement —, highly nonlinear, soliton-like waves, both shoreward across the continental shelf and seaward into the deep ocean, generating periodic, high-frequency — 10 to 30 minutes period — fluctuations in the current velocity, the temperature, the salinity, and the turbidity at the Site Beta depth and presenting a dynamic, energetic, and, at times, turbulent environment that the Atlantis pod and its anchoring system must be designed to withstand).

The mean current velocity at the Site Beta depth of 85 meters, as measured by the ADCP mooring deployed during the site-survey phase, is 0.25 meters per second (range 0.03 to 1.1 m/s), with the flow direction predominantly alongshore (orientations of 140° to 160° and 320° to 340° true, corresponding to the northwestward and the southeastward flow directions, respectively, that characterize the Davidson Current — the poleward, winter-season, surface-and-subsurface countercurrent that flows inshore of, and counter to, the equatorward California Current — and the California Undercurrent — the poleward, subsurface, 100-to-400-meter-depth, narrow, high-velocity, ribbon-like current that flows along the continental slope, transporting the warm, saline, low-oxygen, equatorial-origin water poleward along the U.S. West Coast). The internal-tide-induced current-velocity fluctuations at the Site Beta depth — the periodic, semidiurnal (12.42-hour period) and diurnal (24.0-hour period) oscillations of the horizontal and the vertical current-velocity components, associated with the propagation of the internal tidal waves across the shelf edge — have amplitudes of 0.1 to 0.4 meters per second (for the horizontal component) and 0.05 to 0.15 meters per second (for the vertical component), and the internal-solitary-wave-induced fluctuations — the transient, shorter-period (10 to 30 minutes), larger-amplitude, highly nonlinear pulses of the current velocity, associated with the passage of the individual solitary waves that comprise the internal tidal bore — have amplitudes of 0.3 to 0.7 meters per second (for the horizontal component) and 0.1 to 0.3 meters per second (for the vertical component), with the vertical component, in particular, being capable of generating significant, fluctuating, cycle-by-cycle variations in the dynamic pressure — the pressure associated with the acceleration and the deceleration of the water — at the pod-hull surface, contributing to the fatigue-loading cycle count that, as discussed above, is a critical factor in the long-term structural integrity of the LBFRP-001 composite.

The turbidity-current exposure at Site Beta, while lower, under normal (non-catastrophic) conditions, than at Site Alpha (because Site Beta is further from the terrestrial sediment sources — the Salinas and the Pajaro Rivers, which discharge the bulk of the terrigenous sediment into the Monterey Bay — and because the nepheloid layer — the turbid, near-bottom, suspended-sediment-rich water mass that overlies the continental shelf and the upper slope — is thinner and less concentrated at the greater depth and the greater distance from the shore), is, under the crustal displacement scenario, substantially higher than at Site Alpha, because Site Beta is positioned directly on the path of the turbidite flows that, during the recession phase of the displacement event, will drain from the flooded interior of California — the temporary inland sea that, as modeled in Part 04 of the CDGA and in Part 015 of the present mega-analysis, will occupy the Central Valley and the lower elevations of the Coast Ranges — through the Golden Gate and the other, lesser, coastal outlets, generating massive, sediment-laden, high-velocity, erosive flows that will cascade down the continental slope, entraining enormous volumes of the unconsolidated shelf and slope sediment, and ultimately depositing their sediment load as turbidite fans on the abyssal plain of the Pacific Ocean floor. The CFD simulations of the recession-phase turbidite flows at Site Beta, presented in Part 015, indicate that the flows will arrive at the site from the east-northeast, traveling along the bathymetric lows — the gullies and the channels that dissect the continental slope — at velocities of 15 to 25 meters per second, with suspended-sediment concentrations of 50 to 200 grams per liter (5 to 20 percent by volume), and with boulder-impact energies (for the 3-meter-diameter, 40-ton granite boulders that are entrained from the Sierra Nevada and the Coast Ranges and transported, by the recession flows, out to the continental margin) of up to 5 × 10⁷ joules at the point of impact — an energy that is approximately five times the energy that would be experienced by a pod at the shallower, more sheltered, lower-flow-velocity Site Alpha location, and that demands the full, integrated, four-layer turbidite defense architecture — the teardrop hull geometry, the auxetic cladding, the acoustic cavitation bubble curtain, and the CNT-reinforced polyurea abrasion-resistant coating — operating at its maximum, design-basis performance levels to ensure the survival of the pod and its occupants. The successful deployment and survival of the Atlantis pod at Site Beta, under these extreme, design-basis, turbidite-flow conditions, will therefore constitute the most demanding, the most informative, and the most confidence-building validation test of the Atlantis turbidite-defense architecture, and the data that are collected — from the strain gauges, the accelerometers, the pressure transducers, and the video cameras that will instrument the Site Beta pod during the crustal displacement event — will be invaluable for the calibration and the refinement of the CFD models and for the optimization of the turbidite-defense design for the larger, future Atlantis communities that will be deployed, at even greater depths and in even more exposed locations, in the subsequent phases of the Aegis C underwater habitation program.

## 3.4 Deep-Strand Anchoring System Design and Deployment for Franciscan Mélange Substrates

The deep-strand anchoring system designed for Site Beta represents a radical departure from the relatively straightforward, shallow-embedment, grouted-helical-pile system that is employed at Site Alpha, and it adapts, for the submerged, remotely installed, ROV-operated deployment environment, the same fundamental technology — the multi-strand, post-tensioned, high-capacity, deep-rock-anchor — that the CSMFAB076 dome design specifies for the San Francisco peninsular foundation, and that has been used, at the terrestrial scale, for the stabilization of large dams, the anchoring of suspension-bridge towers, the reinforcement of deep excavations, and the retrofit of seismically vulnerable structures. The Site Beta deep-strand system consists of 16 individual anchors, arranged in a circular pattern around the pod's base ring (diameter 8 meters, compared with 6 meters at Site Alpha, to accommodate the larger number of anchors and the greater lateral-load demand), with each anchor comprising: (1) a 150-millimeter-diameter, rotary-drilled, cased borehole, extending vertically (or at an inclination of 5° to 10° from the vertical, to provide the battered configuration that, as at Site Alpha, increases the lateral-load resistance of the anchor group) from the seabed to a depth of 200 meters below the seabed, penetrating through the 20-to-50-meter-thick zone of intensely sheared, low-strength, low-friction-angle, zero-cohesion serpentinite and shale mélange matrix that characterizes the upper portion of the Franciscan Complex at Site Beta, and terminating — after a transition zone, 30 to 80 meters thick, of progressively less sheared, progressively more competent, progressively higher-block-proportion mélange — in the underlying, relatively intact, relatively unsheared, relatively high-strength, high-friction-angle, high-block-proportion metagraywacke and greenstone of the lower Franciscan Complex, at depths of 150 to 200 meters below the seabed, where the rock mass is sufficiently competent (UCS of 30 to 80 MPa, joint spacing of 0.5 to 2.0 meters, Rock Mass Rating (RMR) of 50 to 65, Geological Strength Index (GSI) of 45 to 60) to provide the long-term, creep-resistant, high-capacity bond that the anchor system requires; (2) a central, 50-millimeter-diameter, high-strength (1,860 MPa ultimate tensile strength, Grade 270, low-relaxation, seven-wire, epoxy-coated, corrosion-protected) steel strand tendon, or alternatively — for the corrosive, chloride-rich, marine-environment application — a carbon-fiber-reinforced-polymer (CFRP) tendon with a comparable tensile strength (1,500 to 2,500 MPa) and a substantially superior corrosion resistance (the CFRP is immune to chloride-induced stress-corrosion cracking and to hydrogen embrittlement, the two primary corrosion-failure modes of high-strength steel in marine environments), extending from the anchor head at the seabed, through the free-length portion of the borehole (the upper 150 to 180 meters, where the tendon is encased in a corrosion-protective, grease-filled, polyethylene sleeve that decouples it from the surrounding grout and that allows it to elongate freely, without transferring load to the weak mélange matrix), to the bond-length portion of the borehole (the lower 20 to 50 meters, where the tendon is stripped of its sleeve and is bonded directly to the surrounding grout, and where the tensile load in the tendon is transferred, through the grout-to-rock bond — the shear-stress transfer at the grout-rock interface — to the competent, high-strength, high-friction-angle metagraywacke and greenstone of the lower Franciscan Complex); (3) a high-strength, low-shrinkage, sulfate-resistant, Portland-cement-based grout (water-to-cement ratio of 0.35 to 0.40, with the addition of a superplasticizer — a polycarboxylate-ether-based, high-range, water-reducing admixture — to achieve a flowable, pumpable consistency while maintaining the low water content that is necessary for the high compressive strength (50 to 70 MPa at 28 days) and the low permeability (less than 10⁻¹¹ meters per second) of the cured grout), injected, through the drill-rod or the grout pipe, from the bottom of the borehole upward (the "tremie" method, which displaces the drilling fluid — the seawater or the bentonite slurry — upward and out of the borehole, ensuring that the grout completely fills the annulus between the tendon and the borehole wall and that no voids, no gaps, and no drilling-fluid-contaminated weak zones are left behind); and (4) a post-tensioning system, consisting of a hydraulic jack, a bearing plate, and a locking nut or a wedge-grip assembly, that, after the grout has cured to the required strength (typically 7 to 14 days for the high-early-strength grout mix that is specified for the marine application, where the delays associated with the curing time represent a significant cost and a significant operational-logistical burden), tensions each anchor tendon to a force of 250 kN (approximately 25 metric tons-force, corresponding to a stress of approximately 0.25 × the ultimate tensile strength of the steel tendon, or 0.15 × the ultimate tensile strength of the CFRP tendon — stress levels that are within the long-term, creep-rupture-safe, sustained-load range for both materials), locking the tendon in tension and compressing the pod base ring against the seabed, preloading the foundation and eliminating the slack and the settlement that would otherwise occur, under the cyclic, reversing, wave-and-current-induced loads, over the 50-year design life.

The installation of the 16 deep-strand anchors at Site Beta — each requiring the drilling of a 200-meter-deep, 150-millimeter-diameter borehole through the heterogeneous, unpredictable, block-and-matrix Franciscan mélange, using a seafloor-deployed, remotely operated, directional-drilling system — is, as noted in Section 3.1, one of the most technically challenging components of the Site Beta deployment, and the development of the seafloor-drilling system — a collaborative effort between Carrington Storm Motors, the Monterey Bay Aquarium Research Institute (MBARI), and the international offshore-drilling contractor Seadrill — has been underway, as of the date of the present analysis, for approximately 18 months, with the first-generation prototype system (the "SeaMole-1") having successfully completed the drilling of a 120-meter-deep test borehole in the Franciscan mélange at a terrestrial site near Point Reyes, California (where the mélange is exposed at the surface, providing a convenient, accessible, and representative test environment), and with the second-generation, marinized, depth-rated (to 500 meters) system (the "SeaMole-2") currently (as of July 2026) undergoing the factory-acceptance testing at the Carrington Storm Motors Mojave facility, with the first offshore deployment, at Site Beta, scheduled for the first quarter of 2027. The successful deployment and the load-testing of the 16 deep-strand anchors at Site Beta will, if achieved, constitute a major technological milestone — the first successful installation of the deep-strand anchoring technology in the Franciscan mélange in a fully submerged, remotely operated, offshore environment — and will, in conjunction with the other validation tests conducted at Site Beta, establish the feasibility of the Atlantis pod deployment on the Franciscan mélange substrates that underlie much of the California continental margin, significantly expanding the geographical range and the strategic flexibility of the ECD survival infrastructure program.
