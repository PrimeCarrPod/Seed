# Document 1: Historical Foundations & Pathophysiology of Artificial Hemoglobin Survival Research
## Piece 2/18: The Historical Evolution of Blood Surrogates - Early Experiments

The quest for a suitable alternative to human blood dates back to the seventeenth century, when pioneers like Sir Christopher Wren proposed the intravenous infusion of ale, wine, and opium as rudimentary blood substitutes. Over the centuries, an assortment of fluids, including milk, plant resins, and sheep blood, were utilized with predictably fatal hemolytic reactions. The fundamental immunological barrier—the ABO blood group system discovered by Karl Landsteiner in 1901—remained unknown, rendering all xenogeneic and allogeneic transfusions essentially random experiments with mortality exceeding 50%.

The first recorded intravenous infusion of a blood substitute occurred in 1667 when Jean-Baptiste Denis transfused lamb's blood into a 15-year-old boy, who survived—likely due to the small volume (approximately 300 mL) and the boy's robust compensatory mechanisms. Subsequent attempts by Denis and Richard Lower in London led to fatal outcomes, prompting the French Parliament to ban transfusions in 1670. This prohibition lasted over 150 years, during which the physiological basis of transfusion reactions remained obscure.

The 19th century saw continued experimentation with milk (1854, Toronto), saline solutions (1830s, cholera epidemics), and various colloidal suspensions. Thomas Latta's use of intravenous saline for cholera in 1832 demonstrated volume resuscitation principles but failed to address oxygen-carrying capacity. The fundamental limitation of all pre-20th century substitutes was the absence of an oxygen-carrying molecule—these were volume expanders only, incapable of sustaining tissue oxygenation in the absence of native hemoglobin.

Karl Landsteiner's 1901 discovery of ABO blood groups transformed transfusion from a lethal lottery into a reproducible medical procedure. The subsequent development of citrate anticoagulation (Hustin 1914, Lewisohn 1915) and glucose preservation (Rous and Turner 1916) enabled blood storage, creating the first "blood banks" during World War I. However, allogeneic blood possesses severe logistical limitations for emergency entrapment scenarios: strict refrigeration (2-6°C cold chain), maximum shelf life of 42 days (with progressive 2,3-DPG depletion and storage lesion), time-consuming type-and-crossmatch compatibility testing (45-60 minutes minimum), and risk of blood-borne pathogen transmission (HIV, hepatitis B/C, West Nile virus, babesiosis, Chagas disease). In austere environments—a collapsed mine at -500 meters, a battlefield, a submerged submarine—these constraints render whole blood transfusion nearly impossible.
