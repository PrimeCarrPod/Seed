# Tracing Humanity's Continuous Knowledge — Part 14
## DNA and Linguistic Correlations: The Deep-Time Relationship Between Genetic Lineages, Language Phyla, and the Co-Evolution of Biological and Cultural Inheritance Systems

### 14.1 The Conceptual Foundations of Gene-Language Coevolution: Theoretical Frameworks for Integrating Archaeogenetic and Historical Linguistic Evidence

The relationship between the genetic lineages of human populations and the languages those populations speak constitutes one of the most fertile, most contested, and most rapidly advancing frontiers of interdisciplinary research in the human sciences, a frontier at which the methodological resources of population genetics—the analysis of mitochondrial DNA, Y-chromosome, and autosomal genome-wide variation to reconstruct the demographic history of human populations, including their divergence times, migration routes, admixture events, and long-term effective population sizes—are brought into systematic dialogue with the resources of historical linguistics—the comparative reconstruction of ancestral proto-languages, the estimation of language family divergence times through glottochronology and Bayesian phylogenetic methods, and the identification of lexical, phonological, and grammatical innovations that define linguistic subgroups and their relative chronology of divergence—to address a set of fundamental questions that are central to the concerns of the present report and that have remained stubbornly resistant to resolution through either discipline operating in isolation: the question of whether specific genetic lineages are consistently and predictably associated with specific language families, such that the branching structure of the human genetic tree can be mapped onto the branching structure of the human linguistic tree, and the temporal depth of such associations provides a direct estimate of the time at which the ancestral populations that carried the ancestral languages diverged from one another; the question of whether language shift—the replacement of one language by another within a population without the corresponding replacement of the population's genetic lineage—is sufficiently frequent in human history that the gene-language correlation, while statistically detectable at the broadest macro-levels (the association of particular mitochondrial and Y-chromosome haplogroups with particular continental-scale population groupings), breaks down at the finer levels of resolution required to trace the specific histories of individual language families and the populations that speak them; the question of whether the most basal, deeply diverged linguistic lineages on each continent—the Khoisan click languages of Southern Africa, the non-Pama-Nyungan languages of northern Australia, the Paleo-Siberian languages of northeastern Eurasia, the language isolates of the Amazon Basin—are spoken by populations who are also the most basal, deeply diverged genetic lineages on their respective continents, a pattern that would support the hypothesis that the deepest linguistic divisions in the human family tree reflect the deepest population splits in the human genetic tree and that both were established during the initial colonization of each continent by anatomically modern humans; and the question, perhaps most consequential for the central concerns of this report, of whether the temporal depth of continuous oral knowledge transmission that has been independently established through the geomythological, archaeogenetic, and palaeo-astronomical evidence reviewed in the preceding parts can be corroborated through the independent calibration provided by the estimated divergence times of the language families spoken by the continuous cultures in question, a calibration that would provide a second, entirely independent line of evidence for the multi-millennial temporal depth of continuous handed knowledge and that would substantially strengthen the already-robust empirical foundation upon which the central thesis of this report—that oral human cultures possess the cognitive and institutional capacity to preserve empirical observations across temporal spans that vastly exceed the entire documented history of Western civilization—is constructed.

The theoretical framework that has guided the investigation of gene-language coevolution across the past three decades has undergone a fundamental transformation, from the simplistic, deterministic models of the early period—which assumed a one-to-one correspondence between genetic lineages and language families, such that the branching structure of the human genetic tree could be read as a direct proxy for the branching structure of the human linguistic tree, an assumption that was quickly falsified by the numerous documented cases of language shift, population admixture, and elite dominance in which the genetic and linguistic histories of a population diverge dramatically—to the more sophisticated, probabilistic models that currently guide research in the field, which recognize that the relationship between genes and languages is complex, contingent, and context-dependent, being modulated by the specific demographic, ecological, and historical circumstances under which particular populations have lived and migrated and interacted, and which are therefore investigated not through the search for a universal, invariant law of gene-language coevolution but through the systematic, case-by-case analysis of the specific historical processes that have generated the observed patterns of correlation and divergence between genetic and linguistic diversity in each region of the world, an analytical approach that, while more demanding and less elegant than the search for universal laws, is the only approach capable of generating the specific, historically grounded, and empirically validated reconstructions of population and language history that are required to address the specific questions that motivate the present analysis.

### 14.2 The Khoisan Linguistic Basality and the San Genetic Lineage: Convergent Evidence for the Deepest Human Population and Language Split

The San peoples of Southern Africa, whose basal genetic lineage—diverging from the common ancestral population of all other living humans between 100,000 and 200,000 years before present—represent the deepest surviving branch of the human genetic tree, as documented in detail in Part 2 of this report, are also the speakers of what are widely recognized by historical linguists as among the most phonologically, grammatically, and lexically distinctive languages on Earth: the Khoisan languages, characterized by their extensive inventories of click consonants produced through the velaric ingressive airstream mechanism, in which a partial vacuum is created within the oral cavity by the retraction of the tongue body against the velum and its subsequent release at one of five primary places of articulation (bilabial, dental, alveolar, palatal, or lateral alveolar), generating the distinctive "click" sounds that are unique, outside of the Khoisan languages themselves, to a small number of neighboring Bantu languages (specifically the Nguni subgroup, including Zulu and Xhosa) that have borrowed clicks through extensive language contact with Khoisan-speaking populations, and that represent what is almost certainly the most ancient phonological feature preserved in any living human language, a feature whose evolution and maintenance across the enormous temporal span of San linguistic isolation—at minimum 100,000 years and possibly more than 200,000 years if the genetic and linguistic divergence chronologies can be aligned—would represent one of the most extraordinary cases of linguistic conservatism ever documented, and whose presence in the contemporary Khoisan languages provides a direct linguistic link to the earliest period of anatomically modern human linguistic evolution at or near the time of the emergence of fully modern syntactic language itself, a period that the archaeological evidence for the emergence of symbolic behavior—the geometric engravings on ochre from Blombos Cave dated to approximately 100,000 years before present, the perforated shell beads from multiple sites in North and East Africa dated to between 80,000 and 100,000 years before present, and the representational art from sites across Africa, Europe, and Island Southeast Asia—suggests was characterized by the full emergence of the cognitive capacity for symbolic communication that underlies all human language, and whose linguistic products, in the form of the ancestral language from which the contemporary Khoisan languages descend, may have been continuously transmitted—through a process of incremental phonological, grammatical, and lexical change that transformed the ancestral language into its modern descendants, but that never severed the continuity of transmission—across the entire temporal span of San biological and cultural existence, a linguistic continuity that, if it could be definitively established, would represent the most temporally profound instance of continuous cultural transmission ever documented and would establish the San languages as the oldest continuously transmitted human intellectual tradition on Earth.

The genetic evidence for the extreme antiquity and isolation of the San population is unequivocal, supported by multiple independent lines of genomic analysis, including the phylogenetic reconstruction of mitochondrial DNA and Y-chromosome lineages that place the San at the base of the human phylogenetic tree, the analysis of whole-genome autosomal SNP data that consistently identifies the San as the most genetically divergent living human population by every measure of genetic distance, and the estimation of Neanderthal and Denisovan admixture proportions that indicate the San have lower levels of archaic admixture than any other human population—consistent with the San ancestors having diverged from the common ancestral population prior to the admixture events with Neanderthals and Denisovans that occurred during the out-of-Africa dispersal—and that collectively establish, beyond any reasonable genetic doubt, that the San represent the direct genetic descendants of the earliest diverged branch of the human family tree, a conclusion that is fully consistent with the linguistic evidence for the extreme distinctiveness of the Khoisan languages and their status as what historical linguists classify as a "residual" or "relict" grouping—a set of languages whose shared features are not indicative of a common genetic descent from a single ancestral proto-language (the Khoisan languages are now widely recognized as comprising at least three and possibly five genetically independent language families, including Khoe-Kwadi, Kx'a, and Tuu, whose deep genetic relationship to one another, if any, is obscured by the enormous time depth involved), but rather reflect the preservation of ancient linguistic features that have been lost from all other language families through the processes of phonological, grammatical, and lexical change, a pattern that is exactly what would be predicted if the Khoisan languages represent the surviving fragments of the original linguistic diversity of Southern Africa, spoken by populations who have been continuously resident in the region since the initial colonization of Southern Africa by anatomically modern humans and whose languages have evolved in situ, in relative isolation from the linguistic developments that transformed the languages of the populations who dispersed out of Africa and into the rest of the world, a linguistic relict pattern that perfectly mirrors the genetic relict pattern of the San population—the deepest branch of the human genetic tree, preserved in geographic isolation at the southern tip of the African continent—and that provides a compelling, convergent line of evidence for the exceptional antiquity and continuity of the San people and their knowledge systems.

### 14.3 The Pama-Nyungan Expansion and the Genomic History of Aboriginal Australia: Aligning Linguistic Phylogeny with Population Genetic Structure

The most extensively documented case of gene-language coevolution outside of Africa—and the case that is of greatest direct relevance to the central concerns of this report given the unparalleled temporal depth of Aboriginal Australian continuous culture documented throughout the preceding parts—is the correlation between the genomic history of Aboriginal Australia, as reconstructed through the high-resolution whole-genome analyses of contemporary Aboriginal populations and the increasingly informative ancient DNA extracted from pre-colonial Australian human remains, and the linguistic history of the Australian continent, as reconstructed through the comparative analysis of the approximately 250 Aboriginal languages that were spoken at the time of European contact and that are classified by historical linguists into two major groupings: the Pama-Nyungan language family, which encompasses approximately 90 percent of the Australian continent and includes the vast majority of the languages spoken across the central deserts, the southern temperate zones, and the eastern seaboard, and which is characterized by a shared set of lexical, phonological, and grammatical innovations that define the Pama-Nyungan family as a genuine genetic grouping descended from a single ancestral Proto-Pama-Nyungan language; and the non-Pama-Nyungan languages, a residual category encompassing approximately 20 linguistically diverse and deeply divergent language families—including Tiwi, Bunuban, Worrorran, and the many other small language families of the Kimberley Plateau and the Top End of the Northern Territory—that are concentrated in the northernmost regions of the continent and that represent the surviving fragments of what was presumably a much greater linguistic diversity that existed across the entire Australian continent prior to the expansion of the Pama-Nyungan languages out of their ancestral homeland—hypothesized on linguistic grounds to have been located in the Gulf Plains region of northwestern Queensland—across the continent, an expansion that, on the basis of the calibrated linguistic phylogeny of the Pama-Nyungan family estimated through Bayesian phylogenetic methods applied to cognate sets of basic vocabulary, is estimated to have begun approximately 5,000 to 6,000 years before present and to have been completed, with the Pama-Nyungan languages occupying essentially their historically documented geographic distribution, by approximately 3,000 years before present, making the Pama-Nyungan expansion one of the most geographically extensive and linguistically transformative language spreads in human history, comparable in its scale and its speed to the Indo-European, Bantu, and Austronesian expansions but occurring entirely independently of agriculture—the Aboriginal Australian economy remained based on hunting, gathering, and fishing throughout the entirety of the Pama-Nyungan expansion, and the spread of the language family must therefore be attributed to some combination of demographic expansion driven by technological, social, or ecological innovations, cultural diffusion through existing social networks, or the prestige-driven adoption of Pama-Nyungan languages by non-Pama-Nyungan-speaking groups, a question that remains unresolved and that is one of the most important and most actively investigated problems in Australian historical linguistics and archaeology.

The genomic evidence for Aboriginal Australian population history, derived from the high-coverage whole-genome sequencing of individuals from contemporary Aboriginal communities representing the major linguistic groupings and geographic regions of the continent—including speakers of Pama-Nyungan languages from the Western Desert, the Pilbara, the Kimberley, Arnhem Land, Cape York, and the southeastern temperate regions, and speakers of non-Pama-Nyungan languages from the Daly River, the Kimberley, and Arnhem Land—has been progressively aligned with the linguistic evidence for the Pama-Nyungan expansion, revealing a pattern of genetic structure that closely mirrors the linguistic geography of the continent and that provides independent genomic confirmation of the Pama-Nyungan expansion hypothesis: the genetic data consistently identify a distinctive Pama-Nyungan-associated autosomal genetic component that is shared across the full geographic extent of the Pama-Nyungan language family, from the Western Desert in the west to Cape York in the northeast and the southeastern temperate regions in the south, and that is most strongly represented in the Gulf Plains region of northwestern Queensland—precisely the region that linguistic evidence identifies as the most likely Pama-Nyungan homeland, on the basis of the principle of linguistic geography that the area of greatest linguistic diversity within a language family is typically the area in which the family originated and from which it subsequently spread—and that progressively decreases in frequency with increasing geographic distance from the hypothesized homeland region, a pattern of genetic clinal variation that is exactly what would be predicted if the Pama-Nyungan expansion involved the demographic movement of Pama-Nyungan-speaking populations out of the Gulf Plains homeland and their subsequent admixture with the pre-existing non-Pama-Nyungan-speaking populations of the regions into which they expanded, generating a gradient of Pama-Nyungan genetic ancestry that parallels the gradient of Pama-Nyungan linguistic replacement, a genomic signature of the language expansion that provides independent, quantitative confirmation of the linguistic reconstruction and that establishes a temporal framework—calibrated through the coalescent-based estimation of the time at which the Pama-Nyungan-associated genetic component diverged from the ancestral Aboriginal Australian gene pool and began its geographic expansion, a divergence time that is estimated to be between approximately 4,000 and 6,000 years before present, closely matching the linguistic estimate—for the Pama-Nyungan expansion that is entirely consistent with the linguistic chronology and that provides a second, entirely independent line of evidence for the multi-millennial timescale of Aboriginal Australian continuous culture, demonstrating that the linguistic geography of the Australian continent, as documented at the time of European contact, was the product of a complex, multi-layered history of population movement, language spread, and gene-language coevolution that extended across the full span of the mid to late Holocene and that, in the non-Pama-Nyungan-speaking regions of the north, preserved linguistic lineages whose divergence from the common ancestral language of the continent may extend to the initial colonization of Australia at approximately 50,000 to 65,000 years before present, making the non-Pama-Nyungan languages, along with the Khoisan languages of Africa and the Paleo-Siberian languages of northeastern Eurasia, among the most ancient continuously transmitted linguistic lineages on the planet and the surviving repositories of an oral knowledge archive whose temporal depth rivals and in certain respects exceeds that of any other continuous human culture.

### 14.4 Click Languages as Basal: The Phonological, Genetic, and Archaeological Evidence for the Exceptional Antiquity of the Khoisan Oral Tradition

The click consonants that are the most distinctive phonological feature of the Khoisan languages—and that have, since the earliest European encounters with Khoisan-speaking populations in the 17th century, been recognized as a linguistic phenomenon of extraordinary uniqueness and presumably exceptional antiquity—represent, from the perspective of the present analysis, not merely a phonological curiosity but what may be the most significant line of evidence for the deep-time continuity of oral knowledge transmission in Southern Africa, a line of evidence whose significance derives from the specific articulatory, acoustic, and aerodynamic characteristics of click consonants, which are produced through the velaric ingressive airstream mechanism—a fundamentally different mechanism of sound production from the pulmonic egressive airstream that powers the vast majority of speech sounds in all other human languages—and which therefore require the coordinated, precisely timed activation of a complex sequence of articulatory gestures involving the tongue, the velum, the pharyngeal walls, and the laryngeal musculature, a motor sequence that is among the most complex articulatory gestures required by any human language and that can only be acquired, with native-like proficiency, during the critical period of early childhood language acquisition, a fact that has profound implications for the historical linguistics of click languages: because clicks cannot be acquired as a second-language feature by adult learners, and because clicks are never spontaneously innovated by languages that did not previously possess them—there is no documented case, in the entire historical linguistic record, of a language independently developing click consonants except through the borrowing of clicks from an already-existing click language through the mechanism of intensive language contact, typically involving widespread bilingualism and intermarriage between click-using and non-click-using populations—the presence of clicks in a language is, in every documented case, an inherited feature, transmitted continuously from the ancestral proto-language in which the clicks originally evolved to its modern descendant languages through the normal process of intergenerational language transmission, a fact that establishes the temporal depth of click consonant usage as coincident with the temporal depth of the linguistic lineage that transmits them, and that therefore implies, for the Khoisan click languages of Southern Africa, a period of continuous linguistic transmission that is measured not in centuries or millennia but in the tens of millennia that genetic and archaeological evidence indicate the San populations of Southern Africa have been continuously resident in the region, a temporal framework that would place the origin of the click consonant inventory—and the emergence of the ancestral Khoisan linguistic lineage that carried it—at a period that may approach or even exceed 100,000 years before present, making click consonants not merely the oldest continuously transmitted phonological feature in any human language but quite possibly the oldest continuously transmitted cultural trait of any kind in the entire human behavioral repertoire, a deeply provocative hypothesis that, while impossible to test directly given the absence of any direct archaeological evidence for language or speech sounds prior to the invention of writing, is nevertheless consistent with all available lines of indirect evidence—the genetic evidence for the extreme antiquity of the San population, the archaeological evidence for continuous occupation of Southern Africa across the Middle and Late Stone Age, the linguistic evidence for the extreme phonological conservatism of click languages—and that, if accepted as a working hypothesis, establishes the oral knowledge traditions of the Khoisan-speaking San peoples as representing not merely the oldest surviving continuous human culture but the oldest surviving continuous human tradition of any kind, a tradition whose origins lie at or near the very emergence of fully modern human language, cognitive capacity, and symbolic behavior itself.

### 14.5 Comparative Genomics and Linguistics: Methodological Synergies, Remaining Challenges, and the Future of Interdisciplinary Deep-Time Human History Reconstruction

The integration of genomic and linguistic evidence for the reconstruction of deep human history—a methodological program that has achieved its most impressive results, as documented in the preceding sections of this part, in the cases where the genetic and linguistic signals are mutually reinforcing and where the independent chronologies derived from each line of evidence converge upon a consistent reconstruction of population and language history—confronts a set of substantial methodological challenges that must be acknowledged and, to the extent possible, addressed if the full potential of the gene-language coevolution research program is to be realized, challenges that include: the fundamental problem of temporal calibration, rooted in the absence of any direct, independently dated calibration points for the estimation of language divergence times—unlike the genetic clock, which can be calibrated against the fossil record of species divergence times, the archaeological record of population movements, and the directly dated ancient DNA sequences that provide absolute chronological anchor points for the genetic phylogeny, the linguistic clock lacks any equivalent calibration mechanism, and all estimates of language family divergence times are therefore dependent upon assumptions—concerning the rate of lexical replacement, the constancy of that rate across languages and time periods, and the specific calibration points employed (typically historically documented language divergence events, the dates of which are themselves often uncertain)—that introduce substantial, difficult-to-quantify uncertainties into the resulting chronologies, uncertainties that must be carefully propagated through any analysis that attempts to align linguistic and genetic divergence times and that preclude the kind of precise, high-resolution temporal alignment that would be required to definitively establish the co-divergence of specific genetic and linguistic lineages; the problem of statistical non-independence, arising from the fact that the genetic and linguistic data employed in gene-language coevolution studies are often derived from the same populations—the same individuals whose genomes are sequenced are also the speakers whose languages are classified and whose vocabulary is used to reconstruct linguistic phylogenies—and are therefore not statistically independent sources of evidence, a non-independence that can generate spurious correlations between genetic and linguistic variation even in the absence of any genuine historical association between the populations and the languages, a problem that is partially addressed through the use of ancient DNA to provide an independent, temporally deeper source of genetic evidence that is not confounded by the same circularity, but that remains a nontrivial analytical challenge; and the problem of language shift, the most important and most frequent mechanism through which the gene-language correlation breaks down, in which a population abandons its ancestral language and adopts the language of a neighboring, invading, or culturally dominant population without the corresponding replacement of the population's genetic lineage, generating a systematic mismatch between the genetic and linguistic histories of the affected population that can persist for millennia and that, when unrecognized, can lead to fundamentally erroneous reconstructions of population history, a problem that is particularly acute in the regions—Europe, South and Southeast Asia, the Middle East, Mesoamerica, the Andes—where the historical record documents repeated episodes of elite dominance, imperial expansion, and language shift that have systematically obscured the deeper genetic-linguistic relationships, and whose resolution requires the integration of archaeological, historical, and ethnographic evidence—in addition to the genetic and linguistic evidence—to identify the specific historical contexts in which language shift occurred and to correct the resulting distortions in the gene-language correlation.

Despite these methodological challenges—which, while substantial, are characteristic of any interdisciplinary research program operating at the frontiers of methodological capability and are progressively being resolved through the development of more sophisticated analytical methods, the accumulation of larger and more diverse datasets, and the increasing integration of independent lines of evidence from archaeology, paleoclimatology, and the other historical sciences—the gene-language coevolution research program has achieved a series of results that are of fundamental importance to the central thesis of this report and that provide powerful, independent corroboration of the multi-millennial temporal depth of continuous oral knowledge transmission that has been established through the geomythological, archaeogenetic, and palaeo-astronomical evidence reviewed in the preceding parts, results that include: the alignment of the deepest genetic divergences in the human family tree—the San of Southern Africa, the non-Pama-Nyungan-speaking Aboriginal Australians of the northern regions, the Paleo-Siberian populations of northeastern Eurasia—with the deepest linguistic divergences on their respective continents, establishing a convergent pattern in which the populations that were the first to colonize each continent are also the speakers of the most deeply divergent, linguistically conservative languages on that continent, and whose continuous oral traditions are therefore the most likely to preserve empirical observations extending to the earliest periods of human occupation of each region; the genomic confirmation of the major language expansions that have shaped the linguistic geography of the inhabited continents during the Holocene—the Pama-Nyungan expansion in Australia, the Austronesian expansion across Island Southeast Asia and the Pacific, the Bantu expansion across sub-Saharan Africa, the Indo-European expansion across Europe and South Asia—each of which is associated with a distinctive genetic signature that identifies the source population of the expansion and traces its demographic and geographic trajectory, and each of which, in the process of its expansion, overprinted and partially or completely replaced the pre-existing linguistic and cultural diversity of the regions into which it expanded, a process of linguistic and cultural homogenization that is directly analogous to—and, in many cases, historically continuous with—the contemporary processes of linguistic and cultural homogenization analysed in Part 13 of this report, and whose historical dynamics provide essential context for understanding the vulnerability of the surviving linguistic and cultural diversity to the homogenizing forces of the contemporary global economy; and, most fundamentally, the independent, multi-disciplinary confirmation—through the convergent testimony of genetics, linguistics, archaeology, and the other historical sciences—that the continuous oral cultures documented throughout this report are genuinely what they appear to be: the direct biological and cultural descendants of the earliest human populations to occupy their respective regions, preserving, through an unbroken chain of intergenerational transmission extending across the full span of the Holocene and, in the most ancient cases, into the terminal Pleistocene, a body of empirical knowledge, adaptive strategies, and cultural wisdom whose origins lie at the very dawn of the human presence on each of the inhabited continents, and whose preservation into the 21st century—against all the odds, against the relentless homogenizing forces of colonization, globalization, and climate change that have extinguished the vast majority of the continuous knowledge systems that once existed—represents one of the most extraordinary, most precious, and most urgently threatened achievements in the entire history of human culture, an achievement whose significance, as the concluding parts of this report will elaborate, extends far beyond the specific communities who are its direct custodians to encompass the entire human species, whose collective adaptive capacity and whose long-term survival may depend, to a degree that is only beginning to be recognized, upon the preservation and continued transmission of the deepest, oldest, and most empirically grounded human knowledge archive on Earth.
