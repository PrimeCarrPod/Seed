# PART 05: California Stratigraphy and Scouring — The Dual-Wave Hypothesis, Tuna Canyon and Modelo Formations, Sierra Nevada High-Elevation Erratics, Bear Creek Valley Hydraulic Carving, and the Signature of Multi-Kilometer Inertial Mega-Tsunami Inundation

## 5.1 The Santa Monica Mountains: Tuna Canyon Formation and the Problem of Mixed Provenance Conglomerates

The coastal canyons of the Santa Monica Mountains — Zuma Canyon, Corral Canyon, Pena Canyon, and the lesser gorges that dissect the southern flank of the range between Malibu and Point Mugu — expose one of the most enigmatic and, from a uniformitarian perspective, persistently problematic stratigraphic sequences in the California geological record: the Late Cretaceous Tuna Canyon Formation and its overlying Miocene-age Modelo Formation, a juxtaposition of lithofacies so starkly contrasting, so deeply chaotic in its internal organization, and so fundamentally resistant to the standard interpretative frameworks of submarine fan sedimentation, turbidite deposition, and debris-flow emplacement that generations of field geologists, trained in the uniformitarian tradition and equipped with the conceptual toolkit of plate tectonic basin analysis, have been compelled to invoke increasingly elaborate, increasingly *ad hoc* auxiliary hypotheses — invoking, at various times and by various authors, catastrophic submarine landslides on a scale never directly observed, seismically triggered liquefaction events producing kilometer-scale slumps, the foundering of carbonate platforms into deep-marine troughs, the gravitational collapse of unstable slope-apron sediments into axial channel systems, and the cascading failure of multiple, kinematically linked slide blocks — none of which, singly or in combination, satisfactorily accounts for the most salient and, from a catastrophic perspective, the most diagnostic feature of these formations: the intimate, chaotic, and thoroughly homogenized mixing of lithologies, fossil assemblages, and grain sizes that, under any conventional model of sedimentary transport, would never have been brought together into the same depositional unit, let alone into the same hand-sample-sized outcrop.

The Tuna Canyon Formation, as formally defined in the North American Stratigraphic Code, consists of massively bedded, poorly sorted, clast-supported to matrix-supported conglomerates, interbedded with thinner, discontinuous lenses of sandstone and siltstone, exposed over a strike length of approximately 30 kilometers along the southern flank of the Santa Monica Mountains and reaching thicknesses, in the most complete sections, of 1,500 to 2,000 meters — a thickness that, under the conventional interpretation of the unit as a submarine fan-delta deposit accumulating at the base of a steep, tectonically active continental slope, would require a sustained subsidence rate of approximately 0.1 to 0.2 millimeters per year over a depositional interval of 10 to 20 million years, during which the sediment supply, the basin configuration, the sea-level regime, and the tectonic setting remained sufficiently constant to produce a monotonously repetitive sequence of texturally and compositionally similar conglomerate beds without any of the systematic vertical trends — fining-upward cycles, progradational-retrogradational sequences, shifts in provenance, changes in clast composition reflecting the unroofing of the source terrane — that characterize every well-documented ancient submarine fan system in the geological record, from the Paleogene fans of the North Sea to the Permian fans of the Karoo Basin to the modern Amazon and Bengal fans imaged by multichannel seismic reflection profiling. The absence of systematic vertical organization in the Tuna Canyon Formation — the fact that bed after bed, for a cumulative thickness of nearly two kilometers, repeats the same textural characteristics (massive, unsorted, boulder-bearing), the same compositional characteristics (a diverse assemblage of plutonic, volcanic, metamorphic, and sedimentary clasts in proportions that vary randomly from bed to bed), and the same fossil characteristics (a chaotic mixture of shallow-marine, deep-marine, and terrestrial organisms, often fragmented and abraded but sometimes exquisitely preserved, as if killed and entombed within hours of their deaths) — is precisely what the uniformitarian model cannot explain and what the catastrophic model, in contrast, interprets as the unmistakable signature of a single, extraordinarily high-energy hydraulic event that stripped the continental margin of its entire sedimentary cover, from the beach to the abyssal plain, and deposited the resulting thoroughly mixed debris as a single, massively thick, internally unstratified unit from which the only subsequent modification has been the incision of the modern canyons by normal fluvial erosion over the past 10,000 to 12,000 years.

The clast population of the Tuna Canyon conglomerates, as documented in the detailed petrographic and provenance studies conducted by the U.S. Geological Survey and by researchers from the University of California, Santa Barbara, and California State University, Northridge, is remarkable both for its lithological diversity and for the extreme rounding and sorting characteristics that are diametrically opposed to what would be expected for a debris-flow deposit emplaced at the base of a steep continental slope. The clasts range in size from granule-grade (2 to 4 millimeters) to boulder-grade (2 to 5 meters in maximum dimension), and they include: (1) well-rounded, highly spherical porphyritic quartz monzonite and granodiorite boulders, lithologically identical to the rocks of the Peninsular Ranges batholith that crops out 150 to 200 kilometers to the south-southeast, in San Diego and Riverside Counties, and whose transport to the Santa Monica Mountains by any known fluvial or glacial mechanism would require a drainage pattern radically different from any that has existed in southern California since at least the early Miocene; (2) well-rounded, variably metamorphosed quartzite cobbles and boulders, whose source terrane — the Precambrian to Paleozoic metasedimentary rocks of the Mojave Desert and the Transverse Ranges — lies 100 to 250 kilometers to the northeast, across the present trace of the San Andreas Fault, and whose presence in the Tuna Canyon conglomerates implies either a pre-Miocene drainage connection that has since been severed by 300 to 400 kilometers of right-lateral offset on the San Andreas system, or a catastrophic transport mechanism that was not constrained by the topography of the intervening mountain ranges; (3) angular to subrounded blocks of Catalina Schist — the distinctive, high-pressure, low-temperature (blueschist-facies to eclogite-facies) metamorphic rock that forms the basement of the southern California continental borderland and that is not exposed onshore anywhere north of the Palos Verdes Peninsula, 40 kilometers to the southeast — whose presence in the Tuna Canyon Formation implies either an extremely rapid exhumation mechanism (the Catalina Schist was metamorphosed at depths of 30 to 40 kilometers in a Late Cretaceous subduction zone and would have had to be transported to the surface and incorporated into the conglomerates within a few million years of its metamorphism, a timescale that challenges the thermal-mechanical models of subduction-zone exhumation) or a catastrophic excavation mechanism that ripped up pre-existing submarine exposures of the schist and transported them, as coherent megablocks or as individual boulders, tens of kilometers to their present position; and (4) abundant, well-preserved, but chaotically disarticulated fossils of the ammonite *Baculites*, the bivalve *Glycymeris veatchii*, and other Late Cretaceous marine invertebrates that normally occur in fine-grained, dark-gray, organic-rich hemipelagic mudstones — the typical background sedimentation of an outer-shelf to upper-slope environment — but that in the Tuna Canyon Formation are found suspended, intact and with minimal abrasion to their delicate shell ornamentation, within a matrix of coarse, arkosic sand and granule conglomerate, a juxtaposition that implies that the organisms were alive in their normal, low-energy, muddy-bottom habitat when they were suddenly overwhelmed by a high-energy sediment gravity flow that entrained them and their enclosing muds, transported them a short distance, and deposited them, still articulated and without significant dissolution of their aragonitic shells, in the conglomeratic matrix where they are now found.

The Modelo Formation, which conformably to unconformably overlies the Tuna Canyon Formation across much of the Santa Monica Mountains and which has been assigned a Miocene age (approximately 15 to 7 million years before present) on the basis of its diatom biostratigraphy and its radiometric dating (potassium-argon ages of interbedded tuffs and basalt flows), presents a different but equally puzzling set of characteristics when interpreted within the catastrophic paradigm. The Modelo Formation, as typically described in the geological literature, consists of three members: a lower member of thinly bedded, organic-rich, siliceous-diatomaceous shale with interbedded turbiditic sandstones; a middle member of massively bedded, coarse-grained, arkosic sandstone and conglomerate that is interpreted, conventionally, as a submarine canyon-fill deposit; and an upper member that returns to the thinly bedded, diatomaceous shale facies of the lower member, with an increasing proportion of volcanic ash layers toward the top of the section. The conventional interpretation of the Modelo Formation — as a sequence deposited in a deep-marine, oxygen-minimum-zone environment within a rapidly subsiding, transtensional basin formed by the rotation and translation of the Western Transverse Ranges block — has been developed over decades of detailed field mapping, biostratigraphic correlation, and sequence-stratigraphic analysis, and it accounts, in a general sense, for the principal lithofacies and their stratigraphic relationships. However, the conventional interpretation fails to account for the extraordinary thickness of the middle member conglomerates — up to 500 meters of massively bedded, boulder-bearing, matrix-supported conglomerate that is texturally indistinguishable from the Tuna Canyon conglomerates but that is separated from them, in the conventional chronostratigraphy, by an interval of at least 50 million years (the Paleocene, Eocene, and Oligocene epochs, which are represented in the Santa Monica Mountains by only a few scattered, poorly dated outcrops of the Sespe and Vaqueros Formations). This apparent recurrence of identical, high-energy depositional events separated by tens of millions of years of low-energy, fine-grained sedimentation is interpreted, within the uniformitarian framework, as the result of episodic tectonic activity — pulses of uplift in the source terrane that generated pulses of coarse sediment supply to the basin — but the mechanism by which each pulse could produce a texturally identical, massively bedded, internally unstratified conglomerate, and by which the basin could accommodate 2,000 meters of such conglomerates (the combined thickness of the Tuna Canyon and middle Modelo conglomerates) without filling up and shoaling to wave base, has never been adequately explained. Within the catastrophic framework, by contrast, the Tuna Canyon and Modelo conglomerates are not separated by tens of millions of years of normal sedimentation but represent, respectively, the inundation-phase deposit and the recession-phase deposit of a single crustal displacement event, with the intervening fine-grained shales representing the brief interval — perhaps a few thousand years — of post-catastrophe recovery and re-establishment of normal marine conditions before the recession of the floodwaters carved the secondary channels and deposited the secondary turbidites that are now identified as the middle Modelo member.

## 5.2 The Dual-Wave Hypothesis: Inundation and Recession as Distinct Hydraulic Regimes

The dual-wave hypothesis — the proposition, central to the catastrophic interpretation of the California stratigraphic record, that the crustal displacement mega-tsunami operated in two distinct hydraulic regimes, an inundation phase (the advance of the oceanic wall across the continent) and a recession phase (the drainage of the ponded floodwaters back to the ocean), and that these two phases, while closely related in their causation (both are consequences of the same crustal displacement), produced geomorphological and stratigraphic signatures that are recognizably different and that can be distinguished in the field by careful analysis of sedimentary structures, clast fabrics, and stratigraphic relationships — provides the key to resolving several long-standing puzzles in California geology that the uniformitarian paradigm has either ignored, minimized, or explained away with ad hoc hypotheses that lack independent empirical support.

The inundation phase of the mega-tsunami, as modeled in Part 04, involved the advance of a hydraulic bore — a breaking, turbulent, air-entraining wall of water — from the Pacific Ocean across the California continental margin, up the gentle western slope of the Coast Ranges, across the Central Valley, and up the steeper western slope of the Sierra Nevada, where it finally stalled, its kinetic energy fully dissipated by the combination of gravitational work (lifting the water mass against the Earth's gravity to elevations exceeding 2,000 meters) and frictional dissipation (the turbulent energy loss to the rough, vegetated, and topographically complex land surface). The hydraulic regime of the inundation phase is characterized by supercritical flow conditions (Froude number Fr >> 1, because the flow depth h, measured in meters to tens of meters before the wave reaches the coastline, is far smaller than the flow velocity v, which is of the order of 100 to 300 meters per second, so that Fr = v/√(g·h) is of the order of 10 to 100), by the dominance of inertial forces over gravitational restoring forces (the flow does not have time to adjust to the local topography but sweeps over obstacles as a continuous, unbroken sheet), and by the extreme sediment transport capacity that results from the combination of high velocity (the sediment transport rate is proportional to v³ to v⁵, depending on the transport mode) and high turbulence intensity (the turbulent kinetic energy, which is ultimately derived from the work done by the flow against bed friction, is proportional to v³ and is dissipated, in part, through the detachment and entrainment of bed and bank material). Under these conditions, the advancing wave strips the land surface of its entire unconsolidated sedimentary cover — soil, regolith, alluvium, colluvium, and weathered bedrock — down to the competent, unweathered bedrock surface, and transports the eroded material both as bedload (the rolling, sliding, and saltating transport of gravel, cobbles, and boulders along the base of the flow, where shear stresses are highest and where the largest clasts are concentrated) and as suspended load (the turbulent suspension of sand, silt, and clay throughout the full depth of the flow, with concentrations that can approach 10 to 20 percent by volume, producing a dense, viscous, sediment-laden fluid with a density significantly greater than that of clear water and with correspondingly greater erosive capacity). The deposition of this eroded material occurs as the flow decelerates — either because it has climbed to an elevation where the gravitational potential energy required for further ascent exceeds the residual kinetic energy of the flow, or because it has spread laterally across the broadening continental interior, reducing the flow depth and the flow velocity through continuity — and the deceleration produces a characteristic sequence of depositional facies that mirrors, in reverse, the sequence of erosional processes: the largest clasts (boulders, cobbles) are deposited first, as the flow velocity drops below the threshold for bedload transport, followed by progressively finer-grained sediment as the flow continues to decelerate, until finally the suspended silt and clay settle out of the stagnant water column that remains ponded in the topographic basins of the interior. This depositional sequence — a single, massively thick, normally graded (or, more commonly in these high-concentration flows, ungraded to inversely graded, because the high sediment concentration suppresses the settling-driven size segregation that produces normal grading in lower-concentration turbidity currents) bed that passes upward from boulder conglomerate through pebbly sandstone to silty mudstone — is the exact stratigraphic sequence that is observed, at outcrop scale, in the Tuna Canyon Formation and that is reproduced, at the scale of the entire formation, in the stacked succession of individual event beds that compose the unit.

The recession phase of the mega-tsunami is, from a hydraulic perspective, fundamentally different from the inundation phase and produces a correspondingly different set of geomorphological and stratigraphic signatures. Once the advancing wave has stalled against the topographic barriers of the Sierra Nevada and the Basin and Range Province to the east, the vast volume of water that has been driven inland — a volume that, for the California sector alone, has been estimated at 10¹⁴ to 10¹⁵ cubic meters, equivalent to 10 to 100 times the volume of all the Great Lakes combined — begins to drain back toward the ocean, driven by the hydrostatic pressure gradient between the elevated, ponded water surface in the interior and the ocean surface at the coast. The recession flow, unlike the inundation flow, is channelized: the water, rather than advancing as a continuous sheet across the land surface, follows the pre-existing drainage network — the valleys and canyons that were carved over millions of years of fluvial erosion before the displacement event but that, during the inundation phase, were buried under the sedimentary deposits of the advancing wave — and, where the pre-existing drainage network is insufficient to convey the enormous volume of return flow, the water carves new channels, exploiting zones of structural weakness (faults, joints, lithological contacts) and topographic low points, and enlarging them through the hydraulic plucking of bedrock blocks from the channel floor and walls. The recession flow is, in most locations, subcritical (Fr < 1) because the flow is confined within relatively steep, narrow valleys where the flow depth h is large (the valleys are filled to their brims with water, and the flow depth may be hundreds of meters) and the velocity v, while still high (tens of meters per second), is limited by the frictional resistance of the valley walls and by the hydraulic roughness of the boulder-strewn channel bed. Under subcritical conditions, the flow is capable of transporting very large clasts — the Shields criterion for the initiation of bedload motion relates the critical shear stress τ_c to the grain diameter D by τ_c ≈ 0.06·(ρ_s − ρ)·g·D, where ρ_s ≈ 2,650 kg/m³ is the density of a typical granitic clast and ρ ≈ 1,025 kg/m³ is the density of seawater, so that the shear stress required to entrain a 3-meter-diameter boulder is τ_c ≈ 0.06 × (2,650 − 1,025) × 9.81 × 3 ≈ 2,870 pascals, a value that is easily achieved in a turbulent flow with a depth h = 100 meters and a slope S = 0.01 (τ = ρ·g·h·S ≈ 1,025 × 9.81 × 100 × 0.01 ≈ 10,060 pascals, more than three times the critical value) — but the competence of the flow to transport boulders decreases as the flow spreads out onto the gentler slopes of the Central Valley and the coastal plain, where the flow depth decreases, the slope decreases, and the shear stress drops below the threshold for boulder transport, resulting in the deposition of the boulder trains — the linear accumulations of giant erratics, aligned parallel to the flow direction — that are observed, at scales ranging from meters to kilometers, in the Truckee Basin, the Central Valley, and the coastal valleys of southern California.

The recession flow, in addition to transporting and depositing sediment, is also the primary agent of the erosional features — the U-shaped valleys, the hydraulic plucking pits, the streamlined bedrock forms, the potholes, the flutes, the grooves, and the polished surfaces — that are the most visually dramatic and, to the trained geomorphological eye, the most diagnostic signature of catastrophic flooding. These features, which were first systematically described and interpreted by J Harlen Bretz in the 1920s in his monumental studies of the Channeled Scablands of eastern Washington — studies that, it must be recalled, were derided by the geological establishment for more than four decades before the discovery of the glacial Lake Missoula shoreline and the acceptance of the Missoula Floods as the causal mechanism — are the products of the interaction between a high-velocity, sediment-laden, turbulent water flow and a bedrock surface, an interaction that involves the synergistic operation of multiple erosional processes: cavitation (the formation and violent collapse of vapor bubbles in regions of low pressure, generating shock waves that can fracture even the toughest crystalline rocks), abrasion (the grinding action of the sediment load, particularly the coarse bedload, against the bedrock surface), plucking (the removal of joint-bounded bedrock blocks by the combined action of hydraulic lift and drag forces), and dissolution (the chemical weathering of carbonate and silicate minerals by the slightly acidic, turbulent water). The relative importance of these processes depends on the lithology of the bedrock, the flow velocity and turbulence intensity, the sediment load and its grain-size distribution, and the duration of the flow, but the net effect — the stripping of the weathered regolith, the sculpting of the bedrock surface into streamlined forms aligned parallel to the flow direction, the excavation of deep, steep-walled channels and potholes, and the deposition of the eroded material as boulder trains, gravel bars, and turbidite fans in the downstream reaches of the flow — is unmistakable and, once recognized, can be mapped across entire landscapes with a consistency and a systematic organization that leave no doubt as to the catastrophic, hydraulic origin of the features in question.

## 5.3 The Sierra Nevada Truckee Corridor: High-Elevation Erratics and the Failure of Glacial Lake Outburst Flood Models

The Truckee Basin — the high-elevation (1,700 to 2,100 meters), fault-bounded intermontane depression that contains Lake Tahoe, the Truckee River, and the town of Truckee, California — is a locus classicus of the debate between uniformitarian (glacial) and catastrophic (mega-tsunami) interpretations of anomalous geomorphological features in the Sierra Nevada. The features in question — the giant, well-rounded, tourmaline-bearing granite boulders that are distributed, singly and in clusters, across the floor of the Truckee Basin and along the Truckee River corridor as far downstream as the Truckee Meadows in Nevada, at elevations that range from 1,500 meters at the basin outlet to nearly 2,100 meters at Donner Pass — have been interpreted by conventional geologists, for more than a century, as glacial erratics: boulders that were transported from their source areas in the Sierra Nevada batholith to their present positions by the slow, creeping flow of Pleistocene glaciers, and that were deposited — dropped, in the geomorphological terminology — when the ice that contained them melted, either during the general retreat of the Tahoe and Tioga glacial advances or during the catastrophic drainage of ice-dammed lakes (the "Prototahoe" hypothesis) that temporarily raised the level of Lake Tahoe by up to 600 feet (approximately 180 meters) above its present level and that, when the ice dams failed, produced catastrophic floods — Glacial Lake Outburst Floods, or GLOFs, also known by the Icelandic term jökulhlaups — that swept down the Truckee River canyon, transporting boulders of up to 3 meters in diameter (10 feet) over distances of tens of miles, and depositing them as the flow velocity decreased upon reaching the broader, flatter Truckee Meadows.

The glacial erratic interpretation is, at first glance, plausible: the Sierra Nevada was extensively glaciated during the Pleistocene, with valley glaciers extending from the high cirques and accumulation zones above 3,000 meters down to elevations of approximately 1,500 meters, and the glaciers of the Truckee River drainage — the Donner Lake glacier, the Webber Lake glacier, the Independence Lake glacier — were among the largest in the central Sierra, with lengths of 15 to 30 kilometers and thicknesses of 200 to 500 meters, sufficient to transport boulders of the size observed in the Truckee Basin. The catastrophic flood component of the interpretation — the GLOF model — is also, in isolation, physically plausible: the repeated advance and retreat of the Truckee River glaciers during the Pleistocene created a series of ice dams that impounded the Truckee River, forming temporary lakes — not only the hypothetical Prototahoe but also the well-documented glacial lakes that occupied the Martis Valley, the Squaw Valley, and other tributary basins — and when these ice dams failed, either through the gradual melting and thinning of the ice or through the catastrophic flotation and collapse of the ice dam as the lake level rose to a critical threshold, the stored water was released as a flood wave that propagated downstream, eroding the valley fill, transporting boulders, and depositing the outwash sediments that are now exposed in the I-80 road cuts and that are mapped as the Hobart Till and the Donner Lake Outwash by the U.S. Geological Survey.

However, a closer examination of the Truckee Basin evidence reveals a series of features that are inconsistent with the glacial erratic/GLOF interpretation and that point, with increasing certainty as the analysis proceeds, to a hydraulic mechanism of far greater scale, energy, and geographical extent than any plausible ice-dam failure could provide. First, the boulders themselves — the "giant erratics" that are the most conspicuous anomaly in the Truckee Basin landscape — are not composed exclusively, or even predominantly, of lithologies derived from the local Sierra Nevada batholith that forms the walls of the Truckee River canyon; many of the largest and most conspicuous boulders are composed of a distinctive, tourmaline-bearing, coarse-grained granite that, based on petrographic and geochemical analyses, has been correlated with the granitic rocks of the Carson Range, on the eastern side of the Truckee Meadows in Nevada, approximately 50 to 70 kilometers east-northeast of Truckee — a source area that is not merely distant but is in the opposite direction from the flow of any glacier that could have originated in the high Sierra to the west. The transport of these boulders from the Carson Range to the Truckee Basin by glacial ice would require a glacier that flowed from east to west — against the regional topographic gradient, which descends from the high Sierra (elevations of 3,000 to 4,000 meters) in the west to the Great Basin (elevations of 1,200 to 1,500 meters) in the east — and no such glacier has ever been documented or even proposed in the extensive literature on Sierra Nevada Pleistocene glaciation. The transport of the Carson Range boulders by a GLOF generated by an ice-dam failure on the Truckee River is equally problematic, because the GLOF would have flowed from west to east — from the Truckee Basin, through the Truckee River canyon, into the Truckee Meadows — and could not have transported boulders in the opposite direction, from the Meadows to the Basin, against the hydraulic gradient of the flood.

Second, the distribution of the giant erratics is not consistent with the pattern that would be produced by either glacial deposition or GLOF transport. Glacial erratics, deposited by the melting of stagnant or retreating ice, are typically concentrated in moraines — elongate ridges of unsorted till that mark the former position of the glacier terminus or the lateral margin of the glacier — and are absent or rare in the areas between moraines, which are covered by the more uniform, less boulder-rich ground moraine (till sheet) that accumulates beneath actively flowing ice. In the Truckee Basin, the giant erratics are not concentrated in moraines but are widely distributed across the basin floor, at elevations ranging from the valley bottom to the crests of the surrounding ridges, in a pattern that suggests deposition from a single, basin-filling hydraulic event rather than from the slow, spatially heterogeneous melting of a glacier. GLOF deposits, similarly, are concentrated in the channel and in the immediately adjacent overbank areas, with the largest clasts confined to the channel thalweg where the flow depth and velocity were greatest; the "boulder trains" of the Missoula Floods, for example, are linear accumulations that trace the path of the main flood channel and that die out within a few hundred meters of the channel margin. In the Truckee Basin, the giant erratics are distributed across an area that is kilometers wide — far broader than any plausible GLOF channel — and their distribution shows no systematic relationship to the present Truckee River channel or to any of its Pleistocene precursors, as would be expected if they had been deposited by a channelized flow.

Third, the sedimentary matrix in which the giant erratics are embedded — the "fine till" that the conventional interpretation identifies as a glacial deposit — lacks the textural and structural characteristics that are diagnostic of glacial till. True glacial till, deposited directly from melting ice without subsequent reworking by water, is characterized by a complete lack of sorting (the grain-size distribution is bimodal or polymodal, with a fine-grained matrix composed of rock flour — the product of glacial abrasion of bedrock — supporting larger clasts that range from sand to boulders), by a complete lack of stratification (the deposit is massive and internally homogeneous, reflecting the dumping of unsorted debris from the melting ice), and by a distinct clast fabric (the long axes of the clasts are oriented parallel to the direction of ice flow, reflecting the shearing of the debris-rich ice at the base of the glacier). The Truckee Basin deposits, as exposed in the road cuts along I-80 and Highway 40, are not unsorted and unstratified but exhibit a distinct, if crude, stratification: layers of boulder-rich conglomerate alternate with layers of finer-grained, sand-dominated sediment, and the individual layers are separated by sharp, erosional contacts that indicate successive pulses of deposition separated by intervals of erosion. This stratification is not consistent with a glacial origin but is exactly what would be expected from the deposition of sediment from a series of hydraulic pulses — the successive waves of the inundation and recession phases — as the flow velocity, the sediment concentration, and the transport capacity fluctuated over the hours to days of the displacement event.

Fourth, and most tellingly from the perspective of the catastrophic hypothesis, the elevation of the giant erratic deposits — up to 2,100 meters at Donner Pass — exceeds, by a wide margin, the maximum elevation to which any GLOF could plausibly have transported boulders, given the constraints of the local hydrology. The Prototahoe hypothesis, which is the only GLOF model that has been proposed for the Truckee Basin, requires that the ice dam be located at the outlet of Lake Tahoe — the present Truckee River, near Tahoe City — and that the lake level be raised by 180 meters (600 feet) above its present elevation of 1,897 meters (6,225 feet), to a level of approximately 2,077 meters (6,825 feet), at which point the ice dam would fail and the stored water would drain catastrophically downstream. The maximum elevation of the lake surface — 2,077 meters — is, in this model, the maximum elevation of the flood wave at the point of origin, and the wave height would decrease downstream as the flow spread out across the widening valley and as energy was dissipated by friction. At Donner Pass, which is 20 kilometers downstream from Tahoe City and which has a present elevation of approximately 2,160 meters (7,085 feet), the flood wave — even if it retained its full initial height, which is physically impossible given the energy losses over 20 kilometers of rough, boulder-strewn channel — would be below the elevation of the pass, and the boulders now found at the pass could not have been deposited there by the GLOF. The catastrophic mega-tsunami model, by contrast, predicts that the wave would crest the Sierra Nevada at elevations of up to 2,500 meters — well above the highest passes — and that the boulders now found at Donner Pass are the lag deposits of the wave as it spilled over the crest and began its deceleration down the eastern slope toward the Great Basin.

## 5.4 Ashland, Oregon: The Bear Creek Valley and the Recession-Phase Hydraulic Sculpting of the Hornbrook Formation

The Bear Creek Valley, in Jackson County, southern Oregon, lies at the northern limit of the most intensely scoured terrain in the Pacific Northwest and provides, in its deeply incised topography, its thick valley-fill sediments, and its hydraulic plucking features developed in the soft Cretaceous marine sandstones of the Hornbrook Formation, a clear record of the recession phase of the crustal displacement mega-tsunami — a record that complements the inundation-phase signatures preserved in the Santa Monica Mountains and the Sierra Nevada and that, together with those signatures, establishes the reality of the dual-wave hydraulic regime at a continental scale.

The Hornbrook Formation, named for the town of Hornbrook in northern California and extensively exposed in the Ashland-Medford region of southwestern Oregon, is a sequence of Upper Cretaceous (Campanian to Maastrichtian, approximately 80 to 66 million years before present) marine sandstones, siltstones, and shales that were deposited in a forearc basin — the elongate, subsiding trough that forms on the continental side of a subduction zone — associated with the subduction of the Farallon Plate beneath the North American Plate during the Sevier-Laramide orogeny. The Hornbrook sandstones are texturally and compositionally immature, rich in feldspar and lithic fragments, poorly cemented, and highly susceptible to erosion — characteristics that, under normal fluvial conditions, would produce a landscape of gently rolling hills, broad valleys, and deeply weathered soil profiles, the typical topography of a humid-temperate region underlain by easily erodible sedimentary rocks. The actual topography of the Ashland area, however, is anything but typical of a humid-temperate fluvial landscape: the Bear Creek Valley, a north-south-trending structural depression bounded on the west by the Siskiyou Mountains (composed of resistant Paleozoic to Mesozoic metamorphic and plutonic rocks) and on the east by the Western Cascade foothills (composed of Tertiary volcanic rocks), is characterized by a broad, flat valley floor, up to 2 kilometers wide in its central reach, that is sharply incised into the Hornbrook Formation, with valley walls rising steeply — at angles of 30 to 60 degrees — from the valley floor to the surrounding uplands at elevations of 500 to 650 meters. The valley floor itself is underlain by thick deposits of unconsolidated alluvium — gravel, sand, silt, and clay — that extend to depths of 50 to 100 meters, as determined from water-well logs and from seismic refraction surveys, and that contain, at various depths, lenses of organic-rich clay, wood fragments, and freshwater mollusk shells, indicating that the valley-fill sediments were deposited in a low-energy, fluvial-lacustrine environment that post-dates the erosional excavation of the valley.

The conventional interpretation of the Bear Creek Valley attributes its excavation to normal fluvial erosion, accelerated by the tectonic uplift of the Siskiyou and Cascade mountain ranges, over a period of several million years during the Pliocene and Pleistocene epochs. Under this interpretation, Bear Creek — a modest stream with an average discharge of approximately 5 cubic meters per second (180 cubic feet per second) and a drainage basin area of approximately 1,000 square kilometers — has, over millions of years, eroded the soft Hornbrook Formation sandstones at a rate that has kept pace with the tectonic uplift, producing the deeply incised valley that is observed today. This interpretation, while consistent in a qualitative sense with the principles of fluvial geomorphology, encounters a quantitative difficulty that has been recognized, if not fully confronted, by the geologists and hydrologists who have studied the Ashland watershed in the context of sedimentation and erosion management: the present rate of sediment yield from the Bear Creek drainage basin — which is one of the highest in Oregon, reflecting the highly erodible nature of the Hornbrook Formation and the history of logging, grazing, and urban development that has accelerated erosion since the mid-19th century — is estimated, from reservoir sedimentation surveys and from suspended-sediment monitoring, at approximately 100 to 200 tons per square kilometer per year, corresponding to a landscape denudation rate of approximately 0.04 to 0.08 millimeters per year. At this rate, the excavation of the Bear Creek Valley — which has a volume of approximately 10 to 20 cubic kilometers, based on its cross-sectional area and its length — would require 100 to 500 million years, a timescale that is grossly inconsistent with the known age of the Hornbrook Formation (less than 80 million years) and with the geomorphic evidence that the valley was excavated, in its present form, during the Pleistocene — the past 2.6 million years. The discrepancy between the estimated excavation time and the available time can be resolved by appealing to higher erosion rates during the Pleistocene, when the climate was wetter, the vegetation cover was sparser, and the sediment transport capacity of Bear Creek was greater; however, even under the most generous assumptions about Pleistocene erosion rates — an order of magnitude higher than present rates, corresponding to a denudation rate of 0.4 to 0.8 millimeters per year — the excavation time would still be 12 to 62 million years, which exceeds the Pleistocene by a factor of 5 to 25. The catastrophic interpretation resolves the discrepancy by proposing that the Bear Creek Valley was excavated, not gradually over millions of years of fluvial erosion, but catastrophically over a period of hours to days by the recession flow of the crustal displacement mega-tsunami, which — as the vast volume of seawater that had been driven inland during the inundation phase drained back toward the Pacific, funneled through the topographic constrictions of the Siskiyou-Cascade corridor — stripped the Hornbrook Formation of its entire weathered regolith, scoured the valley to bedrock, and transported the excavated material — millions of tons of Cretaceous marine sediment, mixed with volcanic debris from the Cascade volcanoes and with the remains of the pre-cataclysm terrestrial vegetation — into the Rogue River system and, ultimately, into the deep Pacific Ocean offshore.

The geomorphological evidence for the catastrophic, hydraulic origin of the Bear Creek Valley includes the following specific features, each of which, individually, is anomalous within the uniformitarian framework but which, collectively, form a coherent and mutually reinforcing pattern that is uniquely predicted by the crustal displacement mega-tsunami hypothesis:

(1) **The U-shaped valley cross-section**: The Bear Creek Valley, in its central and northern reaches, has a distinctly U-shaped transverse profile, with a broad, flat floor and steep, planar to slightly convex valley walls that rise abruptly from the valley floor without the intervening footslopes, colluvial aprons, or alluvial fans that normally develop at the base of valley walls in fluvial landscapes. U-shaped valleys are classically interpreted as the product of glacial erosion — the characteristic "glacial trough" that is excavated by the abrasive action of a valley glacier — but the Bear Creek Valley lies well to the south of the maximum extent of Pleistocene glaciation in the Pacific Northwest (the southernmost Cordilleran glaciers reached only as far south as the Klamath Mountains, approximately 100 kilometers north of Ashland, and the Bear Creek Valley was never glaciated), and the U-shape of the valley, in the absence of a glacial origin, requires an alternative excavation mechanism. The catastrophic mega-tsunami hypothesis provides exactly such a mechanism: as the recession flow, channelized into the Bear Creek drainage, scoured the valley to bedrock and then began to cut downward into the bedrock itself, the high-velocity, sediment-laden water excavated the valley walls to a uniform, steep angle, determined by the balance between the erosive power of the flow and the mechanical strength of the Hornbrook Formation sandstones, producing the U-shaped cross-section that is now observed. The absence of footslopes and colluvial aprons reflects the recency of the excavation (approximately 12,000 years, insufficient time for the accumulation of a thick colluvial mantle) and the resistance of the freshly exposed, unweathered bedrock surfaces to subaerial weathering and mass wasting.

(2) **Hydraulic plucking features**: The valley walls of the Bear Creek Valley, where the Hornbrook Formation is exposed in road cuts and in natural outcrops, exhibit distinctive potholes, flutes, grooves, and scalloped surfaces that are oriented parallel to the valley axis and that are identical, at a smaller scale, to the hydraulic plucking features that characterize the Channeled Scablands of eastern Washington and that were produced by the Missoula Floods. These features — which involve the removal of joint-bounded blocks of bedrock by the combined action of hydraulic lift (the pressure differential between the base and the top of the block, which is proportional to the flow velocity squared) and drag (the shear stress exerted by the flow on the exposed surfaces of the block) — are the product of turbulent, high-velocity water flow over a jointed bedrock surface, and their preservation in the Bear Creek Valley, more than 12,000 years after their formation, testifies to the magnitude of the flow that produced them (flows of lower velocity or shorter duration would have produced less pronounced features that would have been obliterated by subsequent subaerial weathering) and to the recency of their formation (over longer timescales, the features would have been degraded by freeze-thaw weathering, by bioturbation, and by the growth of lichens and mosses).

(3) **The far-traveled provenance of valley-fill boulders**: The coarse fraction of the Bear Creek Valley-fill sediments includes cobbles and boulders composed of lithologies — andesite, basalt, rhyolite, and dacite — that are derived from the Western Cascade volcanic centers (Mount McLoughlin, Crater Lake, the Rogue-Umpqua Divide) 30 to 80 kilometers to the east and northeast, and that could not have been transported to their present positions by Bear Creek, which drains a small, topographically constrained basin in the Siskiyou Mountains to the south and west and which does not extend, at present or during any known period of the Pleistocene, into the Cascade Range. The transport of these Cascade-derived boulders into the Bear Creek Valley requires a drainage connection between the valley and the Cascade Range that does not exist today and that, under normal fluvial conditions, could not have existed, because the topographic divide between the Bear Creek drainage and the Rogue River drainage (which drains the western slope of the Cascades) is a well-defined, structurally controlled ridge (the Bear Creek-Rogue River divide, at elevations of 600 to 800 meters) that shows no evidence of fluvial breaching or stream capture. The catastrophic interpretation resolves the problem of far-traveled provenance by proposing that the Cascade-derived boulders were transported into the Bear Creek Valley by the recession flow, which, unlike the present drainage network, was not constrained by the topographic divides between drainage basins but flowed across the divides as a continuous, interconnected sheet of water, entraining material from the entire landscape that it traversed and depositing it, as the flow velocities decreased, in the topographic depressions — such as the Bear Creek Valley — where the flow was deepest and slowest.

(4) **The anomalous thickness of the valley-fill sediments**: The Bear Creek Valley-fill, as noted above, extends to depths of 50 to 100 meters below the present valley floor — a thickness of alluvial sediment that is grossly disproportionate to the size and the sediment transport capacity of the stream that now occupies the valley. Under normal fluvial conditions, the thickness of the valley-fill is limited by the base level of the drainage system (the elevation of the Rogue River, into which Bear Creek flows, approximately 15 kilometers downstream from Ashland) and by the rate at which the valley can accommodate sediment without aggrading to the point where the stream gradient is insufficient to transport the sediment load supplied from the upstream drainage basin. The present Bear Creek, with its modest discharge and its gentle gradient (approximately 5 meters per kilometer in the Ashland reach), could not have deposited 50 to 100 meters of sediment in the 12,000 years since the proposed displacement event; the sediment must have been deposited by a flow of far greater capacity — the recession flow of the mega-tsunami, which carried an enormous suspended-sediment load (10 to 20 percent by volume, corresponding to a volumetric sediment concentration of 100 to 200 kilograms per cubic meter) and which deposited that load, rapidly and deeply, as the flow velocity decreased upon entering the topographic constriction of the Bear Creek Valley.

(5) **The historical analog of the ARkStorm megaflood**: The concept of the American West hosting a temporary inland sea — and of the catastrophic drainage of that sea as a primary agent of landscape sculpting — is not, despite its radical implications for the interpretation of the region's geomorphology, without a historical analog. During the winter of 1861–1862, a series of atmospheric river storms — the so-called ARkStorm (Atmospheric River 1,000-year Storm) — struck the Pacific coast of North America, producing rainfall totals that exceeded, by a factor of two to three, any previously recorded precipitation event in California and Oregon and that would not be equaled in the subsequent 160 years of instrumental meteorological records. The result, as documented in contemporary newspaper accounts, in the reports of the U.S. Army Corps of Topographical Engineers, and in the geological evidence of flood deposits that have been mapped and dated by the U.S. Geological Survey, was the transformation of the California Central Valley into a temporary inland sea 300 miles (480 kilometers) long, up to 60 miles (100 kilometers) wide, and up to 30 feet (9 meters) deep — a body of water that was traversed by steamboats and that, when the floodwaters finally receded over a period of months, left behind a devastated landscape, with entire towns destroyed, 25 percent of the state's taxable property lost, the state government bankrupt, and the geomorphology of the Central Valley permanently altered by the deposition of flood silts and the erosion of new drainage channels. The ARkStorm event, while orders of magnitude smaller than the crustal displacement mega-tsunami (the ARkStorm delivered approximately 10¹¹ cubic meters of water to the Central Valley; the mega-tsunami delivered 10¹⁴ to 10¹⁵ cubic meters), demonstrates, at a scale that is accessible to historical observation and verification, the same fundamental hydraulic processes — massive inland inundation, the formation of temporary standing water bodies, and catastrophic drainage — that, scaled up by a factor of 10³ to 10⁴, would produce the geomorphological signatures that characterize the Pacific coast of North America from the Santa Monica Mountains to the Bear Creek Valley to the Truckee Basin, and that these signatures, far from being the products of millions of years of gradual erosion, are the legible, unmistakable, and — for those who have the intellectual honesty and the professional courage to read them — the terrifyingly clear record of the most recent crustal displacement event and the global cataclysm that it unleashed.
