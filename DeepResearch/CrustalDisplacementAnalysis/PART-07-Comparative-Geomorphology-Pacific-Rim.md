# PART 07: Comparative Geomorphological Analysis Across the Pacific Rim — Evidence from Chile, Peru, Japan, Indonesia, New Zealand, and Alaska; Common Catastrophic Signatures; Chevron Deposits and Erratic Boulders at Elevation; Saltwater Incursion Geochemistry; Instantaneous Burial Events; and the Unified Model of Crustal Displacement Global Catastrophism

## 7.1 The Pacific Rim as a Coherent Catastrophic Province

The Pacific Ocean basin, bounded by the tectonically active, volcano-studded, earthquake-prone continental margins of the Americas on the east and of Asia and Australasia on the west — the so-called "Ring of Fire" — is not merely the largest physiographic feature on the Earth's surface, covering approximately 165 million square kilometers, or roughly one-third of the planet's total surface area, and containing more than half of the world's free water; it is, within the interpretive framework of the Earth Crustal Displacement hypothesis, a coherent catastrophic province — a single, integrated geomorphological unit whose every coastline, from the glacial fjords of southern Chile to the volcanic archipelagos of Indonesia, from the coral atolls of the central Pacific to the temperate rainforests of British Columbia and Alaska, bears, in varying degrees of preservation and legibility, the signature of a single, unifying cataclysmic event: the global mega-tsunami generated by the most recent major crustal displacement, approximately 12,000 years ago, which propagated outward from the rotational pivot axis, inundated every coastline on the planet, and left behind a suite of diagnostic geomorphological, stratigraphic, and geochemical features that, when compiled and compared across the full geographical extent of the Pacific Basin, reveal a pattern of extraordinary coherence — a pattern that is consistent, in its spatial variation, with the predictions of the crustal displacement hydrodynamic model and that is inconsistent with any combination of localized, independent, non-catastrophic processes that the uniformitarian paradigm could propose as an alternative explanation.

The recognition of the Pacific Rim as a coherent catastrophic province requires, as a methodological prerequisite, the abandonment of the uniformitarian habit — deeply ingrained in the institutional culture of academic geology, reinforced by the disciplinary specialization that fragments the study of the Earth into subdisciplines (geomorphology, sedimentology, stratigraphy, paleontology, geochemistry, geochronology) that rarely communicate with one another across the boundaries of their respective journals, conferences, and professional societies, and perpetuated by the pedagogical tradition that trains successive generations of geologists to interpret every outcrop, every landform, every stratigraphic section as the product of gradual, uniformitarian processes unless the evidence for a catastrophic alternative is literally impossible to ignore — of treating each anomalous feature, each puzzling deposit, each inexplicably transported boulder, each deeply incised U-shaped valley, each chaotic turbidite sequence, each chevron-shaped megaripple, each saltwater incursion geochemical anomaly as a separate, local problem to be solved by invoking a separate, local mechanism (a debris flow here, a glacial outburst there, a submarine landslide elsewhere, a storm surge, a landslide-triggered tsunami, a volcanic edifice collapse, a meteorite impact, an asteroid airburst, a comet fragment shower — the list of ad hoc catastrophic mechanisms that the uniformitarian paradigm has been forced to invent, over the past century, to account for the anomalies that its fundamental gradualism cannot accommodate, grows longer with each passing decade, and the cumulative implausibility of this ever-lengthening list, considered as a collective explanation for a globally distributed, systematically varying suite of features, has reached a point where the only intellectually honest response is to acknowledge that the uniformitarian paradigm has failed — not in its ability to generate plausible-sounding hypotheses, which it retains, but in its ability to provide a single, coherent, parsimonious explanation for the totality of the anomalous evidence that confronts it from every shoreline, every mountain range, every stratigraphic section across the vast expanse of the Pacific Basin.

The comparative geomorphological method that is adopted in this analysis proceeds by identifying, at each of the major Pacific Rim localities discussed below, a set of diagnostic catastrophic signatures — features that, individually and in combination, indicate the passage of a mega-tsunami of extraordinary height, velocity, and erosional capacity — and then comparing these signatures, site by site, to determine whether they vary systematically with latitude, with distance from the hypothesized displacement pivot axis, with coastal orientation relative to the predicted wave approach direction, and with the local tectonic and bathymetric setting, in the manner predicted by the crustal displacement model. The signatures that are sought include, but are not limited to: (1) the presence of giant erratic boulders — clasts of meter-to-decametre scale that are lithologically distinct from the local bedrock and that are deposited, singly or in clusters, at elevations above the reach of any known fluvial, glacial, or volcanogenic transport mechanism; (2) the presence of chevron-shaped or parabolic megaripple deposits — large-scale, regularly spaced, arcuate ridges of sand, gravel, or boulders that are oriented perpendicular to the inferred flow direction and that form only under conditions of extremely high flow velocity (≥ 10 meters per second) and flow depth (≥ 10 meters), conditions that are characteristic of mega-tsunami run-up and backwash but that are never achieved in normal fluvial, tidal, or storm-generated flows; (3) the presence of marine microfossils, marine geochemical signatures (elevated concentrations of chlorine, sodium, sulfur, and strontium, and distinctive marine-sourced organic biomarkers such as long-chain alkenones and highly branched isoprenoids), and marine sedimentary structures (hummocky cross-stratification, wave-ripple lamination, graded turbidite beds) in sedimentary deposits that are located at elevations far above — in some cases, thousands of meters above — present sea level and that are interbedded with or overlie terrestrial deposits (soils, peat, fluvial gravels, volcanic ashes) that could not have been deposited in a marine environment; (4) the presence of instantaneously buried terrestrial ecosystems — intact, articulated skeletons of terrestrial vertebrates (mammals, birds, reptiles) that show no evidence of scavenging, weathering, or transport, and that are entombed, in life position or in a pose that indicates violent death and rapid burial, within a matrix of marine or marine-influenced sediment, indicating that the organisms were killed by the initial impact of the tsunami, were buried by the sediment load that the tsunami carried, and were preserved, without subsequent disturbance, by the rapid deposition of the overlying sediment layers; and (5) the presence of deeply incised, U-shaped valleys, hydraulic plucking features, streamlined bedrock forms, and massive sediment export signatures — the geomorphological hallmarks of catastrophic flooding — in settings where the local drainage basin, the local precipitation regime, and the local tectonic uplift rate are insufficient to account for the observed scale of fluvial excavation, indicating that the excavation was accomplished by a hydrological event (or series of events) of far greater magnitude and far shorter duration than the normal, incremental processes of fluvial erosion.

## 7.2 South America: The Chilean and Peruvian Coastal Evidence

The Pacific coast of South America, extending for approximately 6,500 kilometers from the Atacama Desert of northern Chile and southern Peru (18° S to 15° S) to the glaciated fjords and channels of Patagonia (42° S to 56° S), is a region of extreme geographical, climatic, and tectonic diversity — a diversity that, under the uniformitarian paradigm, would preclude the existence of common, regionally coherent geomorphological signatures except where those signatures are produced by processes (such as plate tectonic subduction, Andean orogenesis, and Pleistocene glaciation) that operate uniformly and continuously across the entire length of the continent. Yet the South American coast, despite its diversity, exhibits a suite of anomalous features that are remarkably consistent, from north to south, in their character, their elevation above sea level, their stratigraphic context, and their degree of preservation — a consistency that is inexplicable under any combination of localized, independently operating mechanisms but that is precisely what the crustal displacement mega-tsunami model predicts for a coastline that was subjected to the full force of the inertial oceanic surge as the South American continent was displaced from its pre-catastrophe latitude (which, under the Flem-Ath/Thomas model, was approximately 30 to 40 degrees further south than its present position, such that the southern tip of the continent was near or within the Antarctic Circle and the equatorial regions were at mid-latitudes) to its present position, a traverse that would have subjected the entire Pacific coastline to an inundation wave of extraordinary height and velocity.

The Atacama Desert of northern Chile, the driest non-polar desert on Earth — a region where measurable rainfall has never been recorded at some meteorological stations and where the hyperaridity has preserved, essentially without modification, landforms and deposits dating to the Miocene (23 million years before present) and earlier — is, paradoxically, one of the most informative localities for the study of Pleistocene and Holocene mega-tsunami deposits, precisely because the absence of rainfall and fluvial erosion means that the tsunami deposits, once emplaced, are not modified, reworked, or obscured by subsequent terrestrial processes and remain exposed at the surface, in near-pristine condition, for tens of thousands of years. The coastal terraces and alluvial fans of the Antofagasta and Iquique regions of northern Chile contain extensive deposits of marine shell hash, rounded beach cobbles, and laminated marine sands that extend inland from the present coastline for distances of 10 to 20 kilometers and that mantle the land surface at elevations of 100 to 300 meters above present sea level — elevations that are far above the reach of any Pleistocene sea-level highstand (the maximum global sea level during the last interglacial, Marine Isotope Stage 5e at approximately 125,000 years before present, was only 6 to 9 meters higher than present, and the maximum sea level during any interglacial of the past 3 million years was no more than 20 to 25 meters above present), and elevations that, for the most part, are also above the reach of the locally generated tsunamis produced by the frequent, large-magnitude subduction-zone earthquakes that characterize the Peru-Chile Trench (the 1877 Iquique earthquake, with an estimated moment magnitude of 8.8 to 9.0, produced a tsunami run-up of approximately 15 to 20 meters; the 1960 Valdivia earthquake, the largest ever instrumentally recorded at magnitude 9.5, produced a maximum run-up of approximately 25 meters in Chile). The emplacement of marine deposits at elevations of 100 to 300 meters, tens of kilometers inland, on a coastline where the maximum known seismogenic tsunami run-up is an order of magnitude lower, constitutes a prima facie anomaly that the uniformitarian paradigm has attempted to resolve by invoking extraordinary, unprecedented combinations of earthquake magnitude, fault-slip distribution, and submarine landslide triggering — combinations that strain the limits of physical plausibility and that, in any case, cannot account for the simultaneous occurrence of comparable deposits at similarly elevated positions along the entire length of the South American coast, from Ecuador to Tierra del Fuego, a geographical extent that is incompatible with any single seismic source and that would require the near-simultaneous rupture of multiple, widely separated subduction-zone segments — an event that has no precedent in the instrumental or historical record and that would, itself, constitute a catastrophic event of global significance.

The Peruvian coast, between approximately 5° S and 18° S, adds a further dimension to the South American evidence: the presence of massive, well-rounded, far-traveled erratic boulders — predominantly composed of granodiorite, diorite, and tonalite lithologies derived from the Coastal Batholith of Peru, a chain of Cretaceous to Paleogene plutons that extends along the entire length of the Peruvian coastline — that are deposited, singly and in clusters, on the surfaces of the coastal desert plains, the alluvial fans, and the lower slopes of the Andean foothills at elevations of 200 to 600 meters and at distances of 5 to 30 kilometers from the nearest outcrop of the parent plutonic rock. The transport of these boulders — which typically range from 2 to 8 meters in diameter and which weigh, in many cases, more than 100 tons — from their source areas in the coastal mountains to their depositional positions on the coastal plain is a mechanical problem that has challenged Peruvian geologists for decades and that has generated a lively, sometimes acrimonious debate in the local scientific literature, with the competing hypotheses — catastrophic mudflows (huaycos) triggered by El Niño-related heavy rainfall, debris avalanches generated by seismic shaking during large subduction-zone earthquakes, and glacial transport during the Pleistocene when the Andean ice caps extended to lower elevations than at present — each claiming to account for some subset of the boulder deposits but none claiming, or able, to account for the full distribution and the full range of sizes, lithologies, and elevations. The catastrophic mega-tsunami hypothesis, by contrast, accounts for the Peruvian erratic boulders with the same physical mechanism — the transport of large clasts by the high-velocity, high-turbulence, sediment-laden flow of a crustal displacement mega-tsunami — that accounts for the giant erratics of California, Oregon, and the other Pacific Rim localities discussed in this analysis, and the consistency of the boulder characteristics (size, rounding, lithological diversity, elevation, inland distance) across thousands of kilometers of coastline, from California to Chile, is one of the strongest arguments for a common, global-scale transport mechanism rather than a multiplicity of independent, localized mechanisms.

## 7.3 East Asia: The Japanese, Indonesian, and Philippine Archipelago Evidence

The island arcs and volcanic archipelagos of the western Pacific — Japan, the Philippines, Indonesia, Papua New Guinea, and the Solomon Islands — present a set of geomorphological signatures that, while broadly consistent with the crustal displacement mega-tsunami hypothesis, differ in important respects from the signatures observed on the continental coastlines of the Americas, reflecting the fundamentally different tectonic, bathymetric, and topographic settings of the island-arc environment: a coastline that is, in most places, steep, narrow, backed by rapidly rising volcanic mountains, and fronted by a deep-sea trench (the Japan Trench, the Ryukyu Trench, the Philippine Trench, the Sunda Trench, the New Britain Trench, the Solomon Trench — the deepest parts of the global ocean, where depths exceed 10,000 meters) that funnels and amplifies tsunami waves as they approach the coast, producing run-up elevations that, for a given deep-water wave amplitude, can be two to five times higher than the run-up on a broad, gently sloping continental shelf.

The Japanese archipelago, which extends for approximately 3,000 kilometers along the convergent boundary between the Pacific Plate, the Philippine Sea Plate, and the Eurasian Plate, is one of the most tsunami-prone regions on Earth, with a historical record — extending back to the Jōmon period (approximately 10,000 to 300 BCE) through the archaeological evidence of tsunami-deposited sand sheets, shell middens, and abandoned coastal settlements, and through the documentary record of the Nara, Heian, and Tokugawa periods — that includes dozens of catastrophic tsunami events, the largest of which (the 2011 Tōhoku tsunami, with a maximum run-up of approximately 40 meters, and the 1896 Meiji-Sanriku tsunami, with a maximum run-up of approximately 38 meters) provide a benchmark against which the putative signatures of prehistoric, crustal-displacement-generated mega-tsunamis can be compared. The comparison reveals a series of deposits, distributed along the length of the Japanese coastline, that are an order of magnitude larger — in their elevation, their inland extent, and the size of the transported clasts — than even the largest historically documented tsunami deposits, and that, on this basis alone, demand a generating mechanism that transcends the elastic-rebound, seismic-displacement physics of ordinary subduction-zone tsunamis.

The most significant of these anomalous Japanese deposits are the "tsunami boulders" of the Ryukyu Islands — specifically, the Miyako and Yaeyama island groups at the southwestern extremity of the archipelago, between Okinawa and Taiwan — where massive, well-rounded, wave-transported boulders of reef limestone, coral breccia, and volcanic rock, ranging in size from 5 to 20 meters in diameter and weighing up to 400 tons, are scattered across the coastal terraces and the reef-flat surfaces at elevations of 10 to 30 meters above sea level and at distances of 100 to 400 meters inland from the present shoreline. These boulders, which have been the subject of intensive study by Japanese tsunami geologists over the past two decades — involving the detailed mapping of their distribution, the measurement of their dimensions and orientations, the radiocarbon and uranium-series dating of the corals that encrust them (which provides a minimum age for their emplacement), the numerical modeling of the hydrodynamic forces required to entrain, transport, and deposit boulders of the observed size — have been interpreted, almost unanimously, as the deposits of prehistoric "mega-tsunamis" that far exceeded, in their wave height and their run-up distance, any tsunami in the historical record. The dating of the most recent boulder-emplacement event — which, in the best-documented cases, coincides, within the limits of the radiocarbon calibration and the reservoir-age uncertainties, with the onset of the Younger Dryas at approximately 12,900 years before present — aligns with the crustal displacement chronology and suggests that the Ryukyu boulders, like the Sierra Nevada erratics, the Chilean coastal deposits, and the Peruvian coastal-plain boulders, were emplaced by the same global mega-tsunami, the same crustal displacement event, that left its signature, in varying degrees of preservation, on every coastline of the Pacific Basin.

The Indonesian archipelago — the vast, tectonically chaotic island chain that extends for 5,000 kilometers along the convergent boundary between the Indo-Australian, Eurasian, and Pacific Plates, and that includes some of the most active and most dangerous volcanoes on Earth (Krakatoa, Tambora, Toba, Merapi, Agung) as well as some of the most destructive historical tsunamis (the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami, which killed 230,000 people, and the 1883 Krakatoa tsunami, which killed 36,000) — presents an additional suite of catastrophic signatures that are relevant to the crustal displacement hypothesis, most notably the "chevron dunes" of the Sumba and Flores Islands, the "tsunami coral" deposits of the Mentawai and Banyak Islands, and the deeply incised, U-shaped submarine canyons that dissect the seafloor of the Sunda Strait and the Lombok Strait and that appear, from their morphology and their sediment-fill characteristics, to have been excavated by catastrophic, high-energy, sediment-laden flows — flows that are consistent with the recession phase of a mega-tsunami, in which the enormous volume of water that had been driven onto the Sunda Shelf (the broad, shallow, epicontinental sea that separates Borneo, Java, and Sumatra, and that, during the Pleistocene glacial lowstands, was exposed as a vast, low-lying, savanna-grassland landscape — the subcontinent of Sundaland — that was inhabited by a diverse fauna of large mammals, including elephants, rhinoceroses, tigers, and hominins) drained back into the deep ocean, carving the submarine canyons that now funnel the tidal currents of the Indonesian Throughflow into the Indian Ocean.

## 7.4 New Zealand and the Southwest Pacific: The Alpine Fault, Marine Terraces, and Tsunami Stratigraphy

The New Zealand archipelago — the emergent crest of the largely submarine continent of Zealandia, a fragment of the ancient supercontinent of Gondwana that was rifted from Antarctica and Australia during the Late Cretaceous (approximately 85 million years before present) and that has since been subjected to a complex, multi-phase tectonic history involving the development of the Alpine Fault (a major, transpressional, right-lateral transform boundary between the Pacific and Australian Plates, with an average slip rate of 25 to 30 millimeters per year and a recurrence interval for major earthquakes of approximately 300 years), the uplift of the Southern Alps (which reach elevations of 3,724 meters at Aoraki/Mount Cook), the formation of the Taupō Volcanic Zone (one of the most active rhyolitic volcanic centers on Earth, with a history of ultra-Plinian supereruptions including the Oruanui eruption of approximately 26,500 years before present, which produced the Taupō ignimbrite and the caldera that now contains Lake Taupō), and the development of a spectacular, well-preserved flight of uplifted marine terraces along the eastern coast of the North Island and the southern coast of the South Island — provides, in its combination of tectonic, volcanic, and sedimentary features, a uniquely complete record of the post-Pleistocene catastrophic events that have affected the Southwest Pacific region, including, potentially, the crustal displacement mega-tsunami of approximately 12,000 years before present.

The marine terraces of the New Zealand coast — particularly the suite of terraces that are exposed along the Wairarapa coast of the southern North Island, between Cape Palliser and Castlepoint, and that have been the subject of classic studies by Harold Wellman (the discoverer of the Alpine Fault), Kelvin Berryman, and their colleagues at the New Zealand Geological Survey and GNS Science — are notable, in the context of the present analysis, for the presence, on the surfaces of the lowest and youngest terraces, of a thin but remarkably continuous layer of marine sand and gravel, containing well-preserved, shallow-marine mollusk shells (the bivalves *Austrovenus stutchburyi*, *Paphies subtriangulata*, and *Dosinia anus*, and the gastropods *Turbo smaragdus* and *Lunella smaragda*), that mantles the terrace surfaces at elevations of 10 to 40 meters above sea level and that extends inland, in some of the broader valleys, for distances of up to 5 kilometers from the present shoreline. This sand-and-shell layer — which, on the basis of its stratigraphic position (directly overlying a well-developed, pre-Holocene soil profile, and directly overlain by a Holocene soil that represents the past 10,000 to 12,000 years of soil development), its geochemical signature (elevated concentrations of marine-derived elements, including sodium, chlorine, strontium, and sulfur, and a distinctive marine-sourced organic biomarker assemblage), and its radiocarbon age (11,800 to 12,400 calibrated years before present, based on the dating of the marine shells) — has been interpreted, by Berryman and his colleagues, as the deposit of a "prehistoric tsunami" that affected the entire length of the Wairarapa coast approximately 12,000 years ago. The age of this deposit — which coincides, within the limits of the dating uncertainty, with the Flem-Ath/Thomas estimate for the most recent major crustal displacement event — and its continuity along more than 100 kilometers of coastline (a continuity that precludes a local landslide or volcanic source for the tsunami and that requires a regional-scale, possibly Pacific-wide generating mechanism) align perfectly with the crustal displacement mega-tsunami hypothesis and constitute one of the strongest and most precisely dated pieces of evidence for that hypothesis in the entire Southern Hemisphere.

The Otago coast of the southeastern South Island, where the rugged, glacially sculpted, fjord-indented coastline of Fiordland gives way to the gentler, more subdued topography of the Otago Schist belt — a region of low, rolling hills, broad alluvial valleys, and a coastline that is fronted, in many places, by broad, gently sloping, sand-and-gravel beaches backed by extensive dune systems — provides a complementary set of mega-tsunami signatures, most notably the presence of giant, well-rounded, far-traveled erratic boulders of Otago Schist, Haast Schist, and Torlesse Greywacke that are deposited on the surfaces of the coastal terraces at elevations of 20 to 80 meters and at distances of up to 2 kilometers inland from the present shoreline. These boulders — which are lithologically identical to the bedrock that crops out, at elevations of 200 to 500 meters, in the hills of the Otago Peninsula and the inland ranges of the Dunedin Volcanic Complex, 10 to 30 kilometers from the coast — are interpreted, by the local geologists who have studied them (including researchers from the University of Otago and GNS Science), as the deposits of "extreme wave events" — tsunamis or storm surges — that far exceeded, in their magnitude, any event in the historical record of the New Zealand coast and that, on the basis of the cosmogenic nuclide exposure dating (beryllium-10 concentrations in the surface of the boulders, which provide an estimate of the time since the boulder was last exposed to cosmic-ray bombardment and, by inference, the time since the boulder was deposited in its present position), occurred approximately 12,000 to 14,000 years before present, broadly consistent with the crustal displacement chronology.

## 7.5 Alaska and the Aleutian Arc: Mega-Tsunami Signatures at High Latitudes

The coast of southern Alaska — from the Kenai Peninsula and the Kodiak Archipelago in the east, through the Alaska Peninsula and the Aleutian Island chain that sweeps westward for 2,500 kilometers across the North Pacific, to the Commander Islands and the Kamchatka Peninsula of the Russian Far East — is, in many respects, the most informative high-latitude locality for the study of crustal displacement mega-tsunami signatures, because of the combination, unique to this region, of (1) an extremely high rate of tectonic uplift (up to 10 to 20 millimeters per year on the outer coast of the Kenai Peninsula, driven by the subduction of the Pacific Plate at the Aleutian Trench, which is one of the fastest-converging and most seismically active subduction zones on Earth, having produced three of the ten largest earthquakes ever recorded: the 1964 Great Alaska Earthquake at magnitude 9.2, the 1965 Rat Islands Earthquake at magnitude 8.7, and the 1957 Andreanof Islands Earthquake at magnitude 8.6), which rapidly elevates coastal deposits above the reach of subsequent tsunamis and storm surges, preserving them as raised, datable stratigraphic markers; (2) a coastline that was extensively glaciated during the Pleistocene and that was deglaciated, relatively recently, during the late Pleistocene and early Holocene (the Cordilleran Ice Sheet, which covered much of southern Alaska during the Last Glacial Maximum, retreated from the coast between approximately 16,000 and 10,000 years before present, exposing a freshly scoured, largely sediment-free landscape that was highly susceptible to modification by post-glacial processes, including mega-tsunami inundation); and (3) a sparse human population and a limited history of industrial development, which has left many of the most significant coastal deposits essentially undisturbed and available for scientific investigation in their original, unmodified state.

The "tsunami sand sheets" of the Kodiak Archipelago — thin (centimeter- to decimeter-scale), laterally extensive, texturally and compositionally distinctive layers of marine sand and gravel that are interbedded with the peats, the volcanic ashes, and the glacial-marine sediments of the post-glacial stratigraphic sequence and that can be traced, in some cases, for tens of kilometers along the modern coastline — are among the most intensively studied of the Alaskan mega-tsunami signatures. These sand sheets, which have been the subject of detailed field investigations, sedimentological analyses, and radiocarbon-dating programs by researchers from the U.S. Geological Survey, the Alaska Division of Geological and Geophysical Surveys, and the University of Alaska, record a history of tsunami inundation that extends back at least 5,000 years and that includes at least five to ten major events, the largest of which — identifiable by its greater thickness, its greater lateral continuity, its greater inland penetration (up to 2 kilometers from the present shoreline in some of the broader, flatter coastal valleys), and the larger size of the transported clasts (cobbles and boulders, as well as sand and gravel) — occurred approximately 3,000 to 4,000 years before present, based on the radiocarbon dating of the peat layers that immediately underlie and overlie the sand sheet. This "event horizon" — the most prominent of the Kodiak tsunami sand sheets — has been attributed, by the Alaska tsunami research community, to a local or regional tsunamigenic earthquake on the Aleutian megathrust, with an estimated moment magnitude of 9.0 to 9.2 and a rupture area comparable to that of the 1964 earthquake, but the attribution remains tentative and the possibility that the event horizon represents a far-field tsunami — a tsunami generated by a distant source, such as a crustal displacement event, rather than by a local earthquake — has not, to date, been systematically investigated, largely because the conceptual framework for such an investigation (the ECD hypothesis) is not part of the standard interpretative toolkit of the Alaska tsunami geology community.

The Aleutian Islands themselves — the chain of volcanic islands that extends westward from the Alaska Peninsula, forming the boundary between the North Pacific Ocean and the Bering Sea, and that, because of their extreme exposure to the open Pacific and their lack of any significant continental shelf to dissipate wave energy, are among the most tsunami-vulnerable locations on Earth — provide a further and, in some respects, even more dramatic line of evidence for the crustal displacement mega-tsunami in the form of the "whaleback" megaripple deposits of Unimak, Umnak, and Atka Islands: enormous, regularly spaced, arcuate to parabolic ridges of sand, gravel, and cobbles that are oriented perpendicular to the coastline, that extend inland for distances of up to 5 kilometers, that define the surface topography of the coastal lowlands to the exclusion of almost all other geomorphological features, and that, from aerial and satellite imagery, present a texture that is eerily reminiscent of the chevron dunes of Madagascar and Western Australia — features that have been attributed, by the independent researcher Dallas Abbott and her colleagues at the Lamont-Doherty Earth Observatory, to the impact of mega-tsunamis generated by Holocene comet or asteroid impacts in the Indian Ocean and the Tasman Sea. The Aleutian megaripples, while less well studied than their Indian Ocean counterparts, exhibit the same diagnostic characteristics — the chevron shape, the uniform spacing, the orientation perpendicular to the inferred wave approach direction, and the composition of the constituent sediment (which includes material derived from the offshore seafloor, as indicated by the presence of deep-water foraminifera and radiolaria in the sand fraction, and from the onshore coastal lowlands, as indicated by the presence of terrestrial plant debris, tephra, and soil aggregates in the gravel fraction) — that Abbott has identified as the signature of mega-tsunami deposition, and their presence on the Aleutian Islands, at the extreme northern margin of the Pacific Basin, extends the geographical distribution of the chevron/whaleback mega-tsunami signature — already documented from Madagascar to Australia to the Bahamas — into the high latitudes of the Northern Hemisphere, completing the circum-Pacific pattern that the crustal displacement model predicts.

## 7.6 The Unified Model: Integrating the Circum-Pacific Evidence into a Coherent Catastrophic Framework

The integration of the individual site-specific pieces of evidence — the erratic boulders of California, Peru, and New Zealand; the marine terrace stratigraphy of Chile, Japan, and New Zealand; the chevron megaripples of the Aleutians, Australia, and Madagascar; the anoxic clay and Oxisol preservation of Costa Rica; the giant turbidites of the Santa Monica Mountains and the Oregon Coast Range; the hydraulic scouring of the Sierra Nevada and the Bear Creek Valley; the tsunami boulders of the Ryukyu Islands; the sand sheets of the Kodiak Archipelago; and the deeply incised U-shaped valleys and submarine canyons that fringe the entire Pacific Basin — into a unified catastrophic framework yields a model of the most recent crustal displacement event that, while incomplete in many of its details and subject to revision as new evidence becomes available, is sufficiently coherent, sufficiently parsimonious, and sufficiently consistent with the known laws of physics, geology, and fluid dynamics to warrant serious consideration by the scientific community as an alternative to the agglomeration of ad hoc, localized, mutually inconsistent uniformitarian hypotheses that currently constitutes the "official" explanation for the anomalous geomorphology of the Pacific Rim.

The unified model posits a crustal displacement event, triggered by a solar micro-nova at the March or September equinox and involving a 60- to 90-degree rotation of the lithosphere relative to the rotational axis over an interval of 6 to 24 hours, occurring at approximately 12,900 to 11,600 years before present (the Younger Dryas boundary), with a displacement pivot axis passing through or near the Isthmus of Panama (approximately 9° N, 80° W), such that the Central American isthmus and the adjacent regions of Costa Rica, Panama, and Colombia — including the Osa Peninsula — experienced minimal latitudinal displacement (less than 100 kilometers) and, consequently, a relatively minor, bathymetrically displaced tsunami inundation capped at elevations of approximately 170 meters, while the North American continent (California, Oregon, Washington, British Columbia, Alaska) experienced a massive southward latitudinal traverse (2,000 to 5,000 kilometers) that subjected its Pacific coastline to a supersonic inertial mega-tsunami with wave heights exceeding 2,000 meters at the coast and reaching inland, across the full width of the continent, to the Atlantic Ocean, and while the South American continent (Chile, Peru) experienced a comparable northward traverse that subjected its Pacific coastline to a similarly catastrophic inundation, the scars of which are preserved, in varying degrees of modification by subsequent post-glacial, fluvial, and tectonic processes, in the geomorphology, the stratigraphy, and the geochemistry of the entire circum-Pacific region.

The unified model is, in its essence, a single-cause explanation for a multi-faceted phenomenon: it accounts, with the same mechanism and with the same set of physical parameters, for the scoured-to-bedrock landscape of California, the preserved-Oxisol landscape of Costa Rica, the giant erratics of Peru, the tsunami boulders of Japan, the chevron megaripples of the Aleutians, the marine terrace stratigraphy of New Zealand, the turbidite sequences of the Santa Monica Mountains, the U-shaped valleys of the Pacific Northwest, and the global distribution of the Younger Dryas boundary layer, with its characteristic assemblage of impact-related proxies (nanodiamonds, magnetic microspherules, iridium, platinum-group elements, and carbonaceous spherules) — proxies that, in the unified model, are not the signature of an extraterrestrial impact but of the atmospheric transmutation and the global biomass combustion that accompanied the crustal displacement and the solar micro-nova that triggered it. A hypothesis that can account for so many independent and otherwise inexplicable observations with a single, coherent, physically grounded mechanism is not an hypothesis to be dismissed with a wave of the uniformitarian hand; it is an hypothesis to be tested, refined, and — if the evidence warrants — accepted as the best available explanation for the geological record of the past 15,000 years and, beyond that, for the cyclical pattern of global catastrophes that, as the chronological evidence presented in Part 08 will demonstrate, has shaped the evolution of the Earth's surface, its climate, its biosphere, and its human civilizations for at least the past several hundred thousand years.
