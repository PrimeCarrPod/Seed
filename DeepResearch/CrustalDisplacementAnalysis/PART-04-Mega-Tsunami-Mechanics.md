# PART 04: Mega-Tsunami Mechanics — Inertial Fluid Dynamics, Equatorial Bulge Hydraulics, Wave Height Scaling Laws, Atmospheric Coupling, and Transcontinental Inundation Modeling of Crustal Displacement-Generated Oceanic Cataclysms

## 4.1 Conservation of Momentum and the Inertial Oceanic Response to Crustal Acceleration

The hydrodynamics of a crustal displacement-generated mega-tsunami are governed not by the conventional tsunami physics of seafloor displacement — in which a submarine earthquake, landslide, or volcanic edifice collapse generates a surface wave by vertically displacing the overlying water column, which then propagates outward as a shallow-water gravity wave with a wavelength of hundreds of kilometers, a wave height at sea of less than one meter, and a propagation velocity given by √(g·h), where g is the gravitational acceleration (9.81 m/s²) and h is the water depth — but by a fundamentally different physical regime in which the oceanic water mass, rather than being displaced vertically by a moving seafloor and then propagating under its own gravitational restoring force, is left behind by a rapidly accelerating lithosphere that slides beneath it, in accordance with the principle of conservation of linear and angular momentum. This distinction is crucial and is the source of the enormous discrepancy — several orders of magnitude — between the wave heights produced by even the largest seismogenic tsunamis (which rarely exceed 30 meters at the coast, and which reached an exceptional maximum of approximately 40 meters during the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami and 40 meters during the 2011 Tōhoku tsunami) and the wave heights predicted by the crustal displacement model (which range from hundreds of meters in regions proximate to the displacement axis to several thousand meters in regions that undergo the maximal latitudinal traverse). The conventional tsunami, however devastating its local effects, is fundamentally limited in its energy budget by the elastic strain energy that can be stored in the lithosphere prior to a seismic rupture — typically 10¹⁵ to 10¹⁸ joules for the largest megathrust earthquakes, corresponding to the energy released by the elastic rebound of a fault segment with a rupture area of 10³ to 10⁵ square kilometers and an average slip of 5 to 20 meters. The crustal displacement mega-tsunami, by contrast, draws its energy not from elastic strain release but from the kinetic energy of the rotating Earth itself — a reservoir of kinetic energy on the order of 10²⁹ joules (the rotational kinetic energy of the Earth is approximately 2.1 × 10²⁹ joules, or roughly 5 × 10¹³ megatons of TNT equivalent), of which a significant fraction — perhaps 10²³ to 10²⁵ joules, corresponding to the differential kinetic energy between the lithosphere and the hydrosphere during a 90-degree displacement — would be transferred to the oceans through the inertial coupling described below.

To develop a quantitative model of the inertial oceanic response to crustal acceleration, consider the Earth as a two-component system consisting of (1) the solid lithosphere, with a mass of approximately 2.7 × 10²² kilograms and a moment of inertia about the rotational axis of approximately 7.0 × 10³⁷ kg·m², and (2) the hydrosphere, with a mass of approximately 1.4 × 10²¹ kilograms (roughly 0.05 percent of the lithospheric mass) and a moment of inertia that, because the oceans are concentrated in the low-latitude basins and distributed in a thin, areally extensive layer averaging only 3.7 kilometers in depth (compared with the Earth's radius of 6,371 kilometers), is approximately 5 × 10³⁴ kg·m² — roughly 0.07 percent of the lithospheric moment of inertia. Under normal conditions, the oceans corotate with the solid Earth as a consequence of the frictional coupling at the seafloor: the turbulent boundary layer at the sediment-water interface transfers angular momentum from the rotating lithosphere to the water column, and over timescales longer than the characteristic spin-up time for the ocean basins — which is of the order of 10³ to 10⁴ years, determined by the balance between frictional acceleration and viscous dissipation — the entire hydrosphere is entrained in the Earth's rotation, with only a small residual differential rotation (the Antarctic Circumpolar Current, for example, flows eastward at velocities of up to 0.5 meters per second relative to the seafloor, driven by the wind stress and the Coriolis force rather than by incomplete frictional coupling).

During a crustal displacement event, the frictional coupling between the lithosphere and the hydrosphere — always weak, because the kinematic viscosity of water (approximately 10⁻⁶ m²/s) is many orders of magnitude lower than the effective viscosity of the asthenosphere — is rendered negligible by the extreme disparity in timescales: the lithosphere accelerates to its terminal displacement velocity (of the order of 10² to 10³ meters per second, corresponding to the 10⁴-kilometer rotation of the crust over 10⁴ to 10⁵ seconds, or 3 to 30 hours, as estimated by the Thomas rapid-displacement model) on a timescale that is far shorter than the hydrospheric response time, which is governed by the shallow-water wave propagation speed — the speed at which a pressure disturbance in the ocean can be communicated to distant points — given by the formula c = √(g·h), where g = 9.81 m/s² and h is the local water depth. For the average ocean depth of 3,700 meters, c = √(9.81 × 3,700) ≈ 190 meters per second, or approximately 680 kilometers per hour. Over the duration of the displacement event — 3 to 30 hours — a shallow-water wave can propagate a distance of 2,000 to 20,000 kilometers, which is comparable to the dimensions of the major ocean basins (the Pacific Ocean is approximately 19,000 kilometers wide at the equator) but is insufficient to allow the entire global ocean to adjust, through gravitational wave propagation, to the instantaneous orientation of the accelerating lithosphere. The result is that the oceans, over most of the Earth's surface, do not have time to "catch up" with the moving continents; they are, in effect, stationary in inertial space — continuing their pre-displacement eastward rotation while the lithosphere beneath them accelerates, decelerates, and ultimately comes to rest in a new orientation.

The relative velocity between the stationary ocean water and the moving lithosphere is the fundamental parameter that determines the severity of the inundation at any given location: at a point on the Earth's surface that, during the displacement, is dragged eastward through the ocean at a velocity v (relative to the water), the coastal inundation is equivalent to the impact of a tsunami wave traveling at velocity v into a stationary coast — or, equivalently, to the impact of a coast moving at velocity v into a stationary ocean, which is the same physical situation viewed from a different Galilean reference frame. The relative velocity v is not uniform across the Earth's surface but varies systematically with the geographical position of the point relative to the displacement axis and the displacement magnitude. For a simple 90-degree rotation of the lithosphere about an axis passing through the Central American isthmus (the hypothesized pivot point), a point on the equator at the longitude of the Pacific coast of South America — say, at 0° latitude, 80° W longitude — would undergo a latitudinal displacement of approximately 6,000 kilometers (roughly 55 degrees of arc), while a point at the latitude of Southern California — approximately 35° N, 120° W — would undergo a latitudinal displacement of approximately 3,500 kilometers, and a point at the latitude of Costa Rica — approximately 9° N, 84° W — would undergo a latitudinal displacement of only a few hundred kilometers or less, depending on its exact proximity to the pivot axis. These displacement distances, divided by the displacement timescale (3 to 30 hours), yield average crustal velocities relative to the water column that range from a few meters per second at the pivot to hundreds of meters per second at the antipodes of the pivot — velocities that, in the extreme case, approach or exceed the speed of sound in air and are comparable to the shallow-water wave speed in the deep ocean, producing hydraulic phenomena — shock waves, hydraulic jumps, and supersonic bores — that have no analog in the uniformitarian experience of tsunamis, storm surges, or river floods.

## 4.2 The Equatorial Bulge and Latitude-Dependent Inundation Severity

The latitude-dependent severity of crustal displacement mega-tsunami inundation — the observation, central to this analysis, that California was scoured by waves reaching elevations of 1,700 to 2,100 meters while Costa Rica experienced inundation capped at approximately 170 meters — is a direct consequence of the geometry of the Earth's equatorial bulge and the physics of the inertial oceanic response to lithospheric rotation. The Earth, as a rotating fluid planet, is not a perfect sphere but an oblate spheroid: the equatorial radius (6,378.1 kilometers) exceeds the polar radius (6,356.8 kilometers) by approximately 21.3 kilometers, a flattening of approximately 0.33 percent that is the direct result of the centrifugal force generated by the Earth's rotation, which opposes gravity at the equator and produces a net reduction in the effective gravitational acceleration (from 9.83 m/s² at the poles to 9.78 m/s² at the equator, a difference of approximately 0.5 percent). The oceans, as a fluid layer resting on the solid Earth, conform to the same oblate spheroidal equipotential surface — the geoid — that the solid Earth would assume if it were a perfectly fluid body in hydrostatic equilibrium: the sea surface at the equator stands approximately 21 kilometers further from the Earth's center of mass than the sea surface at the poles, and this elevation difference is maintained, against the tendency of gravity to flatten the oceans into a sphere, by the centrifugal potential associated with the Earth's rotation.

The critical consequence of the equatorial bulge for crustal displacement mega-tsunami dynamics is that the volume of water per unit area of the Earth's surface — the "water column height" — is not uniform across latitudes but is systematically greater in the low latitudes, near the equator, and systematically smaller in the high latitudes, near the poles. If the oceans were uniformly distributed across the Earth's surface — covering the entire planet to a uniform depth — the equatorial sea level would be approximately 21 kilometers higher, relative to the Earth's center, than the polar sea level, but the depth of the water column (the distance from the sea surface to the seafloor) would be uniform, because the seafloor, as part of the solid lithosphere, would conform to the same oblate spheroidal shape as the sea surface. The actual situation is more complex, because the oceans are not uniformly distributed but are confined to the ocean basins — the topographic depressions of the Earth's surface — and because the continents, which rise above the ocean surface, are concentrated in the Northern Hemisphere (approximately 40 percent of the Northern Hemisphere is continental, compared with approximately 20 percent of the Southern Hemisphere). Nevertheless, the first-order effect of the equatorial bulge on mega-tsunami dynamics can be captured by a simple model: the ocean water, at any given latitude, occupies a volume that is proportional to the ocean surface area at that latitude and to the average ocean depth, and when the lithosphere undergoes a latitudinal shift, the water column that was formerly at latitude λ₁ is relocated — from the perspective of the water, which remains stationary in inertial space — to latitude λ₂, where the local ocean depth and basin geometry are different.

The magnitude of the coastal inundation produced by this relocation is a function of the difference between the volume of water that is "carried" by the moving lithosphere and the volume that the destination basin can accommodate at equilibrium. If a continent that was formerly at a high latitude — where the equatorial bulge is minimal and the water column is relatively thin — is displaced to a low latitude — where the bulge is maximal and the water column is thick — the displaced continent will experience a net deficit of water relative to the equilibrium water volume at its new latitude, and the ocean will flow onto the continent to fill this deficit, producing a transgressive inundation whose magnitude is proportional to the latitudinal displacement distance. Conversely, if a continent is displaced from a low latitude to a high latitude, it will experience a net surplus of water relative to its new equilibrium, and the excess water will drain off the continent in a regressive event — but, because the displacement is catastrophic rather than gradual, the drainage will not be gentle but violent, carving deep erosional channels through the unconsolidated sediments and producing the U-shaped valleys and hydraulic plucking features documented in the Bear Creek Valley of Oregon and the Truckee Basin of the Sierra Nevada.

For the specific case of California, which, under the Thomas rapid-displacement model, would have been displaced from a mid-latitude position (approximately 40° to 60° N in its pre-displacement configuration, if the North Pole was located near Hudson Bay as Hapgood and Flem-Ath suggested) to its present position (32° to 42° N), the latitudinal displacement would have involved a southward movement of approximately 1,500 to 3,000 kilometers, corresponding to a transition from a region of relatively thin water columns (polar flattening) to a region of thicker water columns (equatorial bulge). The volume of water required to fill the deficit at the new, lower latitude would have been enormous — on the order of 10¹⁴ to 10¹⁵ cubic meters, equivalent to the entire volume of the Mediterranean Sea — and this water would have been supplied by the adjacent Pacific Ocean, which, as the continent moved southward into the equatorial bulge, would have surged onto the land as a continuous, unbroken hydraulic wall extending from the coastline to the crest of the Sierra Nevada and beyond. The height of this wall — the maximum elevation to which the inundation would reach — is determined by the balance between the inertial pressure of the advancing water (which is proportional to ρ·v², where ρ = 1,025 kg/m³ is the density of seawater and v is the relative velocity between the water and the continent) and the gravitational restoring force that resists the upward movement of the water against the continental slope (which is proportional to ρ·g·h, where h is the elevation that the water must reach to surmount a given topographic barrier). For a relative velocity of 300 meters per second — a value that, as argued above, is consistent with the Thomas rapid-displacement model for the California sector — the inertial pressure ρ·v² = 1,025 × (300)² ≈ 9.2 × 10⁷ pascals, or approximately 920 atmospheres, which is sufficient to drive a column of seawater to an elevation of h = v²/(2g) = (300)²/(2 × 9.81) ≈ 4,590 meters — more than sufficient to crest the 2,100-meter passes of the Sierra Nevada and to produce the high-elevation turbidite deposits and erratic boulders that are documented in the Truckee Basin. This simple Bernoulli-type calculation — equating the kinetic energy of the advancing water to the potential energy of the elevated water column — is, of course, an idealization that neglects the energy losses due to turbulent dissipation, bottom friction, and the entrainment of sediment and debris, which would reduce the actual maximum inundation elevation by a factor of two to four relative to the ideal limit, yielding values of 1,000 to 2,000 meters — precisely the range of elevations at which the anomalous geomorphological features of California are observed.

## 4.3 Wave Propagation, Bore Formation, and the Hydraulic Jump

The propagation of the displacement wave across the continental surface is not, as the simple Bernoulli model might imply, a smooth, continuous flow resembling a gradually rising tide; it is, rather, a violently turbulent, discontinuously propagating hydraulic bore — a breaking wave front in which the water surface steepens into a near-vertical wall, the flow transitions from subcritical (Froude number Fr < 1, where Fr = v/√(g·h) is the ratio of the flow velocity to the shallow-water wave speed) to supercritical (Fr > 1), and the energy dissipation at the wave front is dominated by intense turbulent mixing, air entrainment, and the incorporation of enormous quantities of sediment, soil, vegetation, and debris into the advancing water mass. The physics of hydraulic bore propagation has been extensively studied in the context of tidal bores — the upstream-propagating waves that form in certain estuaries, such as the Qiantang River in China (which produces a bore up to 9 meters high and 40 kilometers per hour in velocity) and the Severn River in England — and in the context of dam-break floods, which share with crustal displacement mega-tsunamis the characteristic of a sudden, large-amplitude water release that propagates over an initially dry or shallowly submerged surface.

The transition from a smooth, sinusoidal wave form to a breaking bore occurs when the wave amplitude a (the height of the wave crest above the ambient water level) exceeds the critical value a_c = (h₀/3), where h₀ is the undisturbed water depth: for a > h₀/3, the wave profile becomes multivalued — the surface would have to intersect itself, which is physically impossible — and the wave breaks, dissipating its excess energy through turbulence and forming a discontinuous front that propagates at a velocity given by the Rankine-Hugoniot jump conditions for shallow-water flow: the bore velocity U is related to the upstream (undisturbed) depth h₀, the downstream (bore) depth h₁, and the gravitational acceleration g by U = √[(g·h₁/(2·h₀)) · (h₁ + h₀)]. For a displacement wave with an offshore amplitude of hundreds to thousands of meters — an amplitude that, by any standard, massively exceeds the critical breaking threshold for any plausible nearshore water depth — the wave will break in deep water, far offshore, and will approach the coastline not as a gently rising swell but as a turbulent, aerated wall of water, propagating at velocities that, as argued above, approach or exceed the speed of sound in air and that are limited only by the shallow-water wave speed in the depth of the wave itself (which is the sum of the ambient depth and the wave amplitude, and therefore can be thousands of meters, corresponding to a wave speed of c = √(9.81 × h₁) ≈ 100 to 300 meters per second for h₁ = 1,000 to 10,000 meters — velocities that are consistent, within the uncertainties of the model, with the Thomas rapid-displacement timescale).

The advance of the hydraulic bore across the continental surface — the "run-up" phase of the inundation — is governed by the balance between the inertial pressure of the advancing bore front and the gravitational and frictional forces that resist its upslope propagation. The run-up distance L — the maximum inland penetration of the bore, measured horizontally from the pre-event coastline — is related to the bore height at the coast H (which is itself a function of the offshore wave amplitude, the nearshore bathymetry, and the coastal topography), the average slope of the continental surface S, and the bottom friction coefficient C_f by the semi-empirical formula L ≈ H²/(2·C_f·S), derived from the conservation of energy for a turbulent bore advancing up a uniformly sloping surface. For the California coast, with H ≈ 2,000 meters (the approximate elevation of the Sierra Nevada crest above sea level), S ≈ 0.02 (the average slope of the western Sierra Nevada, which rises from approximately 100 meters at the edge of the Central Valley to 2,100 meters at Donner Pass over a horizontal distance of approximately 100 kilometers), and C_f ≈ 0.01 (a typical value for turbulent flow over a rough, vegetated surface with extensive bedload transport), the predicted run-up distance is L ≈ (2,000)²/(2 × 0.01 × 0.02) ≈ 10⁷ meters, or 10,000 kilometers — a value that exceeds the width of the North American continent by a factor of two and that indicates that the bore would have propagated entirely across the continent and into the Atlantic Ocean, which is consistent with the ECD model's prediction of a transcontinental inundation that temporarily created a marine connection between the Pacific and Atlantic basins. For Costa Rica, with H ≈ 170 meters (the maximum inundation elevation reconstructed from the marine terrace and Oxisol preservation data), S ≈ 0.05 (the steeper coastal slope of the Osa Peninsula, which rises rapidly from sea level to elevations of 200 to 300 meters within a few kilometers of the coast), and C_f ≈ 0.01, the predicted run-up distance is L ≈ (170)²/(2 × 0.01 × 0.05) ≈ 29,000 meters, or approximately 29 kilometers — a value that is consistent with the observed distribution of marine shell deposits and anoxic clay bands in the Drake Bay region, which extend no more than 20 to 30 kilometers inland from the present coastline.

The recession phase of the inundation — the "drawdown" or "backwash" — is, from a geomorphological perspective, often more significant than the run-up phase, because the return flow, channelized into the topographic low points that provide the paths of least resistance for the draining water, attains velocities that are much higher than the sheet-like advance of the initial bore, producing the deep, U-shaped valleys, the hydraulic plucking of bedrock, and the massive sediment export that are the most conspicuous signatures of the displacement event in the geological record. The recession flow is driven by the hydrostatic pressure gradient between the inundated interior — where the water surface, ponded behind topographic barriers, may stand hundreds of meters above the ultimate base level (the ocean) — and the coastal outlet, and the flow accelerates through constrictions (narrow valleys, mountain passes) in accordance with the Bernoulli principle: the velocity v at a constriction, where the cross-sectional area of the flow is reduced, is related to the upstream head H by v ≈ √(2·g·H), and for H = 1,000 meters — a typical ponding elevation for the temporary inland sea in the California Central Valley — the constriction velocity would be v ≈ √(2 × 9.81 × 1,000) ≈ 140 meters per second, or approximately 500 kilometers per hour — a flow velocity that, sustained over hours to days, is more than sufficient to carve the deep gorges through the Coast Ranges, to transport 10-foot granite boulders over distances of tens of miles, and to strip millions of tons of sediment from the continental surface and deposit it as turbidite fans in the deep ocean basins offshore.

## 4.4 Atmospheric Coupling: Supersonic Winds and the Transmutation of the Stratosphere

The crustal displacement event, while primarily a lithospheric-hydrospheric phenomenon, also involves a catastrophic perturbation to the atmosphere that, while less extensively documented in the geological record than the oceanic effects, is of comparable magnitude and is essential to a complete understanding of the post-displacement environment in which biological and human communities were forced to recover. The atmosphere, like the oceans, is only weakly coupled to the solid Earth through friction at the planetary boundary layer — the lowest kilometer of the atmosphere, where turbulent eddies transport momentum from the surface into the free atmosphere — and during a crustal displacement event, the atmosphere, like the oceans, will initially continue its pre-displacement rotation while the lithosphere accelerates beneath it. The resulting differential velocity between the stationary atmosphere and the moving land surface generates winds that, at their maximum, exceed by orders of magnitude the most extreme winds observed in the instrumental meteorological record: for a crustal displacement velocity of 300 meters per second (corresponding to a 90-degree rotation in approximately 12 hours), the surface-relative wind velocity — the "effective wind" experienced by an observer on the moving land surface — is also approximately 300 meters per second, which is comparable to the speed of sound in air at sea level (approximately 340 m/s) and which is nearly three times the wind speed in the strongest tornadoes ever recorded (the 1999 Bridge Creek-Moore tornado in Oklahoma, which produced winds of approximately 135 m/s, or 302 mph, as measured by mobile Doppler radar). These supersonic effective winds would be capable of eroding every exposed surface not protected by topographic sheltering, transporting enormous quantities of dust, sand, and debris into the upper atmosphere — including the stratosphere, which under normal conditions is isolated from the troposphere by the strong thermal inversion at the tropopause, but which would be breached by the intense vertical mixing driven by the supersonic surface winds — and producing a global stratospheric dust veil whose climatic effects (reduction of surface solar radiation by 90 to 99 percent for months to years, global cooling of 10°C to 20°C, suppression of photosynthesis, collapse of terrestrial and marine primary productivity) would be indistinguishable from the "nuclear winter" scenarios that were extensively studied during the 1980s in the context of the potential climatic consequences of a full-scale nuclear exchange.

The Equinox Convergence framework proposes an additional atmospheric consequence of the displacement event that transcends conventional meteorology: the "transmutation" of atmospheric constituents through high-energy plasma interactions in the upper atmosphere. The intense electromagnetic fields associated with the solar micro-nova and the magnetic field collapse that triggers the displacement are hypothesized to drive nuclear reactions in the stratosphere — specifically, the fusion of atmospheric nitrogen and oxygen nuclei, catalyzed by the extreme electric fields and plasma densities produced by the solar discharge, into heavier elements, including the isotopes of carbon, beryllium, and other cosmogenic nuclides that are observed in anomalous concentrations at the Younger Dryas boundary. This transmutation process — sometimes referred to as the "Wormwood event" in the esoteric literature, a reference to the Biblical "star called Wormwood" that, in the Book of Revelation, falls from heaven and makes the waters bitter — would convert solar electromagnetic energy directly into matter, producing the torrential rainfall ("deluge") that, in many mythological accounts, accompanies the crustal displacement: the hydrogen and oxygen liberated by the dissociation of atmospheric water vapor recombine to form precipitation that is anomalously enriched in heavy isotopes and that, falling on a devastated landscape already saturated by the oceanic inundation, compounds the hydrological catastrophe to an extent that is difficult to overstate.

## 4.5 Scaling Laws and the Energy Budget of Crustal Displacement Mega-Tsunamis

The energy budget of a crustal displacement mega-tsunami can be estimated, to within one or two orders of magnitude, by applying the principles of conservation of energy and momentum to the simplified two-component (lithosphere-hydrosphere) model developed above. The total kinetic energy of the Earth's rotation is E_rot = (1/2)·I·ω², where I ≈ 8.0 × 10³⁷ kg·m² is the Earth's moment of inertia about its rotational axis, ω = 7.292 × 10⁻⁵ rad/s is the angular velocity of rotation (corresponding to one rotation per 86,164 seconds, the sidereal day), and the product (1/2)·I·ω² ≈ 2.14 × 10²⁹ joules. During a crustal displacement event, a fraction f of this rotational kinetic energy is converted — through the intermediary of the differential rotation between the lithosphere and the hydrosphere — into the kinetic energy of the oceanic water mass, which is initially at rest in the inertial frame and is subsequently accelerated, by the frictional and pressure forces exerted by the moving lithosphere, to velocities comparable to the lithospheric displacement velocity. The fraction f is not a free parameter but is determined by the geometry of the displacement and the mass distribution of the hydrosphere: for a 90-degree rotation of the lithosphere, the differential angular velocity between the lithosphere and the hydrosphere is at most ω (the full rotational angular velocity, because the hydrosphere initially corotates with the lithosphere but then, when the crust accelerates, initially remains stationary in the inertial frame, producing a relative angular velocity equal to the pre-displacement angular velocity), and the kinetic energy of the relative motion is ΔE = (1/2)·I_h·ω², where I_h ≈ 5 × 10³⁴ kg·m² is the moment of inertia of the hydrosphere about the rotational axis. Substituting numerical values, ΔE ≈ (1/2) × (5 × 10³⁴) × (7.292 × 10⁻⁵)² ≈ 1.33 × 10²⁶ joules — a staggering quantity of energy, equivalent to approximately 3 × 10¹⁰ megatons of TNT, or roughly 600 million times the energy of the largest thermonuclear weapon ever tested (the Soviet Tsar Bomba, which had a yield of approximately 50 megatons). This energy is not released instantaneously — it is dissipated, over the 3- to 30-hour duration of the displacement event, through the turbulent mixing of the oceanic water column, the entrainment and transport of sediment, the erosion of the continental surface, the generation of atmospheric acoustic-gravity waves, and the ultimate deposition of the water mass as it comes to rest against the continental topography — but the average power dissipation during the event, P ≈ ΔE/Δt ≈ (1.33 × 10²⁶ J)/(10⁴ to 10⁵ s) ≈ 10²¹ to 10²² watts, is roughly 10,000 to 100,000 times the total power consumption of human civilization at present (approximately 1.8 × 10¹³ watts), and it is this enormous power flux, directed against the continental margins over a period of hours to days, that produces the geomorphological signatures — the scoured bedrock, the turbidite sequences, the erratic boulder trains, the U-shaped valleys, and the deeply incised drainage channels — that are the principal empirical evidence for the reality of the crustal displacement hypothesis.

The scaling laws that relate the displacement parameters — the angular displacement magnitude Δθ, the displacement timescale Δt, the lithospheric velocity v = R·Δθ/Δt (where R = 6,371 km is the Earth's radius), the hydrospheric kinetic energy ΔE = (1/2)·I_h·(Δθ/Δt)², and the average power dissipation P = ΔE/Δt — to the observable geomorphological effects — the maximum inundation elevation H ≈ v²/(2g) (from the Bernoulli balance), the run-up distance L ≈ H²/(2·C_f·S) (from the energy-dissipation model), the volume of sediment eroded V_erode ≈ P·ε/ρ (where ε is the erosional efficiency — the fraction of the dissipated energy that is converted into the mechanical work of sediment detachment and transport, typically 0.01 to 0.1 for turbulent water flows over unconsolidated sediment), and the volume of turbidite deposited V_deposit ≈ V_erode (assuming that the eroded sediment is transported to the deep ocean basins and deposited as turbidite fans, with minimal storage in intermediate positions) — provide a quantitative framework for testing the crustal displacement hypothesis against the field observations. The agreement between the predicted and observed values of H (1,700 to 2,100 meters for California, 170 meters for Costa Rica), L (transcontinental for California, ~30 kilometers for Costa Rica), and V_deposit (the volume of the Tuna Canyon and Modelo Formation turbidites is estimated, from their areal extent and average thickness, at 10³ to 10⁴ cubic kilometers, which is consistent with the scaling-law prediction for a displacement of Δθ ≈ 60 to 90 degrees and Δt ≈ 3 to 30 hours) is, while not a rigorous test — given the substantial uncertainties in the parameter values — sufficiently encouraging to warrant the further development and refinement of the quantitative crustal displacement model as a legitimate scientific hypothesis, rather than as a speculative fantasy to be dismissed without serious engagement.
