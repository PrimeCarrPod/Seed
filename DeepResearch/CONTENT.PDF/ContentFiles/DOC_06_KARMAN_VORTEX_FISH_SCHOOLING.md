# Kármán and Reverse Kármán Vortex Streets: Hydrodynamic Foundations of Fish Schooling

## Abstract

This document presents a comprehensive mathematical analysis of vortex street hydrodynamics as the foundation for understanding fish schooling behavior. We examine the classical Kármán vortex street generated by bluff bodies, the reverse Kármán vortex street produced by undulatory swimmers, and the Strouhal number as the key dimensionless parameter governing wake topology. The optimal Strouhal window (0.2-0.4) for maximum propulsive efficiency is derived from first principles. We analyze the immersed boundary method for computational fluid dynamics of flexible swimmers, the vortex shedding frequency scaling laws, and the hydrodynamic mechanisms by which trailing fish extract energy from leader wakes. The Kármán gait—phase-matched tail beat synchronization with oncoming vortices—is derived mathematically and shown to enable passive propulsion from the leader's wake.

---

## 1. Introduction: Vortices as the Currency of Aquatic Locomotion

Fish schooling represents one of nature's most elegant exploitations of fluid dynamics. At Reynolds numbers ranging from 10⁴ to 10⁶, inertial forces strictly dominate viscous forces, and the wake structures generated by swimming bodies become the primary medium for hydrodynamic interaction. The classical Kármán vortex street—alternating vortices shed from a bluff body—becomes, in the hands of evolution, a reverse Kármán street: a propulsive wake of alternating vortices with opposite rotation that trailing fish can harvest for thrust.

The Strouhal number, St = fA/U, where f is tail-beat frequency, A is amplitude, and U is swimming speed, emerges as the universal parameter governing this phenomenon. Across diverse species—tuna, trout, eels, dolphins—the Strouhal number clusters in the narrow window 0.2-0.4, a kinematic signature of optimal vortex formation and thrust production.

## 2. Classical Kármán Vortex Street: Bluff Body Wake

### 2.1 Flow Past a Circular Cylinder

Consider a circular cylinder of diameter D in a uniform flow U at Re = UD/ν. The wake exhibits a sequence of regimes:

- **Re < 5**: Steady, attached flow, no separation
- **5 < Re < 40**: Steady separation, symmetric recirculation bubbles
- **40 < Re < 150**: Laminar vortex shedding, 2D Kármán street
- **150 < Re < 300**: Transition to 3D, spanwise instabilities (Mode A, B)
- **Re > 300**: Turbulent wake, broadband spectrum

### 2.2 Vortex Shedding and the Strouhal Number

In the laminar shedding regime, vortices are shed alternately from the top and bottom of the cylinder at frequency f_s. The Strouhal number is:

St = f_s D / U

For a circular cylinder at 300 < Re < 10⁵, St ≈ 0.21 (remarkably constant).

The vortex street geometry:
- Streamwise spacing: λ = U/f_s = D/St
- Cross-stream spacing: h ≈ 0.28λ (from stability analysis)
- Vortex core radius: r_c ~ D/10

### 2.3 Stability of the Vortex Street

The Kármán street is a stable configuration of point vortices. For a street of vortices with circulation ±Γ, spacing λ and h, the street is stable if:

h/λ = 0.281... (Kármán's result)

This is derived from the condition that each vortex moves with the same velocity (the street translates without deformation).

### 2.4 Drag and Momentum Deficit

The time-averaged drag on the cylinder is related to the momentum deficit in the wake:

C_D = (2/ρU²D) ∫ (ρU u_deficit) dy

For the Kármán street, the drag coefficient C_D ≈ 1.2 for Re ~ 10³-10⁵.

## 3. Reverse Kármán Vortex Street: Propulsive Wake

### 3.1 Undulatory Swimming

A fish swimming by undulatory propulsion (e.g., carangiform, thunniform) flexes its body and tail, generating a traveling wave of curvature:

y(x,t) = A(x) sin(kx - ωt)

where k = 2π/λ is the wavenumber, ω = 2πf is the angular frequency, and A(x) is the amplitude envelope (increasing toward the tail).

### 3.2 Wake Topology: Reverse Kármán Street

Unlike the bluff body wake, the swimming wake has:
- Vortices of alternating sign (like Kármán)
- **But** the rotation direction is reversed: the vortex pair induces a jet **behind** the swimmer (thrust), not a momentum deficit (drag)

The vortices form a reverse Kármán street with:
- Downstream jet (positive momentum)
- Thrust-producing wake

### 3.3 Strouhal Number for Swimmers

For a swimming animal, the Strouhal number is defined as:

St = f A_tail / U

where A_tail is the peak-to-peak tail amplitude.

Empirical data across species:
- Tunas, mackerels: St ≈ 0.25-0.35
- Trout, salmon: St ≈ 0.25-0.30
- Eels, anguilliform: St ≈ 0.30-0.40
- Dolphins, cetaceans: St ≈ 0.25-0.35
- **Universal window**: 0.2 ≤ St ≤ 0.4

### 3.4 Why This Window? Optimal Vortex Formation

The optimal Strouhal window corresponds to the condition where each vortex is fully formed before the next is shed, but not so far apart that the jet dissipates.

**Vortex formation number**: The ratio of vortex formation time to convection time.

Formation time: t_f ~ A/f (time for tail to sweep amplitude)
Convection time: t_c ~ λ/U = 1/f

The formation number: t_f/t_c ~ A/λ = St

For St too small (< 0.2): Vortices merge before separation, weak jet.
For St too large (> 0.4): Vortices too far apart, jet breaks down, efficiency drops.

The optimal St ~ 0.3 maximizes the vortex impulse per cycle.

## 4. Hydrodynamics of the Reverse Kármán Street

### 4.1 Vortex Impulse and Thrust

Each vortex pair in the reverse street carries impulse:

I = ρ Γ h

where Γ is the circulation of each vortex and h is the cross-stream spacing.

The average thrust is the rate of impulse production:

T = I f = ρ Γ h f

The circulation scales with tail kinematics:

Γ ~ f A²

Thus T ~ ρ f² A² h

### 4.2 Jet Velocity and Thrust Coefficient

The jet velocity behind the swimmer scales as:

U_jet ~ Γ/h ~ f A²/h

The thrust coefficient:

C_T = T / (½ ρ U² S) ~ (f A/U)² (h/A) ~ St² (h/A)

For fixed St, larger h/A gives more thrust but requires more power.

### 4.3 Efficiency: Froude Efficiency

The Froude efficiency (propulsive efficiency) is:

η_F = T U / P_input

where P_input is the mechanical power expended by the fish.

For the reverse Kármán street, theoretical maximum:

η_F,max = 1 / (1 + (U_jet/U)²)

At optimal St, U_jet/U ~ 0.5-1.0, giving η_F ~ 0.5-0.8.

## 5. Immersed Boundary Method for Flexible Swimmers

### 5.1 Formulation

The immersed boundary method (IBM) solves the Navier-Stokes equations on a fixed Eulerian grid while representing the flexible swimmer as a Lagrangian mesh that moves with the fluid.

**Fluid equations (Eulerian)**:

ρ_f (∂u/∂t + u·∇u) = -∇p + μ ∇²u + f
∇·u = 0

**Structure equations (Lagrangian)**:

∂X/∂t = u(X,t)  (no-slip)
m ∂²X/∂t² = F_elastic + F_fluid

**Coupling**:

f(x,t) = ∫ F(s,t) δ(x - X(s,t)) ds
u(X,t) = ∫ u(x,t) δ(x - X(s,t)) dx

where δ is a regularized delta function, X(s,t) is the Lagrangian position, and F is the force density.

### 5.2 Elastic Forces

For a flexible filament representing the fish body:

F_elastic = -∂/∂s (T ∂X/∂s) + ∂²/∂s² (EI ∂²X/∂s²)

where T is tension, EI is bending stiffness.

### 5.3 Prescribed Kinematics vs. Free Swimming

- **Prescribed kinematics**: X(s,t) given, compute forces and flow
- **Free swimming**: Only muscle forces prescribed, body moves freely

Both approaches have been used to validate the reverse Kármán street and optimal St.

## 6. Vortex Shedding Frequency Scaling Laws

### 6.1 Scaling with Reynolds Number

For swimming at Re = UL/ν ~ 10⁴-10⁶:

The tail-beat Strouhal number St = fA/U is independent of Re in the inertial regime.

The vortex shedding frequency from the tail:

f_shed = f (one vortex per half-cycle, so two per cycle)

The vortex circulation:

Γ ~ U A (from dimensional analysis)

### 6.2 Wake Wavelength

The streamwise wavelength of the vortex street:

λ_wake = U / f = A / St

At St = 0.3, λ_wake ≈ 3.3 A.

The cross-stream spacing:

h ≈ 0.28 λ_wake ≈ 0.93 A

### 6.3 Three-Dimensional Effects

Real fish have finite span (tail height H). The wake is a series of vortex rings or linked vortex loops, not 2D vortices.

Vortex ring spacing: λ_ring = λ_wake
Ring radius: R ~ H/2

The induced velocity from a vortex ring:

u_ind ~ Γ / R

## 7. The Kármán Gait: Energy Extraction from Leader Wake

### 7.1 Trailing Fish in a Reverse Kármán Street

Consider a trailing fish swimming in the wake of a leader. The leader's wake is a reverse Kármán street with vortices of circulation ±Γ, spacing λ, h.

The trailing fish experiences an oscillating flow field:

u(x,y,t) = Σ_{n} u_vortex(x - nλ, y ± h/2, t)

where u_vortex is the velocity field of a single vortex.

### 7.2 Phase-Matched Tail Beats

The trailing fish adjusts its tail-beat phase φ to synchronize with the oncoming vortices. The optimal phase is:

φ_opt = -π/2 (or equivalent)

At this phase, the tail encounters the vortex at the optimal angle to extract energy.

### 7.3 Mathematical Model: Forced Oscillator

The trailing fish's tail motion is modeled as a forced oscillator:

I d²θ/dt² + b dθ/dt + k θ = τ_muscle + τ_hydro

where τ_hydro is the hydrodynamic torque from the leader's vortices.

The hydrodynamic torque from a vortex at distance d:

τ_hydro ~ ρ Γ A_tail² / d² sin(φ - φ_vortex)

### 7.4 Energy Extraction Mechanism

When the tail beats in phase with the vortex-induced velocity:

- During the power stroke, the tail pushes against the vortex-induced flow, doing positive work
- During the recovery stroke, the vortex assists the motion

Net work per cycle:

W_cycle = ∮ τ_hydro dθ > 0

For optimal phase, W_cycle is maximized.

### 7.5 Passive Propulsion Limit

Immersed boundary simulations (Zhu et al., 2007; Akhtar et al., 2007) show that a **passive** flexible foil placed in a reverse Kármán street can achieve **forward propulsion without any muscle actuation**. The foil's natural frequency locks to the vortex shedding frequency, and it extracts energy from the wake.

This demonstrates that the Kármán gait does not require active sensing—it is a mechanical resonance phenomenon.

## 8. Schooling Formations and Hydrodynamic Interactions

### 8.1 Diamond/Staggered Formation

Fish in schools often adopt a diamond or staggered formation:

- Streamwise spacing: Δx ~ λ_wake/2 to λ_wake
- Cross-stream spacing: Δy ~ h/2 to h

This positions each trailing fish in the upwash region of the leader's vortices.

### 8.2 Hydrodynamic Benefits

For a trailing fish in the optimal position:
- **Drag reduction**: Up to 30-50% reduction in mean drag
- **Thrust enhancement**: Up to 20-40% increase in thrust
- **Net energy saving**: 15-30% reduction in metabolic cost

### 8.3 Multiple Neighbors

In a school, each fish interacts with multiple neighbors. The total hydrodynamic force is the superposition of wakes from all nearby fish. The optimal position balances:
- Benefit from anterior neighbors' upwash
- Avoidance of downwash from lateral neighbors
- Collision avoidance

## 9. Lateral Line System and Rheotaxis

### 9.1 Sensory Mechanism

The lateral line system consists of neuromasts (superficial and canal) that detect:
- Local water velocity (superficial neuromasts)
- Pressure gradients (canal neuromasts)

The signal is proportional to the local velocity gradient or pressure gradient.

### 9.2 Rheotaxis: Alignment with Flow

Rheotaxis is the tendency to align with oncoming flow. The lateral line detects the velocity gradient across the body:

∂u/∂y ~ U/L_body

This provides a directional cue even in the absence of vision.

### 9.3 Vortex Detection

The lateral line can detect the oscillating velocity field of the Kármán street:

u_vortex ~ Γ/r

Fish can sense the phase of oncoming vortices and adjust their tail beats accordingly.

## 10. Neural Network Models for Autonomous Swimmers

### 10.1 Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KANs)

Recent work uses KANs for perception and decision-making in simulated swimmers:

y = Σ_i w_i φ_i(Σ_j w_{ij} x_j)

where φ_i are learnable activation functions (splines).

### 10.2 Input Features

For a simulated swimmer in a vortex street:
- Local velocity (u, v) from lateral line
- Pressure gradient
- Body orientation and curvature
- Neighbor positions (if schooling)

### 10.3 Output Actions

- Tail-beat frequency f
- Tail-beat amplitude A
- Phase φ (relative to vortex)
- Turn rate

### 10.4 Learning Optimal Gait

Reinforcement learning with KANs discovers:
- Optimal St ~ 0.3
- Phase locking to vortex street
- Schooling positions matching diamond formation

The network learns to "surf" the vortices, confirming the hydrodynamic theory.

## 11. Metabolic Cost Reduction in Schools

### 11.1 Cost of Transport

The cost of transport (COT) is the energy per unit distance per unit mass:

COT = P / (mgU)

where P is metabolic power, m is mass, g is gravity.

### 11.2 Schooling Effect

In a school, the average COT is reduced:

COT_school = COT_solitary (1 - β)

where β = 0.15-0.30 for optimal formations.

The reduction comes from:
- Lower drag (upwash reduces effective angle of attack)
- Higher thrust (vortex-assisted propulsion)
- Reduced maneuvering cost (hydrodynamic coupling stabilizes position)

### 11.3 Scaling with School Size

For a school of N fish:
- Edge fish: less benefit (fewer neighbors)
- Interior fish: maximum benefit
- Average benefit: β_avg ~ β_max (1 - c/√N)

where c is a constant depending on formation geometry.

## 12. Stability of School Formations

### 12.1 Hydrodynamic Stability

The schooling formation is hydrodynamically stable if small perturbations decay. Linear stability analysis of the coupled fish-wake system shows:

- Diamond formation: stable for St in optimal range
- In-line formation: unstable (trailing fish in downwash)
- Side-by-side: neutral (lateral stability from wall effect)

### 12.2 Behavioral Feedback

Fish actively adjust position based on sensory feedback (lateral line, vision). The control law:

d(Δx)/dt = -k_x (Δx - Δx_opt)
d(Δy)/dt = -k_y (Δy - Δy_opt)

where k_x, k_y are behavioral gains.

This active stabilization maintains the formation despite turbulence and perturbations.

## 14. Summary and Conclusions

The hydrodynamics of fish schooling is built on the physics of vortex streets. Key results:

1. **Classical Kármán street**: Alternating vortices from bluff bodies, St ≈ 0.21, drag-producing.

2. **Reverse Kármán street**: Alternating vortices from undulatory swimmers, opposite rotation, thrust-producing. St = fA/U clusters in 0.2-0.4 across species.

3. **Optimal Strouhal window**: St ~ 0.3 maximizes vortex formation efficiency—vortices are fully formed but not over-separated.

4. **Immersed boundary method**: Computational framework for flexible swimmers, validating wake topology and forces.

5. **Kármán gait**: Trailing fish phase-lock tail beats to leader's vortices, extracting energy. Passive foils can propel themselves in a reverse Kármán street.

5. **Schooling formations**: Diamond/staggered positions fish in upwash regions, reducing drag 30-50% and enhancing thrust 20-40%.

6. **Lateral line sensing**: Detects vortex phase, enables rheotaxis and active gait adjustment.

7. **Neural network control**: KANs learn optimal vortex-surfing behavior, confirming hydrodynamic optimality.

8. **Metabolic savings**: 15-30% reduction in cost of transport for schooling fish.

The reverse Kármán vortex street is the hydrodynamic currency of aquatic schooling—a physical mechanism that evolution has tuned to near-optimal efficiency across hundreds of millions of years.
